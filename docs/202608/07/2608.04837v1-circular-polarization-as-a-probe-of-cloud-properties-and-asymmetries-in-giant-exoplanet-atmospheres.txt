# Circular polarization as a probe of cloud properties and asymmetries in giant exoplanet atmospheres 

M. B. Michaelis and S. Wolf 

Institute of Theoretical Physics and Astrophysics, Kiel University, Leibnizstr. 15, 24118 Kiel, Germany e-mail: mmichaelis@astrophysik.uni-kiel.de 

Received <date > / Accepted <date >

ABSTRACT 

Context. Polarimetry is a promising method for characterizing clouds in exoplanetary atmospheres. For planets in the Solar System, circular polarization measurements complement linear polarimetry by providing additional information on cloud particle properties. As the disk-integrated circular polarization is zero for symmetric planets, observing intrinsic circular polarization of spatially unre-solved exoplanets requires stable spatial asymmetries such as circumplanetary rings. 

Aims. We investigated the potential of circular polarization measurements at optical and near-infrared wavelengths to determine optical properties of cloud particles in the atmospheres of giant exoplanets and characterize asymmetries. 

Methods. For 20 selected cloud condensates that cover a large range of refractive indices, we calculated optical properties assuming Mie scattering theory. The circular polarization of starlight scattered by cloudy exoplanets was calculated with Monte Carlo radia-tive transfer simulations. To explain the connection between optical properties and planetary circular polarization, we derived an interpretative model of the first two scattering orders. 

Results. Planetary hemispheres with atmospheres including cloud particles with a large imaginary part, k, of the refractive index show distinct circular polarization phase curves dominated by scattering first by gaseous molecules and then by cloud particles. The intrinsic degree of circular polarization, Pc, is at most 3 · 10 −4. When the cloud particles have a low k, they instead induce even smaller but more predictable circular polarization dominated by scattering solely by cloud particles. The predictability allows for a characterization of the underlying asymmetry. For example, this could be used to determine which half of the planet is covered by a circumplanetary ring. 

Conclusions. Circular polarization of starlight reflected by giant exoplanets is sensitive to cloud particle composition and large-scale asymmetries but remains a subtle signal. While promising for characterizing clouds under favorable conditions, practical detection requires technological advances in polarimetry and careful disentanglement from stellar background signals. 

Key words. Polarization – Radiative transfer – Scattering – Methods: numerical – Planets and satellites: atmospheres 

1. Introduction 

Eye-catching features of planetary atmospheres in the Solar Sys-tem are clouds, such as terrestrial water clouds, sulfuric acid clouds on Venus (Young 1973; Hansen & Hovenier 1974), and di ff erent cloud layers in the Jovian atmosphere containing am-monia and ammonium hydrosulfide among various other con-densates hidden in deeper layers (Atreya et al. 1999). Similarly, clouds are predicted to form in the atmospheres of exoplanets (e.g., Sánchez-Lavega et al. 2004; Helling 2019; Roman et al. 2021). Their presence blocks our view of the gaseous layers be-neath, limiting our ability to characterize the atmosphere using transit or eclipse spectroscopy (e.g., Seager & Sasselov 2000). Indeed, absorption features appear muted or are obscured en-tirely in optical or near-infrared transmission spectra of many observed exoplanets (e.g., Charbonneau et al. 2002; Tinetti et al. 2007; Pont et al. 2008, 2013; Bean et al. 2010; Berta et al. 2012; Gibson et al. 2013; Knutson et al. 2014; Kreidberg et al. 2014, 2018; Sing et al. 2016; Espinoza et al. 2019). Clouds also a ff ect the phase angle dependence of the re-flected light flux from exoplanets (e.g., Seager & Sasselov 2000; Heng & Demory 2013; Webber et al. 2015; Dyudina et al. 2016; Oreshenko et al. 2016). Therefore, phase curve measurements at visible wavelengths are of great value for determining the prop-erties of the planet and its clouds (García Muñoz & Isaak 2015; Feng et al. 2018; García Muñoz & Cabrera 2018; Vaughan et al. 2023; Akinsanmi et al. 2024). In Solar System research, po-larimetric phase curves further constrain cloud particle prop-erties. For instance, Hansen & Hovenier (1974) determined the composition, particle size distribution, and vertical struc-ture of the Venusian clouds using linear polarization mea-surements performed by Lyot (1929), Co ff een & Gehrels (1969), Dollfus & Co ff een (1970), and Veverka (1971). Kawata (1978) interpreted circular polarization measurements con-ducted by Kemp et al. (1971), Swedlund et al. (1972), and Michalsky & Stokes (1974) and narrowed down the particle sizes and refractive indices of Jovian and Saturnian clouds com-pared to an analysis using only linear polarimetry. Consequently, polarimetry is a promising method for char-acterizing atmospheres of exoplanets (e.g., Rossi et al. 2022). Numerical studies demonstrate that linear polarization measure-ments of starlight reflected by exoplanets constrain their cloud composition and cloud particle size (e.g., Karalidi et al. 2011; Bailey et al. 2018; Lietzow & Wolf 2022), or the fraction of the planet covered by clouds (Rossi & Stam 2017; Winning et al. 2024). Recently, Wiktorowicz et al. (2025) reported the discov-ery of linearly polarized flux of the hot Jupiter HD 189733b in the B band with an amplitude of 40 ppm of the stellar flux. Their measurements are in accordance with previous spectro-

Article number, page 1 arXiv:2608.04837v1 [astro-ph.EP] 5 Aug 2026 A&A proofs: manuscript no. aa60315-26 

metric detections of small SiO 2 particles (Inglis et al. 2024). In addition, Wiktorowicz et al. (2025) detect circularly polarized flux that is attributed to star-planet interactions, as the amplitude of 67 ppm exceeds that expected for a planetary atmosphere. For reference, the intrinsic circular polarization of sunlight re-flected by the planets in the Solar System is three orders of mag-nitude below its linear polarization, even if the planetary hemi-spheres above and below the planetary scattering plane, which contains the observer and the centers of star and planet, are ob-served separately (e.g., Kemp et al. 1971; Swedlund et al. 1972; Michalsky & Stokes 1974). If a planet is mirror-symmetric with respect to the scatter-ing plane, the circularly polarized fluxes of the two hemispheres have the same amplitude but opposite handedness, which is called the polar e ff ect. As a consequence, the circular polariza-tion of starlight reflected by unresolved planets or exoplanets is zero, unless an asymmetry between the hemispheres exists (e.g., Hansen 1971a; Hansen & Travis 1974; Wolstencroft 1976). For instance, the Saturnian ring system partially occults one hemi-sphere of Saturn for an observer on Earth, eliminating the polar eff ect and resulting in a net circular polarization for unresolved measurements (Smith & Wolstencroft 1983). For ocean planets and Earth analogs, the circularly polarized flux is four magnitudes smaller than the linearly polarized flux (García Muñoz 2015; Rossi & Stam 2018; Groot et al. 2020). Although polarimetry is currently more promising for giant ex-oplanets (Wiktorowicz et al. 2025), we are not aware of any de-tailed numerical studies that discuss their circular polarization. This study investigates the potential to characterize cloud layers in hydrogen-dominated planetary atmospheres by observ-ing the circular polarization that is due to the presence of a large-scale asymmetry at wavelengths from 0 .3 μm to 1 μm, with a fo-cus on 0 .5 μm. Sect. 2 introduces our framework for describing the polarization state and our calculations of the optical proper-ties of the cloud compositions considered. To facilitate the expla-nation of the connection between the optical properties of cloud particles and the circular polarization of light reflected by one hemisphere of an exoplanet, Sect. 3 introduces an interpretative second-scattering-order model. The findings are compared to a model including all scattering orders and an atmosphere consist-ing of hydrostatic gaseous layers and a cloud layer in Sect. 4. More realistic scenarios in which a nonzero circular polariza-tion is expected, such as circumplanetary rings, and the observ-ability of planetary circular polarization signals, are discussed in Sect. 5. We summarize our findings in Sect. 6.

Assuming spherical cloud particles is a common approxima-tion for modeling exoplanetary atmospheres (e.g., Rossi & Stam 2018; Benneke et al. 2019; Lietzow & Wolf 2022; Mullens et al. 2024; Wiktorowicz et al. 2025; Batalha et al. 2026). However, the polarization of the reflected light is sensitive to the shape of the scattering particles (Mishchenko & Travis 1994). For ex-ample, cuboid KCl particles induce polarization directions per-pendicular to those expected for spherical particles (Hamill et al. 2024). The presence of irregularly shaped aggregate particles in the atmospheres of Jupiter and Titan is inferred from polarimet-ric observations (West & Smith 1991; Dlugach & Mishchenko 2005). Modeling non-spherical particles is beyond the scope of this study. However, possible e ff ects of irregularly shaped particles are discussed qualitatively in Sect. 5. Depending on the temperature profile of a giant exoplanet, dif-ferent materials are expected to form clouds in the upper lay-ers of the atmosphere (Sánchez-Lavega et al. 2004; Allard et al. 2012; Morley et al. 2013). Ammonia and water ice are expected in atmospheres of cool gas giants at temperatures lower than 350 K (e.g., Atreya et al. 1999; Sánchez-Lavega et al. 2004). Graphite clouds are expected in atmospheres of rocky or gaseous exoplanets with high atmospheric C /O ratios at gas temper-atures of around 500 K (Herbort et al. 2022; Li et al. 2025). Other species discussed in this study are expected to condense in the atmospheres of hot exoplanets (Wakeford & Sing 2015; Kitzmann & Heng 2018; Helling 2019; Roman et al. 2021), with condensation temperatures reaching from about 700 K for KCl to more than 1800 K for corundum (Al 2O3) (e.g., Lodders 2002; Wakeford et al. 2017; Batalha et al. 2026).

Often, one change in the handedness of Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie occurs at an overall scat-tering angle, θgeom , close to 90 ◦, similar to the geometric e ff ect suggested by Kawata (1978). Given that circular polarization is induced when linearly polarized light is scattered by cloud parti-cles, Kawata (1978) argued that a resemblance of features in Ps

and Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie is expected, implying that Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie is dominated by process (i). While this is a major contribution for selected materials, other contributions are not negligible. 

3.1.1. Large imaginary part of the refractive index 

At a wavelength of 0 .5 μm, FeO has an imaginary part, k, of the refractive index of 0 .74, Fe 2O3 of 0 .78, graphite of 1 .2, FeS of 1 .4, iron of 3 .1, and chromium of 4 .5. For all of these ma-terials, the second scattering induces the largest circular polar-ization when linearly polarized radiation is scattered by a small angle, θ2, between 5 ◦ and 30 ◦ due to a positive peak in F34 /F11 

(see feature a in Fig. A.2). Therefore, the main contributions to 

Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie for these materials are from process (i) (see Fig. F.1 and F.2). Excluding Fe 2O3 at large wavelengths, Ps is positive at all scattering angles and considered wavelengths for the aforemen-tioned particle species (Lietzow & Wolf 2022). Thus, the hand-edness of Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie changes once at an overall scattering an-gle, θgeom , of approximately 80 ◦. For overall scattering angles, θ,smaller than θgeom , Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie is positive, while negative values occur when θ is larger than θgeom (see Fig. 2). The positive branch of Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie reaches higher absolute values, as Ps is larger at scattering angles smaller than θgeom (see Lietzow & Wolf 2022). The maximum of Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie is between 20 ◦ and 45 ◦ and moves to larger θ for larger wavelengths.

3.1.2. Influence of the real part of the refractive index 

For condensates with a complex part, k, of the refractive in-dex below 0 .01, the behavior of Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie is determined by the real part, n, of their refractive index. This includes water ice ( n = 1.31 at 0 .5 μm), ammonia ice (1 .44), KCl (1 .50), SiO 2 (1 .55), Mg 2SiO 4 (1 .62), MgAl 2O4 (1 .72), and Fe 2SiO 4

(1 .85) particles. For all seven materials, large absolute values of 

F34 /F11 are found in a negative plateau at scattering angles of between 130 ◦ and 170 ◦ (see feature b in Fig. A.2). Thus, process (ii) is equally important as process (i) for these materials. Only for the materials with the largest n, MgAl 2O4 and Fe 2SiO 4, is process (i) dominant. Process (ii) contributes less for MgAl 2O4

and Fe 2SiO 4, because the influences of the negative peak (b) and the positive peak (c) in F34 /F11 (see Fig. A.2) tend to cancel out. When k is small, the single scattering linear polarization, Ps,is predominantly negative (Lietzow & Wolf 2022), which results in an inverse general trend in the circular polarization compared to materials with large k. Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie is predominantly negative when θ is smaller than θgeom and positive if θ is larger than 

θgeom (see Fig. 2). Water ice, ammonia ice, and KCl have n of at most 1 .5 and θgeom around 110 ◦. Mg 2SiO 4, MgAl 2O4, and Fe 2SiO 4 have n larger than 1 .6 and θgeom close to 90 ◦. Between real parts of the refractive index of 1 .5 and 1 .6, a transition be-tween these behaviors occurs. For SiO 2, n is between 1 .58 and 1.54 at the considered wavelengths. 3.1.3. Variable imaginary part of the refractive index 

Our optical dataset includes materials for which k decreases with increasing wavelength (Kitzmann & Heng 2018, and refs. in Ta-ble 1). Out of these, Fe 2O3 and olivine were discussed in Sect. 3.1.1. Decreases in k are also found for Al 2O3, CaTiO 3, MnS, NaCl, SiO, and ZnS. At a wavelength of 0 .3 μm, the behavior of Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie is similar to particle species with high k discussed in Sect. 3.1.1. This behavior extends up to a θ and material-dependent wavelength λk. At λk, a transition occurs in the behav-ior of Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie and Ps (see Lietzow & Wolf 2022). For wave-lengths larger than λk, the behavior is more similar to that of materials in Sect. 3.1.2. For CaTiO 3, SiO, and ZnS, n is larger than 2 and process (i) dominates (see Fig. F.1 and F.2). For these materials, the negative plateau in Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie at large overall scattering angles extends to an overall scattering angle, θ, of 180 ◦ at wavelengths larger than λk, similar to the positive plateau in Ps (see Lietzow & Wolf 2022). For MnS, the negative plateau in Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie at large θ is not found. The positive plateau in Ps occurs only at scattering an-gles larger than 165 ◦ (Lietzow & Wolf 2022) and, consequently, no negative plateau in Pc2 ,Mie ,Mie is found (comp. App. F.1).

4. Radiative transfer simulations 

The results of Sect. 3 only include the first two scattering orders. In this section, we determine whether similar features occur in a more realistic scenario for a wavelength of 0 .5 μm. For this purpose, three-dimensional Monte Carlo radiative transfer simu-lations with POLARIS 1 (Reissl et al. 2016; Lietzow et al. 2021) were performed to calculate the planetary Stokes vector includ-ing contributions of all scattering orders. The atmospheric model used in the simulations (see Table 2) was identical to the one used in the study by Lietzow & Wolf (2022). The atmosphere was described on a logarithmically spaced pressure grid with a top pressure of 10 −5 bar and a bot-tom pressure of 100 bar. A cloud layer with homogeneous den-sity was placed between 1 bar and 0 .1 bar with an optical depth of 10 at a wavelength of 0 .5 μm. Note that the optical depth of the atmosphere is independent of its temperature profile in this setup, such that its choice has a negligible e ff ect on the re-sults. Additionally, the contribution of planetary thermal emis-sion to the total flux is insignificant at a wavelength of 0 .5 μm. For all simulations, the model atmosphere was therefore isother-mal. Cell borders and gas number densities were calculated as-suming hydrostatic equilibrium and an ideal gas using the for-mulae in Lietzow et al. (2021). The overall optical depth of the atmosphere was 28 at a wavelength of 0 .5 μm. As this would be a su ffi cient approximation of a semi-infinite atmosphere (e.g., Buenzli & Schmid 2009), the properties of the lower boundary of the atmosphere did not influence the results. Results are given as a function of the planetary phase an-gle, αp, in 5 ◦ steps. αp is the angle between the directions from the planet toward the star and the observer. An overview of the results for the total intensity, I, the linearly polarized intensity, 

Q, the circularly polarized intensity, V, and the intrinsic degree of circular polarization, Pc, is given in Fig. 4. As is common in numerical studies (e.g., Stam et al. 2004; Lietzow et al. 2021), the Stokes vector was normalized so that I equals the geometric albedo, Ag, of the planet at a planetary phase angle of 0 ◦. While I

and Q were integrated on the entire planet, V and Pc were calcu-lated for one planetary hemisphere. Realistic asymmetries such as circumplanetary rings are discussed in Sect. 5.

5. Discussion 

> 5.1. Influence of realistic asymmetries

So far, all circular polarization results have been computed for one planetary hemisphere, as Pc would otherwise be zero due to the polar e ff ect. In reality, a clear phase angle dependence of circular polarization is only possible as a consequence of stable large-scale asymmetries, because the planetary disk cannot be resolved in observations of exoplanets. For example, a large part of one hemisphere of the exoplanet could be occulted by a circumplanetary ring. For Saturn, the cir-cular polarization is much smaller for the rings than for the visi-ble disk at optical wavelengths (Swedlund et al. 1972). However, light scattered by the ring is linearly polarized (Kemp & Murphy 1973) and may still enhance the planetary circular polarization through a second scattering in the planetary atmosphere (Kawata 1978). Nevertheless, the small contribution to the circularly po-larized flux and the invariable viewing geometry of circumplan-etary rings around exoplanets would make them excellent targets for future missions aimed at observing circular polarization. Due to the additional scattered light flux, Fring , of the ring, the actual degree of circular polarization is smaller than the val-ues presented in this study. Additionally, a ring blocks only a fraction, fcover , of the flux of one hemisphere. The additional flux from the uncovered part further reduces the circular polarization. While this formula may serve as a fast approximation, a more thorough study using radiative transfer simulations is needed to quantify the influence of cir-cumplanetary rings. In particular, this would allow one to ac-count for the distribution of circularly polarized flux on one plan-etary hemisphere. Another option is the presence of asymmetries in the at-mospheric properties between the morning and evening ter-minators (e.g., Demory et al. 2013; Line & Parmentier 2016; MacDonald & Madhusudhan 2017; Coulombe et al. 2025). For example, a part of the planetary day side near the morning ter-minator might be covered by clouds. In contrast, the remainder would be cloud-free as clouds dissolve due to higher dayside temperatures (e.g., García Muñoz & Isaak 2015; Helling et al. 2019; Roman et al. 2021; Helling et al. 2021). When the incli-nation of the planetary orbit is not 90 ◦, the scattering plane is no longer equivalent to the orbital plane of the planet. In the most extreme scenario of a face-on orbit, it would be perpen-dicular to the orbital plane. If the asymmetry were stable over time, this would result in a nonzero Pc, although in a reduced range of phase angles. In this scenario, the additional flux of the cloud-free part again reduces the degree of circular polarization compared to the values presented in Sect. 4. The exact di ff er-ence depends on the cloud coverage percentage and the viewing geometry. 

> 5.2. Irregularly shaped particles

As discussed in Sect. 3.1.2, the sign of the circularly polarized flux of exoplanets depends on the direction of linear polarization after single scattering. For cubic KCl particles, this direction is measured to be perpendicular to what is expected for spherical particles (Hamill et al. 2024). In that case, the handedness of Pc

would also be opposite to that found in our results. Due to the lack of strong scattering-angle-dependent features in the matrix elements for irregularly shaped particles (see, e.g., Hamill et al. 2024), the behavior of Pc would otherwise be as featureless as in Sect. 4.2, with a single change in the handedness of circular polarization around 90 ◦.Because the handedness of circular polarization is connected to the direction of linear polarization, the phase curve of Pc re-mains predictable. Consequently, it is still possible to use Pc for characterizing the ring inclination, as described in Sect. 4.2, if the linear polarization is also measured. In addition, the form of the scattering matrix is similar to Eq. (5) if particles are ran-domly aligned and the particle ensemble includes mirror images of all particles (van de Hulst 1957). However, if particles are not only irregularly shaped but also homochiral, the matrix element F14 is no longer zero (van de Hulst 1957; Wolstencroft 1976). This eliminates the po-lar e ff ect and may result in a larger intrinsic planetary circular polarization, which might even serve as a biomarker for terres-trial exoplanets (Sparks et al. 2009a,b, 2012, 2021). 

> 5.3. Observability and star-planet interactions

Not a single cloud composition considered in this study resulted in an amplitude of Pc of more than 6 · 10 −4, even though the stel-lar flux was ignored and the Stokes vector was integrated only on one planetary hemisphere. When the exoplanet and its host star are not separated in observations, the planetary contribution

to the circular polarization of the entire system is at most 10 −8

for all condensates in our model and at most 10 −9 for SiO 2, as-suming a planetary radius of 1 Jovian radius and a star planet dis-tance of 0 .03 au (similar to HD 189733b, see, e.g., Addison et al. 2019). Polarization levels on the order of a few times 10 −9 are well below the precision of a few parts per million achiev-able for exoplanets with current polarimeters such as HIPPI-2 (Bailey et al. 2020) or POLISH-2 (Wiktorowicz et al. 2023). Ad-ditionally, even the disk-integrated circular polarization of the quiet Sun is on the order of 0 .1 ppm (Kemp et al. 1987). For more active stars, star spots cause a disk-integrated circular po-larization of hundreds of parts per million (see Elias & Dorren 1990; Wiktorowicz et al. 2025). While the linear polarization of HD 189733b measured by Wiktorowicz et al. (2025) is well explained by small SiO 2 par-ticles in the atmosphere of the planet, the circular polarization of the HD 189733 system is clearly of a di ff erent origin. This supports the hypothesis by Wiktorowicz et al. (2025) that the observed circular polarization is due to star spots induced by star-planet interactions. If circular polarization signals due to star spots, which are locked to the planetary phase angle, are ubiqui-tous for hot Jupiter systems, this severely limits the prospects of ever observing the degree of circular polarization of hot exoplan-ets. Star-planet interactions lead to a plethora of e ff ects, includ-ing planetary material loss, which produces spatially asymmetric features such as gaseous tails (e.g., Matsakos et al. 2015; Vidotto 2025), thereby obscuring planetary circular polarization signals. Measuring the circular polarization of exoplanets, therefore, requires technical advances in the measurement of circular polar-ization, very stable calibrations, very limited linear polarization cross-talk, and precise modeling of star spot circular polariza-tion. Ideally, the planet is resolved from its host star, which will be possible for cool gas giants with future coronagraphic mis-sions such as the Habitable Worlds Observatory, thereby mini-mizing stellar background signals (Min et al. 2025). 

6. Conclusion 

Using an interpretative model of a planet with a homogeneous semi-infinite atmosphere, we analyzed the correlation between the optical properties of cloud particles, the planetary geome-try, and the circular polarization of radiation that was scattered twice in its atmosphere for wavelengths between 0 .3 and 1 μm. To compare these results to realistic circular polarization phase curves, we performed three-dimensional Monte Carlo radiative transfer simulations for a wavelength of 0 .5 μm. Our planetary model consisted of a hydrostatic atmosphere and a cloud layer as in the study by Lietzow & Wolf (2022). As circular polarization cancels out in observations of symmetric planets due to the polar eff ect (e.g., Hansen 1971b; Wolstencroft 1976; Kawata 1978), all results were integrated solely on the planetary hemispheres above the scattering plane. Depending on the complex refractive index of the considered cloud particle species, we find characteristic features in the in-trinsic degree of circular polarization, Pc, of the hemispheres of model exoplanets as a function of the planetary phase angle, αp.However, the largest values of Pc are smaller than 3 · 10 −4 for all considered cloud condensates. The general trends of Pc found in our second scattering order model also appear in our multiple scattering results for all con-sidered cloud condensates. We conclude that circular polariza-tion of starlight reflected by clouds in exoplanetary atmospheres arises mainly from the second scattering order, as presumed by Hansen (1971b), among others. In our planetary model, the dom-inant contribution comes from double scattering by cloud parti-cles for materials with small k ≲ 0.1, and from scattering by gaseous molecules first, followed by scattering by cloud parti-cles for k ≳ 0.1. Multiple scattering and the influence of gaseous molecules reduce the amplitude of Pc. For planetary models that include cloud condensates with small k such as H 2O, NH 3, or KCl, char-acteristic features found in Pc for the second scattering order vanish in multiple scattering calculations. The resulting behavior is very predictable, with a single phase-angle-dependent change in the sign of Pc between αp of 90 ◦ and 100 ◦. Therefore, mea-suring Pc would allow for a characterization of the underlying asymmetry. In particular, it would allow one to determine which half of a planet is covered by a circumplanetary ring. Circular polarization phase curves of planetary models that include cloud particle species with high k, such as graphite, iron, FeS, FeO, Fe 2O3, or chromium, show distinct features caused by a change in the handedness of circular polarization at charac-teristic, phase-angle-dependent wavelengths when scattering by gaseous molecules before scattering by cloud particles is dom-inant. As the linear polarization behavior is very similar for all materials with k ≳ 0.8 (see Lietzow & Wolf 2022), precise mea-surements of circular polarization would allow for a better dif-ferentiation of these cloud particle species. As the wavelength dependence corresponds to a dependence on the size parameter 

xeff , it would also allow for a more accurate determination of the eff ective particle radius. In summary, the circular polarization of starlight reflected by giant exoplanets is sensitive to cloud particle composition and at-mospheric asymmetries. It is typically three orders of magnitude smaller than the linear polarization, and therefore challenging to detect in unresolved observations due to stellar contamination (see Wiktorowicz et al. 2025). Nonetheless, it o ff ers a promising complementary tool for atmospheric characterization with fu-ture high-precision instruments and coronagraphic observations of exoplanets.

F.2. Relevance of the second scattering angle for iron 

For the purpose of interpreting the influence of cloud particle species on the circular polarization of the second scattering or-der in Sect. 3, it is useful to visualize Vi j (θ, θ 1, ϕ 1) · P′(θ, θ 1, ϕ 1),

which is the integrand in Eq. (15). Vi j P′ quantifies the contri-bution of the set of paths defined by θ, θ1, and ϕ1 to the overall circularly polarized flux of the second scattering order V2,i j . As an example, VMie ,Mie P′ is shown for scattering by iron particles for a wavelength of 0 .5 μm in Fig. F.3. Gray contour lines show values of θ2 that are computed according to Eq. (B.2). The main contribution for iron is from process (i), that is, scattering in a direction of θ1 ≈ θ first. In Fig. F.3, this corre-sponds to a positive and a negative feature of VMie ,Mie P′ at small azimuthal scattering angles ϕ1. If θ1 is slightly smaller than θ,negative polarization is induced. If θ1 is slightly larger than θ,positive polarization is induced. Both features span a similar range ∆θ1 of about 15 ◦. The second scattering is roughly for-ward by a scattering angle θ2 of less than 30 ◦.At overall scattering angles θ above 90 ◦, two shallow fea-tures spread over a wide range of azimuthal scattering angles ϕ1.A positive feature is found for values of the overall scattering angle θ of 120 ◦ and 150 ◦ and for first scattering angles θ1 mostly larger than 90 ◦ in Fig. F.3. These directions correspond to second scattering angles between 30 ◦ and 90 ◦. A negatively polarized feature is found at θ1 below 90 ◦ in directions corresponding to second scattering angles of more than 90 ◦. When integrated on 

ϕ1, these features result in the additional positive and negative features found in Fig. F.1 (see also Sect. 3.1.1). When θ is less than 90 ◦, an additional positive contribution appears for paths where θ1 is between 0 ◦ and 60 ◦, while ϕ1 is close to 90 ◦ (see Fig. F.3). These contributions cause a faint pos-itive feature in Fig. F.1 for overall scattering angles θ below 90 ◦.This contribution is significantly more prominent for FeS at a wavelength of 0 .5 μm in Fig. F.1.
