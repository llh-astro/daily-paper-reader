ABSTRACT 

Context. The composition of rocky planets depends on the dust in their natal protoplanetary disk (PPD), potentially containing water ice. Crystalline water ice was detected in the PPD d216-0939 in the Orion Nebular Cluster (ONC). 

Aims. We aim at constraining the spatial distribution and crystallization state of water ice in the d216-0939 disk using recent obser-vations of the water ice absorption feature at a wavelength of ∼3 μm collected with JWST. 

Methods. We perform 3D Monte Carlo radiative transfer (MCRT) simulations to constrain the free parameters of an accretion disk model by fitting the calculated spectrum in the wavelength range from 1 .6 μm to 24 μm to the spectral energy distribution (SED) observed with the JWST instruments NIRSpec and MIRI. Additionally, archival, spatially resolved HST observations were used to constrain the global spatial structure of the disk, as the parameter space is degenerate with respect to the fit to the SED. Successively, we fit the water ice absorption feature using polychromatic MCRT simulations with spectral importance sampling to produce synthetic observations at high spectral resolutions. 

Results. By probing the upper and outer disk layers, we found that a PPD model with dust containing 5 .4 % crystallized water ice beyond the snowline fits the observations well, necessitating outward transport of material, since crystalline ice is unlikely to form in situ in the probed disk layers. In the spectral region of the water ice absorption feature, scattering and thermal dust emission both contribute significantly to the total flux. 

Key words. protoplanetary disks – radiative transfer – stars: individual: d216-0939 – stars: variables: T Tauri, Herbig Ae /Be 

1. Introduction 

Observations of protoplanetary disks (PPDs), the progenitors of planetary systems, allow us to study the physical conditions in which planet formation takes place (e.g., Keppler et al. 2018; van Capelleveen et al. 2025). The presence of ices in PPDs im-pacts the process of planet formation, as their freeze-out from the gas phase defines snowlines in the disks (e.g., Öberg et al. 2011; Leemker et al. 2026). Solid-state water is the most abun-dant ice in astrophysical environments (e.g., van Dishoeck et al. 2021; Ballering et al. 2021) and its spatial distribution in PPDs is linked to the water delivery process to forming planets (e.g., Sato et al. 2016; Topchieva et al. 2024; Williams et al. 2025). For Earth, this process is not yet conclusively explained by the-ories on the formation and evolution of the Solar System (e.g., Hartmann et al. 2017; O’Brien et al. 2018). Water ice was found in many PPDs (e.g., Schegerer & Wolf 2010; Aikawa et al. 2012; Terada et al. 2012; Terada & Toku-naga 2017), with an almost edge-on geometry favoring the de-tection via the stretching mode of H 2O ice in transmission with a minimum at a wavelength of ∼3 μm (“water ice feature”). Ob-servations with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) in the infrared (IR) reveal complex chemistry of ices in PPDs (e.g., Rocha et al. 2024; Potapov et al. 2025). The shape of the water ice feature is not only influenced by the chemical composition and crystallinity of the ice but additionally depends on the grain size and disk geometry (e.g., Pontoppidan et al. 2005; Ballering et al. 2021; Sturm et al. 2023b; Dartois et al. 2024; Martinien et al. 2025b). Detailed studies of the PPDs HH 48 NE (Sturm et al. 2023c,b,a; Bergner et al. 2024), PDS 453 (Martinien et al. 2024), Tau 042021 (Dartois et al. 2025), and d114-426 (Ballering et al. 2025) demonstrate the usefulness of 3D radiative transfer (RT) simulations to draw conclusions on the physical origin of the water ice feature. These studies show that the spatial structure of PPDs, i.e., large inner cavities in HH 48 NE and PDS 453, a tilted inner disk in d114-426, and a disk wind in Tau 042021, influences the appearance of edge-on disks. Additionally, scat-tered radiation shifts the minimum of ice features (Martinien et al. 2025b), may saturate them (Martinien et al. 2025a), and, depending on the inclination angle of the disk, may lead to ex-cess emission in the ice features (e.g., Dartois et al. 2022). The silhouette disk d216-0939 in the Orion Nebular Cluster (ONC), which we investigate in this work, was observed with the Hubble Space Telescope (HST; Smith et al. 2005). Here, the shadow of the PPD is seen against the H α background emission of the outskirts of the ONC. Observations of the disk have been obtained at (sub-)millimeter wavelengths with the Submillime-ter Array (SMA; Mann & Williams 2009), the Very Large Ar-ray (VLA; Sheehan et al. 2016), and, finally, the Atacama Large Millimeter /submillimeter Array (ALMA; Mann et al. 2014; Fac-

3. Disk and dust model 

The PPD model consists of a rotationally symmetric 3D dust density distribution described in Sect. 3.1. The dust is modeled as a continuous grain size population of spherical compact dust grains of di ff erent chemical and mineralogical composition (see Sect. 3.2). As we aim to reproduce the observations from visual to mid-IR wavelengths, we only consider the small grain popu-lation in the disk. Finally, the water ice content and spatial distri-bution is modeled via incorporation of a snowline, as described in Sect. 3.4.

3.4. Water ice inclusion 

While icy grains are often modeled as dust cores covered by an ice mantle (e.g., Dartois 2006; Dartois et al. 2022; Sturm et al. 2023b), we use measured ( n, κ )-values of silicate-water ice mix-tures (MgSiO 3/H2O; Potapov et al. 2018, 2021, see Sect. D) to include water ice in the dust model, which is motivated by the best-fit chemical composition of the observed water ice feature found by Potapov et al. (2025). Consequently, we use MgSiO 3/H2O optical constants, ( n, κ ), with a silicate-to-water ice mass ratio of 2 .7 (Potapov et al. 2018). We consider water ice in amorphous and crystalline form using 100 K and 150 K silicate-water ice optical constants, ( n, κ ), measured at the re-spective temperatures, as the onset of crystallization occurs be-tween 120 K and 140 K (e.g., Jenniskens & Blake 1994; Mifsud et al. 2022). 4. Radiative transfer simulations 

Our modeling approach is based on RT simulations to calcu-late the SED and spatially resolved images for the disk model within the considered parameter space. Both scattered radiation as well as thermal reemission radiation of the dust, based on self-consistently calculated spatial dust temperature distributions, are considered.

5.1. Reference model 

The parameter space (Table 2) is highly degenerate with respect to the fit to the SED. While the maximum grain size, flaring ex-ponent, stellar luminosity, and reference scale height are con-strained to amax = 1 μm, β = 1.250, L⋆ > 0.75 L ⊙, and href >

12 .5 au, the disk mass, outer radius, and inclination remain un-constrained within the explored parameter ranges. We assessed the suitability of 12 best-fit candidates with χ2cont < 0.378, la-beled a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, k, and l, by increasing χ2cont , to represent a continuum baseline in the wavelength range 2 .6 μm to 4 .0 μm, selecting models a, e, f, h, k, and l to compare to HST observa-tions. The selected and rejected models are shown in Figs. 2 and E.1, respectively, with their parameter values listed in Table E.1. To produce synthetic observations comparable to the HST Hα-image (Smith et al. 2005) a constant background flux inten-sity Ibg (λ) = fbg × Bλ(Tbg ), with a scaling factor, fbg , and the blackbody function Bλ, is incorporated into the radiative transfer modeling, neglecting scattering of background radiation. We as-sume a background temperature of Tbg = 10 000 K, as expected in the outskirts of the Orion Nebula (Balick et al. 1974). The appearance of the disk in H α for pre-selected models is shown next to the HST observations in Fig. 3. Background intensity scaling factors of 1 × 10 −13 , 1 .75 × 10 −13 , 2 .25 × 10 −13 ,and 4 ×10 −13 were used for the models a, e, h, and k, respectively. The comparison of normalized fluxes 2 shows that the mod-els a and e overestimate the spatial extent of the emission sig-nificantly (which is also the case for the models f and l). The outer radius is therefore constrained to Rout ≤ 400 au. Based on visual inspection of the shape and extent of the emission region, we select model k with an inclination angle of 77 .5◦ as best fit to the continuum. The synthetic image of model h ( ι = 72 .5◦) is also in good agreement with the HST observations. However, the flux overestimation extends over a larger area and reaches higher amplitudes above the disk midplane than for model k, as shown in the di ff erence images in Fig. 3. > 5.2. Impact of water ice in the disk on the SED

Based on the dust temperatures of the reference model, we include water ice into the model beyond the snowline (see Sect. 3.4). Successively, scattering and dust emission simula-tions are performed to compute flux densities employing the spectral importance sampling method with a wavelength bin-width of ∆λ = 0.1 μm (see Sects. 4.2 and B). 

> 5.2.1. Disk temperature structure

The dust temperatures, T , of the best-fit model, k, are shown in Fig. 4. The position of the snowline at Tsnow = 170 K strongly depends on the elevation above the midplane, as the inner rim of the disk shields the disk regions close to the midplane from irra-diation by the host star. Water ice condenses at ∼5 au, measured from the central star along the LOS, and dust temperatures drop rapidly to 100 K at ∼10 au. In the midplane of the disk, water ice can condense in-place at distances of ≲1 au from the central star. Considering, that water ice anneals into a crystalline phase at ∼130 K (Jenniskens & Blake 1994; Mifsud et al. 2022), the region in the disk model, in which we assume crystalline water ice to form, is small compared to the disk dimensions. 

> 5.2.2. Dust model fit to the water ice feature

To constrain the abundance and crystallinity of water ice we per-formed MCRT simulations using the dust mixtures # 1–6 listed in Table 1. The resulting SEDs are shown in Fig. 5. The flux overestimation, with respect to the reference model, at wave-lengths outside the water ice feature can be attributed to the dif-ference in scattering opacities (see Fig. 1), which impacts the LOS extinction. The position of the flux minimum matches the observations only for the 150 K silicate-water ice mixtures, i.e., the dust mixtures containing crystalline ice. The synthetic SED of dust mixture # 4 resembles the observed SED best with re-spect to the shape and depth of the water ice feature. To improve the fit to the observations, we artificially lower the bulk density of dust mixture # 4 and adjust its water-ice con-tent. The artificially lowered density is e ff ectively a dust density discontinuity at the snowline, which is physically justified as wa-ter is expected to freeze out from the gas phase and adds up to the total dust mass. We found that an artificial dust density of 

ρart mix = 1.65 g cm −3 and a MgSiO 3/H2O component mass frac-tion of 0 .2, resulting in a total silicate-to-ice mass ratio of 6 .63,fit the observations best as shown in Fig. 6. The fit to the water ice absorption feature is very good in a wavelength range of 3 .0 μm to 3 .2 μm, and the overall shape of the feature is well reproduced. The water ice content of 5 .4 % is well constrained by the depth of the feature. The systematic over-estimation of flux in the red wing of the absorption feature can be attributed to the lack of the “chemistry” component in our model, which would increase the opacity in the corresponding wave-length range (see Potapov et al. 2025). This component contains spectral signatures primarily associated with ammonium carba-mate and carbamic acid. It is required to concurrently reproduce the wing shape of the 3 μm water ice absorption feature and some distinct absorption features in the 6 μm to 8 μm range in the compositional analysis of the d216-0939 spectrum reported by Potapov et al. (2025). However, the “chemistry” component could not be incorporated into the radiative transfer model be-cause the required refractive indices are not available. 5.2.3. Discussion 

The water ice mass fraction of ∼5 % beyond the snowline is low compared to the DSHARP water-ice mass fraction of 20 % (Birnstiel et al. 2018). However, the NIRSpec observations probe the upper and outer disk layers (Potapov et al. 2025), while grain growth (e.g., Birnstiel 2024) and successive dust settling of larger grains (e.g., Dubrulle et al. 1995; Schräpler & Henning 2004; Brunngräber & Wolf 2020; Sturm et al. 2023c; Dartois et al. 2025) may result in a vertical ice mass fraction gradient, with the ice mass increasing toward the disk midplane. Further-more, photodesorption (e.g., Hogerheijde et al. 2011; Arabhavi et al. 2022) could reduce the ice mass fraction in the upper disk layers. The optical depth from the observer to the central star of the best-fit model of d216-0939 at the minimum of the water feature

To compare the optical depth of only the water ice to previous results, we roughly estimate the optical depth of the water ice in the dust mixture as τ ext →⋆λ=3.1 μm − τ ext →⋆λ=2.8 μm = 0.2, which is less than the value of ∼0.6 found by Terada & Toku-naga (2012) and Potapov et al. (2025) using a 1D LOS extinc-tion model. Crystalline water ice in the disk atmosphere at mod-erate optical depths, as found for d216-0939, was also detected by Schegerer & Wolf (2010) in the young stellar object YLW 16 A. Sturm et al. (2023b) derive a similar optical depth of 0 .3 for the water ice feature in the upper layers of the PPD of HH 48 NE from spatially resolved JWST observations. The temperature structure of the best-fit disk model, shown in Fig. 4, provides evidence for outward material transport, e.g., through a disk wind (e.g., Kwan et al. 2007; Meskini et al. 2024; Dartois et al. 2025), as crystalline ice is present in disk regions, where temperatures are far below the crystallization temperature of water ice. Dust grains of sizes ∼1 μm containing water ice are present in the upper disk layers of HH 48 NE (Sturm et al. 2023b, 2024) and d114-426 (Ballering et al. 2025). While the ice detected in HH 48 NE is predominantly in an amorphous form, which is indicated by the round shape of the measured absorption fea-ture, the crystalline ice in d216-0939 results in a sharp mini-mum of the water feature. Bergner et al. (2026) find amorphous water ice in a sample of five other edge-on disks, making the pronounced crystalline feature detected in d216-0939 a notable outlier. As HH 48 NE is more inclined ( ι = 82 .3◦; Sturm et al. 2023c) than d216-0939, a vertical crystallinity gradient of water ice in PPDs could explain the observations. Additionally, Sturm et al. (2024) hypothesize that a dusty disk wind above the disk could be necessary to shield the water ice in the upper disk lay-ers from photodesorption by ultraviolet irradiation and Dartois et al. (2025) find icy dust grains at high disk altitudes and a disk wind in Tau 042021 ( ι = 87 .5◦). Assuming a similar morphol-ogy to these disks, the observations of d216-0939 could probe the extended atmosphere or a disk wind, in which crystalline ice formed close to the snowline is transported outward. To test this hypothesis, spatially resolved observations of d216-0939 in the spectral range of the water feature are necessary. The outward material transport, successive grain growth and dust settling toward the midplane impact the chemical composi-tion of dust available for planet formation. As the atmospheric compositions of exoplanets are used as tracers of their formation histories (e.g., He et al. 2026), possible radial material trans-port in their natal disks needs to be considered. Additionally, amorphous water ice traps volatiles, such as CO and CO 2 (e.g., Collings et al. 2003), which are released by volcanic desorption upon crystallization of the water ice (Smith et al. 1997; Williams et al. 2025). Consequently, the distribution and crystallinity of the water ice couple to the spatial distribution and chemistry of other ices. A dedicated model of the CO 2 ice feature of d216-0939 (see Potapov et al. 2025) is necessary to assess the coupling of water ice crystallinity and distribution to the abundances of other ice species. 

6. Conclusions 

We found a 3D model of the density structure and water ice dis-tribution of the PPD d216-0939, that fits the SED in the near- and mid-IR, observed with JWST, as well as the spatially resolved HST flux density map in H α. The best-fit model was analyzed to gain new insights into the spatial distribution of water ice in the PPD, which was modeled via MCRT simulations of a passively heated disk including scattering. 

– The SED in the near- and mid-IR probes the upper disk lay-ers, which host dust grains up to a size of ∼1 μm. This dust grain population is estimated to account for ∼10 −4 M⊙ of the total dust mass in the d216-0939 disk, resulting in a mod-erate optical depth from the central star to the observer of 

∼2 at the minimum of the water ice feature. We estimate the inclination angle of the edge-on disk as ∼78 ◦.

– Direct starlight, attenuated along the LOS, accounts for up to ∼70 % of the total flux in the spectral region of the water ice feature. The flux contributions by scattering and thermal emission of hot dust in the PPD account for ≤ 40 %. 

– We estimate the water ice mass fraction in the upper and outer disk layers beyond the snowline as ∼5 %. 

– Crystalline water ice is present in the upper disk layers at temperatures far below the crystallization temperature of wa-ter ice, which is indirect evidence for outward material trans-port in the upper disk layers of the PPD. The developed framework to model the water ice feature can be applied to JWST observations of other edge-on disks (e.g., Bergner et al. 2026).
