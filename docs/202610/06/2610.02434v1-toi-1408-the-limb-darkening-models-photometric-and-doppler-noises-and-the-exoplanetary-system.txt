Abstract We perform a self-consistent analysis of Doppler and photometric TESS and ground-based transit observations available for TOI-1408, known to host a system with two planets orbiting near 2:1 mean-motion commensurability and a distant companion. In general we confirm its orbital configuration derived in previous studies, however we found that the likelihood function contains multiple local maxima spread over a wider paramet-ric domain, thus making some model uncertainties significantly larger than deemed be-fore. In particular, the planet b radius still appears poorly constrained (from ∼ 1.2 RJup 

to ∼ 3.5 RJup ) because of the near-tangential transit geometry. We also show that the de-rived model is very prior-dependent regarding the astrophysical (spectrum-derived) con-straints on the stellar mass M⋆ and radius R⋆. While such estimations may potentially contribute a significant information to the fit, their prior distribution must be accurately specified to be useful. Presently so accurate characterization (e.g. the correlation between 

M⋆ and R⋆) is not available in the literature. 

Key words: techniques: photometric — techniques: radial velocities — methods: data analysis — planetary systems — stars: individual: TOI-1408 

1 INTRODUCTION 

TOI-1408 b is a TESS-detected planet 1 with an orbital period of Pb ≃ 4.42 d and a radius initially estimated by rb = 0 .700 ±0.055 RJup . It was confirmed by Galazutdinov et al. (2023) based on Doppler measurements acquired in 2021-2023 by the FFOREST spectrograph mounted at the SAO RAS 6-m telescope BTA-6 (Valyavin et al. 2022a,b). According to them, the planet mass appeared to be 1.69 ±

0.20 MJup , but the planet radius was ill-fitted because of the tangential transit geometry, with a low limit of about 1 RJup (thus the initial TESS-derived rb was rejected). Additionally, the Doppler curve implied a significant non-sinusoidal shape which was consistent with the orbital eccentricity of eb ≃ 0.25 . It was also guessed that such an eccentricity could possibly indicate a presence of other unseen companions in the system. This analysis did not consider potential transit timing variations (TTVs). Later on, Korth et al. (2024) published a massive set of new Doppler observations and ground-based photometry for TOI-1408 obtained at multiple sites, as well as their analysis. They argued that there are two planets in the 2:1 mean motion resonance, with the second small planet c with rc = 2 .22 ± 0.06 R⊕

moving on an internal orbit. The second planet is transiting as well, although its transits cannot be   

> 1https://exofop.ipac.caltech.edu/tess/view_toi.php arXiv:2610.02434v1 [astro-ph.EP] 1 Oct 2026 2R. V. Baluev et al.

detected individually. The planets are gravitationally perturbing each other, causing observationally detectable TTVs with the amplitude of ∼ 8 min on planet b and ∼ 6 − 8 hrs on planet c. Their re-sults in fact confirm the initial guess by Galazutdinov et al. (2023) about the existence of additional planets. However, contrary to them Korth et al. (2024) obtained a robustly determinable planet radius corresponding to rb = 2 .2 ± 0.4 RJup . Additionally, Korth et al. (2024) noticed a significant nonlinear Doppler trend indicating the presence of an outer long-period body, however its orbital parameters and mass could not be determined reliably. As we can see, the treatment of this system appears rather complicated and involving multiple subtle factors that may potentially affect the results significantly. The following issues have so far been insufficiently studied. 1. The limb darkening law should adequately reproduce the near-limb range, because of the near-tangential geometry of the planet b transit. Notice that Korth et al. (2024) tried to fit the limb dark-ening coefficients directly from the photometric observations, but this resulted in ill-fitted model in fact. The limb darkening is important to know to correctly assess exoplanetary radii. 2. The star’s astrophysical (spectrum-derived) mass and radius assessments appear quite different in Galazutdinov et al. (2023) and Korth et al. (2024). Probably both are not entirely reliable. It is nec-essary to verify these estimations and investigate how this information actually affects the derived system parameters. 3. Doppler and photometric noises, possible systematic errors in the data were not investigated in pre-vious works in sufficient detail. While Korth et al. (2024) tried to reduce stellar rotation by adding a sinusoidal stellar rotational signal, the actual quality of such a model (e.g. the residual noises, including non-white noise) was not assessed in sufficient detail. 4. Additional subtle but potentially important effects need to be assessed, for example the Rømer effect (variable light arrival delay) that the outermost body induces on the inner two-planet subsystem. The goal of the present work is to perform a more detailed analysis treating the issues listed above, and also taking into account new TESS observations of TOI-1408 that appeared since Korth et al. (2024). The paper structure is as follows. In Sect. 2 we investigate the limb darkening models for TOI-1408. Sect. 3 describes the input Doppler and photometric data and their preliminary analysis, in particular their noises. Sect. 4 presents the self-consistent analysis of these data as well as the derived orbital configuration of the planetary system.

Such theoretically constructed LDLs can be helpful but may involve systematic errors. For example, Baluev et al. (2015) revealed minor systematic biases in the a and b values from Claret & Bloemen (2011), as compared to the ones obtained by fitting transit data directly. However, the latter approach of direct fitting does not always work well. In the case of TOI-1408 an attempt to fit the quadratic LDL without resorting to theoretic predictions for a and b was made by Korth et al. (2024), but they obtained an ill-fitted value for b. Unfortunately, possible errors in theoretical Ik or Lk models are difficult to predict because of their complicated nature. One can list the following three major levels where such errors can appear: 1. uncertainties of the star spectral parameters Teff , log g, [Fe/H], and vturb ;2. inaccuracy of the analytical law (1,2) approximating numerically computed LDL; 3. inaccuracies in the stellar atmosphere physics assumed when computing that raw LDL. Here we aim to characterize all three types of the LDL errors in the TOI-1408 case; for shortness they are now referred to as ‘level-1’, ‘level-2’, and ‘level-3’ errors. Possible level-1 errors can be characterized by Monte Carlo simulations based on an estimation of spectral parameters ( Teff , log g, [Fe/H], vturb ). Here we used a remodelling of HARPSN spectra from a forthcoming work Galazutdinov et al. According to Claret (2017), the quartic LDL should have good accuracy for all tabulated spectral parameters and over the entire stellar disc, but this model is difficult for practical transit modeling because of compli-cated mathematics. Quadratic LDL is much more practical in this aspect: analytic formulae are avail-able for the exoplanetary transit curve along with its parametric derivatives (Mandel & Agol 2002; Abubekerov & Gostev 2013), as well as for the Rossiter-McLaughlin effect (Baluev & Shaidulin 2015). However, the quadratic LDL may not fit the intensity over the entire stellar disc equally well. In partic-ular, its error may increase near the limb. 3 OBSERVATIONS AND THEIR NOISES 3.1 Photometry 

Regarding the TESS observations of TOI-1408, we used the same 12 Sectors as in Korth et al. (2024), but also added newer observations for six more Sectors 77, 78, 79, 84, 85, and 86, downloadable from the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes 4. Notice that several TESS data products are offered that correspond to different processing levels of the same observations. Korth et al. (2024) used the PDC (Presearch Data Conditioning) data for their analysis. However based on a comparative investigation detailed in Baluev & Sokov (2025) we prefer the DVT (Data Validation Timeseries) LC INIT data. Most of the time these data differ from the PDC ones by quite a small amount, but they correct for minor edge-jumps effects likely related to the satellite momentum dumps occurring every few days. Before proceeding to the final analysis, we investigated the quality of these data. For example, we needed to ensure that there is no significant stellar variability, which was not demonstrated by Korth et al. (2024) clearly. Similarly to what was done in (Baluev & Sokov 2025) for WASP-33, we cut away all exoplanetary transits from the TESS data to keep only possible intrinsic stellar variations. Notice that transit times are not strictly periodic because of mutual planetary perturbations, so we used transit times derived from a preliminary fit similar to the ones discussed in Sect. 4 below (we omit

The resulting out-of-transit TESS data were then split per 18 sectors, and for each sector we computed a DCDFT (or GLS) periodogram (Ferraz-Mello 1981; Zechmeister & K¨ urster 2009). To keep only the systematic noise we averaged all these single-sector periodograms between sectors, and such intersector-averaged periodogram is shown in Fig 3. We can see a large power excess in the period range from ∼ 1 d to ∼ 10 d, likely indicating some photometric variability (or possibly instrumental effects). If we analysed TESS data on a per-sector basis we would need to model it in some way. However, we do not need to model each Sector in full, since we only need to capture individual planetary transits which have a much shorter duration ( ∼ 1.6 hr for planet b

and ∼ 2.6 hr for planet c). The power excess noticed above is small at such timescales so it is unlikely to affect planetary transit lightcurves. Therefore, to get rid of this variation we extracted only limited segments of TESS data centered on each transit ( ±2 hr for planet b and ±3 hr for planet c). Inside of each such segment the variability under discussion would manifest itself, at the worst, as a long-term trend which can be effectively modelled by a polynomial. 5 We also investigated each of single-sector periodograms and ensured that none revealed any significant individual peaks inside our sensitive period range. Korth et al. (2024) also released a set of 18 ground-based lightcurves of planet b transits. We tested these data for possible presence of variations as well. Based on a preliminary fit we computed the cor-responding residuals and performed their periodogram analysis (see Fig 4). Four of these lightcurves revealed statistically significant long-term variations, namely those from the telescopes M3 ( z-band, taken at 2021 Oct 14), NOT ( i-band, 2022-08-20 and 2022-08-28), Palomar ( K-band, 2022-09-02). Some of these variations may look rather marginal, but nevertheless we decided to remove all the af-fected lightcurves from our final analysis. An important notice is that periodograms in Fig. 4 remain practically independent of the choice of a likelihood maximum discussed in Sect. 4. 

3.2 Doppler data 

First high-precision Doppler data for TOI-1408 were published by Galazutdinov et al. (2023), which contained 19 RV measurements acquired by the new FFOREST spectrograph mounted at 6-m BTA telescope in Special Astrophysical Observatory (SAO) in Russia. Further on, Korth et al. (2024) released a large set of additional Doppler measurements obtained at multiple other sites. In total we have 12 independent Doppler datasets corresponding to either different telescopes or, in some cases, to different detectors on the same telescope, or to different spectrum calibration methods. In our self-consistent analysis below we aim to include all these data. Korth et al. (2024) noticed that Doppler data may be affected by stellar rotation which they modelled by adding “a sinusoidal stellar rotational signal”. However, such stellar activity (including rotationally-modulated one) does not usually manifest itself as a literal sinusoid. Usually it is a quasiperiodic Doppler variation representing an autocorrelated random process. It is unlikely that modelling such a variation through a deterministic function like sinusoid may offer reliable results. One standard way to treat such data is the Gaussian Process (GP) method relying on some parametric model for the correlation function (Baluev 2011, 2013b; Rajpaul et al. 2015; Foreman-Mackey et al. 2017; Baluev 2018). However, before that we should investigate the spectrum of this noise to decide which particular model to choose. Based on a preliminary fit that assumed, besides the inner planets b and c, only white noise in all Doppler data and a quadratic RV trend to model the long-period companion d, we derived the RV residuals and computed their periodograms per each of 12 subsets. These are shown in Fig 5, middle column. In the most cases there is no significant individual peak, although many periodograms reveal a clear power excess in different frequency ranges. To verify this we passed these data through the red noise detection algorithm of P LANET PACK software (Baluev 2018). A significant red noise was detected in 7 datasets. However, these periodograms reveal no obvious hint of a consistent stellar rotation period. Moreover, the power spectrum looks quite differently for different datasets. In the best-accuracy

However, other datasets like TLS (“Andor” and “CCD3”) or TRES reveal a considerably different power excess with a much larger amplitude and more narrow frequency localization. Since different datasets reveal clearly different noise spectrum, their underlying physics is unlikely to be the same. While the correlated noise in HARPSN may indicate some intrinsic stellar activity indeed, we believe that other datasets with correlated noise may reveal some long-term instrumental effects rather than stellar activity. 4 SELF-CONSISTENT DATA ANALYSIS AND PLANETARY SYSTEM PARAMETERS 4.1 TTV pre-analysis 

The hypothesis of planet TOI-1408 c existence was largely based on the interpretation of planet b TTV signal. Therefore, before proceeding to two-planet models we ought to verify additionally that this TTV interpretation is not affected by our changes to the noise models or by adding new data. For this goal we considered only lightcurves containing planet b transits and performed two single-planet fits, namely assuming (i) null (no-TTV) hypothesis with strictly linear model for planet b transit times and (ii) alter-native hypothesis allowing all transit times to be fittable. The null fit appeared rather similar to the one discussed in (Galazutdinov et al. 2023). ∼ 450 corresponding to the chi-square test statistic of ∼ 900 , which is very large even though the num-ber of degrees of freedom was 108 (the same as the number of planet b transit lightcurves). 4.2 Three-planet model analysis 

Our final three-planet system model involved two inner planets b and c gravitationally interacting with each other while the companion d being gravitationally detached, moving on a distant circular orbit. The orbital configuration was assumed coplanar. Thanks to the gravitational perturbations in the inner subsystem the mass of the star can be estimated purely dynamically, so in the first treatment attempt we did not use any astrophysical estimations for it, as well as for the star radius. The limb darkening model, as well as noise models in the photometric and Doppler data, were described above. All the fitting job was done using P LANET PACK software (Baluev 2013c, 2018) implementing the likelihood maximization. We can confirm that orbital model given by Korth et al. (2024), or ones close to, can effectively explain the observed TTVs. However our results appear more pessimistic regarding the parametric un-certainties. We found that the likelihood function has numerous local maxima, basically demonstrating a “rippled” shape near its top. This is shown in Fig 7 that demonstrates so-called profile-likelihood curves for each of the 18 system parameters: planetary masses mb,c,d (true ones, not m sin i), orbital periods 

Pb,c,d , mean longitudes λb,c,d , orbital eccentricities eb,c , pericentra arguments ωb,c , planet radii rb,c ,common orbital inclination ib = ic, star radius R⋆ and mass M⋆. Each profile-likelihood curve shows the log of the likelihood ratio statistic ∆ log L as a function of a given parameter, i.e. the log-likelihood maximized with respect to all the remaining free parameters (fixing the selected one), and subtracted from the global maximum. In such a way all the maxima turn into minima inverting the graph. In case if there was only a single extremum, the parametric uncertainty for a given parameter could be determined in the classic way, that is by the abscissa range in which ∆ log L remains be-low some threshold determined by the asymptotic χ2 test. Namely, each nσ threshold would require 

∆ log L ≤ n2/2, e.g. those thresholds should be 0.5, 2.0, and 4.5 for the 1σ, 2σ, and 3σ significance levels, respectively. Because of multiple extrema this rule will need a correction which is considered below, but before that we will use such simplified thresholds as some rough reference levels. Different local extrema are best separated along the parameters rb, ib,c , and R⋆. Notice that these three parameters jointly define the planet b transit geometry, so this phenomenon is likely related to the near-tangential character of the transit. Simultaneously these parameters attain considerably larger uncertainties compared to Korth et al. (2024). To be more strict, it should be understood in the average sense, but it can also be estimated based on the actual number of local maxima observed in the likelihood of a particular task. In the analysis above we observed Nbranches ∼ 10 , but this number may need some correction. On the one hand, there is still a risk that we missed some extrema and thus undervalued it, but on the other hand the branches that we identified are unlikely to be all independent. Moreover, some of them are only partly separated from the others and some span rather narrow parametric ranges. Since our approach is already conservative by its design, in order to avoid being overconservative we adopted Nbranches ∼ 5. Then, for example, to achieve the post-correction 

α probabilities of 68% , matching the 1σ level, one should require α1 to be such that it maps to the (uncorrected) 1.8σ level. Notice that effective Nbranches is a somewhat subjective choice here; this particular value of 5

was partly motivated by the observation that for the parameters rb, ib,c , and R⋆ most of the branches are grouped into close pairs, so the number of independent branches should be about half of the total number. 4.4 Discussion of subtle effects that were neglected 

4.4.1 Orbital non-coplanarity 

In the above analysis we considered only coplanar models of the system. Non-coplanar configurations were considered by Korth et al. (2024) by allowing different inclinations ib and ic. However, their in-clination estimations appeared the same within the uncertainties. Simultaneously, their model assumed strictly coinciding orbital nodes which means that it was not entirely noncoplanar. We tried to fit entirely noncoplanar configurations with an additional free angle for the inter-orbital ascending node. This angle appeared rather robustly fittable, and it always was consistent with zero (below 1◦), as well as the mu-tual inclination between planets b and c orbits. However, the issue of multiple likelihood extrema was significantly exacerbated, requiring a larger amount of numeric computations. Because of that we did not consider non-coplanar configurations in this study in detail. Currently we did not detect any hints of an interorbital inclination, but this issue perhaps needs additional investigation in future. 

4.4.2 Rømer delay because of the outermost body 

A distant orbiting body may cause a time delay of the observed light arrival because of a combination of a finite light speed with the star motion about the system barycenter. This effect was initially observed in the Solar System and is often named after Ole Rømer. Such an effect may be important in some extrasolar systems where it can cause an apparent TTV (Harre & Smith 2023). In case of TOI-1408 this effect may come from the outermost body d.Notice that to predict the time delay variation it is unnecessary to accurately know the outer body orbit and mass. One can just use the observed non-linear Doppler trend which is constrained quite well. The Rømer effect can be determined by integrating this Doppler trend, which yields the star radial coordinate, and then dividing this integral by the speed of light. The resulting variation is shown in Fig 11. Notice that this variation has a free offset (the integra-tion constant) tied to the total time the light needs to reach the observer. Also, this variation involves a freely variable linear trend because of a relative nature of Doppler data. The latter effect depends on the absolute radial velocity and simply scales all the orbital periods up by the same near-unit coefficient (or, alternatively, scales the time unit). In practice such a correction is rarely applied, even if it exceeds the periods uncertainties. Both these linear effects are not important in our study, so to keep only the essential non-linear part of the time delay variation we subtracted the best fitting linear trend from it. In Fig 11 one can see that after such a subtraction the effect remained below 0.5 min over the entire observation range. This is well below the typical uncertainty of a single TESS transit timing ( & 2 min) and should not affect our analysis. Nevertheless, to additionally verify that we applied our time correc-tion from Fig 11 to all input data and rerun all the fits. It appeared that all the parameters shifted only negligibly, by a quantity no more than ∼ 0.1 times the respective parameter uncertainty (even before 20 R. V. Baluev et al.

4.4.3 Rossiter-McLaughlin effect 

Korth et al. (2024) and Galazutdinov et al. (2026) both gave v sin i ∼ 10 km/s based on the spec-trum models, and considering this value together with the derived planet b transit depth and the limb-darkening near the stellar limb, the expected maximum amplitude of the Rossiter-McLaughlin (RM) effect should be ∼ 10 m/s. This is comparable to the uncertainty of a single HARPSN measurement (summing up all its modelled noises). Therefore, the effect would perhaps be detectable had it that large magnitude. However, given the near-grazing transit geometry, the maximum magnitude is reached only for specific misalignment angle λ = 90 ◦. If the planet b orbit and stellar equator are aligned then the planet would transit only the near-pole region of the star, resulting in a negligibly small RM effect. Regarding the planet c, its RM effect should be ∼ 1 m/s at most, which is negligible regardless of the orbital alignment. Our attempt to estimate the RM effect from the Doppler data (including both planets) implied, formally, v sin i ∼ 15 km/s, with the uncertainty comparable to the value, therefore this effect did not appear reliably detectable. Contrary to that, the formal estimation for the alignment angle λ appeared apparently informative, namely 0◦ ± 7◦ (rather independent on the likelihood extremum being chosen). This might be a null-detection constraint actually, that is λ is estimated to be small because otherwise the RM effect magnitude would be large which is not the case. Nevertheless the planet b orbit appears more likely to be aligned with the star equator, however this requires an additional detailed analysis of several accompanying issues (e.g. of the “spectral” limb darkening law). Since the RM effect was not a primary goal of our study, we did not investigate this further. However, we ensured that adding or removing it to our models does not significantly change the best fitting parameters. The maximum shift On the TOI-1408 exoplanetary system 21 

appeared just ∼ 0.05 times the corresponding parameter uncertainty (even before their correction for multiple extrema). This included the effect from both transiting planets, TOI-1408 b and c.

4.4.4 Uncertainties of the limb darkening law 

Finally, we considered the effect of the LDL uncertainties on the fit parameters. We perturbed each of quadratic LDL coefficients by ±1σ, assuming the level-1 uncertainties from Table 1. In this case the maximum relative shift of the best fitting parameters was only 0.06 times the given parameter uncertainty (again before their correction for multiple extrema). This is entirely negligible. Though the LDL has additional systematic errors which are more difficult to characterize accurately, it does not seem likely that their effect may grow so large to become significant for our analysis. 

5 SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION 

In this work we performed a detailed reassessment of the TOI-1408 planetary system architecture, in-cluding additional new TESS data as well as involving a more detailed analysis of the limb darkening, photometric and Doppler noises, and the structure of the derived likelihood function. We generally con-firm the three-body system interpretation by Korth et al. (2024), with two inner planets being at least close to the 2:1 mean-motion resonance and causing TTV effects on each other. However we derived considerably larger parametric uncertainties, in particular the planet b radius still remains ill-determined according to our treatment. The reason for such difference with Korth et al. (2024), as we see it, is prob-ably because they adopted rather narrowly localized priors for several parameters. Not only the star mass and radius were assumed to have a narrow prior, but a few other parameters as well, and the motivation for that is not always obvious. Notice that for a two-planet dynamics to be termed resonant it is usually assumed enough if at least one critical angle librates (Malhotra 2012). For all our local likelihood maxima we verified that this remains nearly the same as in Korth et al. (2024), that is one critical angle (for the planet c) librates with the period ∼ 138 d, while the other one (for the planet b) circulates with the period of ∼ 295 d. Clearly, the primary 138 d TTV period seen in Fig 6 for both the planets is owed to the libration, while the circulation of the second angle is not seen in TTV but it results in a drift of planetary transit phases relative to each other (so sometimes two transits overlap, sometimes not). However Korth et al. (2024) performed a more detailed dynamical analysis and did not find the resonance separatrix in the Hamiltonian, implying that the motion is not resonant in a more strict dynamical sense. In the context of this work this difference between two definitions of a resonance is not very important, as considering the dynamics of TOI-1408 in detail was not among our goals. Moreover, to certainly resolve the issue of whether the motion is resonant or not, it is necessary to consider the effect of parametric uncertainties on the system dynamics. This question was not consid-ered here, as well as by Korth et al. (2024), at least not comprehensively. Nevertheless, any reasonable orbital solution has to keep the robustly observable TTV signal from Fig 6. It is possible, in principle, that entirely non-resonant configurations (with both critical angles circulating) exist, simultaneously ex-plaining the observed TTV. However, such a qualitative transition from librating dynamical regime to a circulation-only one would not appear smooth enough in terms of the likelihood function. Possible cir-culating solutions, if they exist, would necessarily appear as separate likelihood modes, but modes that we found all correspond to librating solutions. Hence we remain sceptical regarding the possibility that this TTV signal could be reproduced without preserving the libration dynamics of the same qualitative type. However, the effect of parametric uncertainty on the existence of a resonance separatrix is less obvious. The separatrix may, in theory, appear if we step some away from the nominal solution, and simultaneously the goodness of the fit may be possibly kept reasonable as long as such a change does not trigger abrupt change of the dynamical regime on the observation time range. The parameters of the third body TOI-1408 d that manifests itself through a nonlinear Doppler trend still cannot be constrained well even in the simplified approximation of a circular orbit. Its orbital period appears indistinguishable from the infinity, as well as this object mass. However, lower limits can be placed on these parameters that cast doubts on its planetary nature. Nevertheless it is still possible that either (i) the system contains multiple long-period objects responsible for the observed cumulative Doppler trend, and in such a case some or even all of them still can have planetary masses, or (ii) the companion’s orbit may be eccentric which violates our initial assumption. By admitting nonzero ed the lower limit on md may reduce, however such a model is difficult to process in our task because it would have a high level of degeneracy. Regarding the astrophysical star mass and radius estimations for TOI-1408, we believe their uncer-tainties given by Korth et al. (2024) may be overly optimistic because they did not consider systematic errors owed to differences between stellar models (their systematic errors). In this work we used the star mass and radius estimations from Galazutdinov et al. (2026) who considered multiple stellar models and obtained significantly larger uncertainties for M⋆, R⋆. However, another issue appeared here because the prior distribution for M⋆, R⋆ has to be characterized in considerable detail to be useful with TOI-1408. For example the planetary model depends significantly on the correlation between M⋆ and R⋆, which is usually not provided. Here the underlying issue is because the analysis involves a set of nonlinear models chained to each other sequentially, namely raw spectra are first fitted to obtain Teff , log g, and [Fe/H], then these stellar parameters are used as input data for stellar evolution models to obtain M⋆

and R⋆, and finally these M⋆ and R⋆ are used as input data for our three-planet model fit. However, in each link of this chain one has to make simplifying assumptions regarding all the intermediary parame-ters, in particular their correlations are omitted and their distribution is assumed to be Gaussian. In the case of TOI-1408 a better and more reliable way would probably be to perform a self-consistent fit of a combined model involving all the underlying models simultaneously, namely the transit and Doppler models, as well as stellar evolution models and raw spectrum models, thus using the raw spectra as input data. In this way one would avoid intermediary estimations, as well as any assumptions about them. We also performed a detailed investigation of the TOI-1408 limb darkening and constructed a cor-rected quadratic model tuned to fit better the near-limb zone, which is transited by the planet b. We provided these models for a set of 18 passbands, including the TESS one as well as classic photometric systems typically used in ground-based observations. Based on this investigation one may argue that possible remaining LDL errors are likely negligible in the context of modelling TOI-1408 exoplanetary transit curves. Regarding the noises seen in Doppler data, we did not reveal clear hints of stellar rotation period in them. It seems that in the most cases they are more likely to have an instrumental nature, while for the best-accuracy HARPSN dataset there is only very modest correlated noise with no convincing signs of rotational modulation.
