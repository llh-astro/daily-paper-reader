ABSTRACT How planetesimals form is a major open question in studies of planet formation, as the growth of smaller solids must overcome barriers such as radial drift, fragmentation, and bouncing. The lead-ing model is that planetesimals form by the gravitational collapse of dense particle clumps that have been aerodynamically collected via a process known as the streaming instability (SI). While the SI has passed several observational tests, a critical test remains: a successful comparison with the size distribution of Solar System planetesimals. In this work, we conducted a series of SI simulations for a range of input parameters and combine a more careful accounting of initial planetesimal mass with more advanced statistical techniques compared to previous studies in order to compare the simulation outcomes against observations of the most primordial population in the solar system: the Cold Clas-sical Kuiper Belt (CCKB). Using both cumulative distribution function (CDF) model parameters and distribution comparison methods like the Kolomogorov-Smirnov test, we show that, when planetesimal masses are measured at formation and analyzed with modern statistical methods, the SI-predicted size distributions can quantitatively reproduce the high-mass end of the observed CCKB size distribution, though the extent of the agreement depends on the physical parameters chosen, providing further support for the SI theory of planetesimal formation. 

1. INTRODUCTION Planet formation is a process that spans more than 13 orders of magnitude in spatial scale. A crucial step along the way from ≲ μm grains to ∼ 10 4-10 5 km planets is the formation of planetesimals, objects roughly 1-100 km in size that are held together with their own gravity. The formation of planetesimals faces several barriers, including radial drift, where orbiting particles lose an-gular momentum due to gas drag, causing them to move inward on potentially very short timescales (S. J. Wei-denschilling 1977a), as well as collisional barriers such as bouncing and fragmentation of larger solids into smaller grains (J. Blum & G. Wurm 2008; A. Zsom et al. 2010; E. Chiang & A. N. Youdin 2010; A. Johansen et al. 2014; J. Blum 2018). Over the past two decades, the streaming instability (SI) has emerged as a promising candidate to overcome these barriers. The SI arises from solid particles and gas in protoplanetary disks exchanging angular momentum through aerodynamic drag (A. N. Youdin & J. Good-man 2005; A. Youdin & A. Johansen 2007); this results in the formation of dense filamentary structures of solid particles (A. Johansen & A. Youdin 2007; A. Johansen et al. 2009; X.-N. Bai & J. M. Stone 2010a; C.-C. Yang & A. Johansen 2014; C. P. Abod et al. 2019). Within these filaments, particle clumps that have local overden-sities exceeding the Roche density gravitationally col-lapse to produce bodies at the ∼ 100 km scale (see e.g. A. Johansen et al. 2015; J. B. Simon et al. 2016), which roughly agrees with many of the largest planetesimals in the Solar System. Adding to this preliminary evidence favoring the SI model, detailed studies of individual gravitationally bound clumps produced by the SI demonstrate that this mechanism favors the formation of binary systems with angular momentum distributions strongly consis-tent with those observed in the Cold Classical Kuiper Belt (CCKB), including the predominance of prograde rotation and a retrograde fraction of roughly 20% (D. Nesvorn´ y et al. 2019, 2021). However, more recent work (R. G. Visser & M. G. Brouwers 2022) has demonstrated that the preference for prograde binaries may simply be the result of gravitational collapse of a pebble cloud orbiting in a Keplerian potential, though they did not arXiv:2610.04114v1 [astro-ph.EP] 2 Oct 2026 2show that the 80%/20% split of prograde/retrograde is an outcome of this collapse. Thus, while the properties of KBO binaries may provide strong support of the SI model, the remaining uncertainties motivate us to find alternative tests of this mechanism. One such additional observational test is a comparison between the observed and predicted initial size distribu-tions of planetesimals in the Solar System. While the SI seems to favor planetesimals being born big, in agree-ment with the size distribution of asteroids in our solar system (A. Morbidelli et al. 2009), more work needs to be done to verify that the size distributions of planetes-imal populations is in agreement with SI models. In particular, more robust statistical comparisons between observed and simulated planetesimal populations need to be carried out over a range of planetesimal size scales. In order to make progress along this front, several pre-vious studies have focused on quantifying the planetes-imal initial mass function (IMF). They found relatively top-heavy distributions with a large fraction of the mass contained in the largest objects (A. Johansen et al. 2015; J. B. Simon et al. 2016; U. Sch¨ afer et al. 2017a). J. B. Simon et al. (2017) further suggested that the shape (in that work, they fit a simple power law to their IMFs and compared the power law indicies) of the IMF was largely insensitive to particle stopping time, interpreting this as evidence for a degree of universality in SI-driven collapse; however subsequent studies expanded both the accessible mass range and the explored parameter space, enabled in part by improved clump-identification algo-rithms, demonstrating that the IMF can vary in both functional form and best-fit parameters (R. Li et al. 2019). Despite the progress made by this early work, however, agreement between observed and predicted size distributions has remained elusive (see the review by J. B. Simon et al. 2024, as well as discussion in G. M. Bernstein et al. 2026). On the observational front, the magnitude (and thus size) distribution of the Cold Classical Kuiper Belt objects (CCKBOs) has been studied extensively (e.g., W. C. Fraser et al. 2014). The CCKBOs are the most pristine population of objects observationally accessible, having undergone little to no collisional evolution (but see A. H. Parker & J. J. Kavelaars 2012; A. Brunini & M. Zanardi 2016 for a discussion of the collisional evolution of 10-30% of CCKBOs that may have occurred), and containing loosely bound binary pairs implying in situ 

formation (A. H. Parker & J. J. Kavelaars 2010; but see H. M. Campbell et al. 2025). At low-magnitudes (large sizes), the cumulative size distribution has an exponen-tial taper (J. J. Kavelaars et al. 2021), and dimmer ob-jects have a size distribution described by a power law (K. J. Napier et al. 2024; M. R. Eduardo et al. 2026; G. M. Bernstein et al. 2026). Most recently, G. M. Bernstein et al. (2026) note that the observed exponential taper in size is much gen-tler than previous SI-driven IMF works suggest, fur-ther solidifying earlier concerns of disagreement be-tween observed and predicted size distributions (J. B. Simon et al. 2024). In this work, we attempt to rec-oncile these differences via several methodological im-provements. One noted issue in aforementioned SI-driven IMF works that these studies typically rely on snapshot-based identification of bound clumps, which can bias inferred masses due to processes such as merg-ers, tidal stripping, and continued accretion after for-mation. Therefore, in addition to varying key physical parameters and employing modern clump-identification methods, we crucially incorporate clump tracking to measure planetesimal masses at the time of formation (c.f. D. A. Gole et al. 2020). We then employ the use of a robust statistical framework to enable direct and rig-orous comparisons between predicted and observed size distributions. We find that, depending on the values of the key physical parameters that control the outcome of the SI, our simulations can quantitatively reproduce the high-mass end of the observed CCKB distribution. This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe our numerical algorithm in detail, including ini-tial conditions and parameter choices, how to identify planetesimals and construct a predicted mass (and thus size) distribution, the disk model to convert from code to physical units, and the statistical tools we use to char-acterize the distribution. We present our results for the distribution characteristics and observational compar-isons in Section 3. In Section 4, we discuss potential im-pacts of our assumptions on the predicted distribution, along with the associated uncertainties. We summarize and conclude in Section 5.

The SI clumps the parti-cles until filament structures of solids are formed, after which particle self-gravity is turned on. We use the Athena (J. M. Stone et al. 2008) code to simulate a small, vertically stratified section of a pro-toplanetary disk within the local shearing box approxi-mation (J. F. Hawley et al. 1995; J. M. Stone & T. A. Gardiner 2010). For algorithmic choices in Athena , we employ the corner transport upwind integrator (CTU; P. Colella 1990), the piecewise parabolic method for third order spatial reconstruction (PPM; P. Colella & P. R. Woodward 1984), and the Harten-Lax-van Leer with contact Reimann solver for flux quantities (HLLC; E. F. Toro 1997). The shearing box approximation is justified by the small size of the box compared to the large distance from the star, and maps the (cylindrical) disk coordi-nates ( R, ϕ, z ′) onto a local Cartesian system ( x, y, z )(P. Goldreich & D. Lynden-Bell 1965). The local box is centered at a radius R0 from the central star in the midplane where: x = R − R0, y = R0ϕ, z = z′ and the Keplerian frequency and velocity are Ω and vK = Ω R0,respectively. We employ orbital advection, in which the background Keplerian shear flow is analytically sepa-rated from the dynamical velocity field (J. M. Stone & T. A. Gardiner 2010). In this approach, the total ve-locity is decomposed into a prescribed linear shear and a residual velocity (see below), the latter of which is evolved by the hydrodynamic solver. The background shear is advanced as a separate advection step, allow-ing for larger timesteps and reduced numerical diffusion while preserving the correct epicyclic dynamics of the shearing box. To identify gravitationally bound clumps inside our domain, we employ the use of the PLanetesimal ANa-lyzer code (PLAN; R. Li et al. 2019). PLAN uses the same approach as HOP (D. J. Eisenstein & P. Hut 1998), a code that identifies dark matter halos in cosmologi-cal simulations, but applied to pebble clouds instead. First, simulation particles are stored in a data struc-ture based on a Barnes-Hut tree (J. Barnes & P. Hut 1986). This allows for fast searches of a given particle’s neighbors. Then, particles in dense regions are identified using the HOP algorithm, which is capable of grouping together physically related particles. For the fiducial resolution (see below), a grid cell is ∼ 4×10 −4H, where H is the vertical gas scale height, H = cs/Ω; at the CCKB radius of 45 AU, this scale corresponds to a grid cell size of approx-imately 10 5 km, orders of magnitude larger than even the largest KBOs. Thus, clumps in our simulations will have an enhanced size compared to the actual scales of planetesimals, and we do not expect our simulations to correctly capture the post-formation evolution of plan-etesimals (e.g., mergers between planetesimals will not, in general, be accurately treated). Therefore, we follow D. A. Gole et al. (2020) and em-ploy a clump tracking approach ( ClumpTracker ) to counter these effects. In short, the algorithm tracks clumps from one frame to the next and creates a mass history for each clump. From this data, we can then look at every clump that ever existed in the simulation and measure its mass at the time of formation. Ulti-mately, measuring initial masses with this method is able to solve three problems stemming from the arti-ficially large size of simulated clumps: 

• Accretion: After formation, clumps will likely ac-crete particles at an artificially enhanced rate due to the enhanced collisional cross section. Examin-ing only the initial masses ignores this accretion. 

• Mergers: Similarly, clumps will merge at an en-hanced rate. If two clumps merge, a snapshot would count them as one large clump. With this tracking method, we measure the initial formation mass of the two separate clumps. 

• Splits and Tidal Stripping: If clumps are able to fully collapse before interacting with other clumps, the rate of clumps being tidally stripped and split into multiple clumps should be quite low. How-ever, this occurs quite often with the artificially large clumps in these simulations, which are less compact and thus more easily tidally stripped. The algorithm is able to ignore this effect by mea-suring only the initial mass of the parent clump. Note that this is a separate approach than employ-ing sink particles as in e.g., U. Sch¨ afer et al. (2024). However, U. Sch¨ afer et al. (2024) showed using a clump tracker produces approximately the same IMFs as pro-duced in simulations using sink particles. One caveat of the clump tracking algorithm (and the sink particle algorithm) is that it is unable to capture the sub-grid collapse dynamics of the pebble clumps. It is known already that clumps formed via the SI are most likely to form binary (or smaller many-body) ob-jects (D. Nesvorn´ y et al. 2021). We are unable to resolve this collapse, and there is no known method of calculat-ing the final number of bodies from clump properties alone. As a workaround, we implement a toy model to approximately model this sub-grid collapse using the results from D. Nesvorn´ y et al. (2021) (described in Sec-tion 4.5). We find that it does not lead to drastic changes in the size distribution. Therefore, for the purposes of this paper, we assume that each pebble cloud collapses into one single planetesimal; we refer to this as mono-lithic collapse. 52.3. Parameters, Initial Conditions, and Numerical Setup 

In this work, we isolate the impact of each physical parameter ( τ , Z, Π, ˜G) by varying them individually while holding the others fixed. We chose these param-eters because of their identified significance in previous IMF studies (J. B. Simon et al. 2017; C. P. Abod et al. 2019; R. Li et al. 2019). In addition to physical param-eters, several numerical factors are known to influence the resulting IMF, such as resolution and domain size (J. B. Simon et al. 2016; J. J. Rucska & J. W. Wad-sley 2021). The fiducial simulation uses a domain size slightly larger than in previous studies (J. B. Simon et al. 2016, 2017; C. P. Abod et al. 2019), with dimensions 

Lx × Ly × Lz = 0 .4H × 0.2H × 0.2H. The corresponding fiducial resolution is Nx×Ny ×Nz = 1024 ×512 ×512, and the total number of particles is fixed at Ntot = 2 .7×10 8.At initialization, this setup results in an average of one particle per grid cell. As the simulation progresses, par-ticles settle toward the midplane due to vertical gravity of the star, leading to an increased number of particles per cell around the midplane. In addition to simulation size/scale, the simulation du-ration and timing have the potential to affect the IMF. Each simulation was run to tend , with slight variations in the self-gravity activation time tSG . The extent to which differences in tend affect resulting IMFs will be addressed in Section 4.3.2. Importantly, while we decrease the resolution for two of our simulations to test the effect of changing the grid scale, Ntot is kept constant. Therefore, decreasing the resolution by a factor of two in each spatial direction re-sults in an average of eight particles per grid cell at ini-tialization. Previous studies have shown that, provided sufficient resolution is achieved, the onset and nonlinear evolution of the SI are not strongly sensitive to particle resolution (A. Johansen & A. Youdin 2007; X.-N. Bai & J. M. Stone 2010b; C.-C. Yang et al. 2017). A summary of the parameters used in each simulation is provided in Table 1, where the naming convention indicates the pa-rameter combinations used in the simulation (e.g. Pi05 means Π = 0 .05, and Z5 means Z = 0 .05, etc.). The particles are initially distributed uniformly in x

and y and with a Gaussian profile in z. They are ini-tialized with NSH velocities (Y. Nakagawa et al. 1986a) in x and y, and vertical velocities are initialized to zero. While we add random noise to the particle locations to seed the SI, it is worth mentioning that the particles are not initially in an equilibrium state in z, and experience a transient settling phase early in the simulation. 2.4. Typical MCMC chains consist of 24 walkers and 11,000 steps. We discard the first 1,000 steps as burn-in and assess convergence using the integrated autocorre-lation time, τ , of each model parameter. Since both J. J. Kavelaars et al. (2021) and K. J. Napier et al. (2024) identified the unique exponential tapered shape of the distribution of CCKBO magni-tudes/size, one of our primary goals is to examine if the SI is capable of reproducing this taper. Here we ap-ply normalization techniques to isolate the shape of the PSD; this eliminates the use of a disk model and the assumption of a number of parameters, such as albedo and material density of planetesimals. Subsequent com-parisons will reintroduce the physical scaling in order to test the simulations with fitted distribution parameters. The most widely used normalization is the z-score transformation, which standardizes a distribution by ex-pressing each value in terms of the number of standard deviations it lies from the mean. This is given by: 

zi = xi − ¯xσ (39) where zi is the standardized value for the i-th obser-vation, xi is the original value, ¯ x is the mean of the distribution, and σ is the standard deviation. Because the z-score transformation is a linear trans-formation, it preserves the relative spacing and ordering of the data. As a result, the overall shape of the distri-bution – such as its symmetry, skewness, and modality – remains unchanged. Further details of this normaliza-tion will be discussed in subsequent sections. The z-score removes the need for assumptions regard-ing a disk model; however, an additional challenge is the issue of completeness. Ideally, the simulations would generate a sufficient number of planetesimals to repre-sent the complete population expected in a primordial CCKB (i.e. the total numbers match). In practice, how-ever, it is not feasible for a single shearing-box simula-tion to achieve this, as it represents only a small patch of the disk for only a brief period in time. In addition, the smallest objects formed in the simulations, which are roughly the scale of ∆ x, are likely underrepresented. As a result, we remove them for a more robust com-parison (see Appendix A for full discussion). Since the number of objects in the PSD is less than the predicted number of objects at the same size in observation, it becomes necessary to sample the observational fits to match PSD numbers. Statistical analysis will be used to confirm the visual agreement in Figure 1 in Section 3.2.1, and finally a statistical comparison with the parametrized model de-scribed is presented in Section 3.2.2. 3.2.1. The mapping between PSDs and the observed CCKB size distribution is degenerate, as different combinations of albedo, ρmat , and disk parame-ters ( Md, Rc, q) can yield similar physical size scales. In this work, we adopt fiducial values of an albedo of 0.15 and a bulk density of 1 g cm −3, and consider disk mod-els spanning plausible solar nebula conditions (c.f. C. T. Lenz et al. 2020). For a given PSD, we determine the value of MH required to align the distribution with the observed N (> D ), and infer the corresponding disk pa-rameters ( Md, Rc, q; see Table A1 and Appendix B). The disk model is therefore used solely to provide a physical interpretation of the inferred mass scale. For all simulations, we perform this alignment by matching the 29th largest object in the PSD to the smallest object in the MPC sample, since the MPC sample contains 29 objects. It is possible to perform a one-sample K-S test between the aligned PSD and the observational distri-butions, but the K-S test statistic is strongly tied to the value chosen for MH . Because the absolute mass scal-ing MH is uncertain, we do not calculate any one-sample K-S test between PSD and observational distribution. Upon performing this alignment, a significant issue be-comes apparent. In every simulation, the smallest plan-etesimal, Dmin , is larger than any of the characteristic sizes Dexp inferred from the observations (corresponding to the observational parameter HB ; see Equation 42). Moreover, when a VTPL is fit to a PSD following Sec-12 tion 2.5, the imposed priors truncate the Dexp posterior at Dmin . Thus, for every simulation considered here, es-sentially the entire resolved planetesimal population lies in the taper-dominated regime of the size distribution, governed by β, rather than in the power-law regime gov-erned by α. Consequently, α is only weakly constrained by fits to the PSDs because very few, if any, simulated planetesimals lie below Dexp , where the power-law com-ponent is most relevant. More importantly, an α inferred from a PSD would characterize a fundamentally differ-ent size range from an α inferred from the observations, since Dmin ,sim > D exp ,obs .G. M. Bernstein et al. (2026) notes a related discrep-ancy between previous SI studies and their observational fits, finding that the CCKB size distribution favors sub-stantially smaller taper parameters ( β) than those in-ferred from earlier SI simulations. The limited size range sampled by the simulations may help explain this differ-ence. Because the simulated planetesimals lie predom-inantly in the taper-dominated regime, a full VTPL fit can yield a poorly constrained and artificially steep α.Since α and β jointly determine the shape of the VTPL, bias in the inferred α can in turn shift the fitted value of β, complicating previous direct comparisons between simulation and observational fits. This suggests that there may be more potential in allowing Dexp to be outside the bounds of the PSD, and constraining it, along with α, to observational values. This allows us to focus on β, which dominates the large-mass/size edge of the distribution and is better probed by our simulations. We can also use this as an exercise to see if the observationally inferred parameters create a better fit to the PSDs. Therefore, we constrain α and 

Dexp to the seven combinations of values determined by fits to observational data, performing seven fits to β

alone for each PSD. These fits are presented in Tables 3 and 4, and the parameter comparison is visualized in Figure 2. To test whether the VTPL where we only vary β is a reasonable fit, we can use the one-sample K-S test be-tween the PSDs and the one-parameter VTPL, and in-terpret the K-S test statistic DKS , representing the max-imum absolute separation between the empirical and model cumulative distribution functions, to quantify the distributional discrepancy. In general, fewer than 5 per-centage points in discrepancy ( DKS < 0.05) is consid-ered a “good fit”. The values for each PSD and fit are presented in parentheses for in Table 3. We see that using the α and Dexp from observations is not always favored by the simulations. Specifically, the Tau1 Pi05 Z5 G05 and Tau1 Pi1 Z5 G1 simulations never have a low distributional discrepancy, indicating they are not able to be described well by the observa-tional values of α and Dexp , regardless of β value. For those that have a low distributional discrepancy, we find agreement between the β values fit to the PSDs and ob-servational distributions (e.g. Tau1 Pi05 Z2 G1 ). There is an apparent trend where PSD β values agree with observations more often when the observationally constrained α values are lower. For instance, the largest 

α (K21-2) has the smallest number of PSDs that show reasonable agreement with observations, while the sig-nificantly lower observationally-fit α values (E26-1, E26-2, B26-1) have a broader agreement. (2026) can be estimated visually from their scatter plot for fitted β vs α, this shows that the β values fit to the PSDs do generally fall within the range from B26-1 (see Figure 2). Altogether, this is sug-gesting when the observationally fit α values are lower, there is stronger agreement between the observed and predicted size distributions. In summary, it is clear that the PSDs for a number of runs are demonstrably similar to observation, espe-cially when fixing α and Dexp to the observationally constrained values. This agreement is supported by the K-S tests (Section 3.2.1) and the consistent agreement between the β values fit to observations and the PSDs. The SI is capable of producing the correct exponential taper in the CCKB size distribution.

Comparison to Previous IMF Studies 

An additional result from our work is that the IMF from SI simulations is not universal; we find that any physical parameter change (within the bounds of that which we probed) has noticeable effects on the result-ing IMF/PSD, so much so that statistical comparisons with the CCKB size distribution are affected. These re-sults reinforce the interpretation that the planetesimal IMF is set by the local disk environment—rather than representing a universal outcome of the instability. This finding agrees with R. Li et al. (2019) but in stands in contrast to earlier SI work focused on the IMF (J. B. Simon et al. 2016, 2017). However, these earlier studies used a different clump finding algorithm, which tended to limit the mass range of detected clumps. They also used individual snapshots to characterize the IMF instead of a clump-tracking algorithm as we have done here. We suspect that the main reason for the different conclusions results from the different clump finding al-gorithm since R. Li et al. (2019) also characterized the IMFs from individual snapshots. 4.3. Simulation Uncertainties 

4.3.1. Stochasticity 

A source of uncertainty in our results is the stochastic-ity associated with a given simulation and the resulting PSD. J. J. Rucska & J. W. Wadsley (2021) ran simu-lations with different seeding of the random perturba-tions to the system while fixing the parameter values; they demonstrated a degree of stochasticity in calculated 15 planetesimal IMFs (though these were not using a clump tracking algorithm). These simulations were performed at a resolution of 600 grid zones per H for varying grid domain sizes. Here, we carry out a test of the stochas-ticity of our PSDs by repeating a simulation with three different random seeds for particle location and velocity fluctuations (as described in Section 2), and comparing the resulting PSDs. The parameters chosen were those of the 

Tau1 Pi05 Z5 G1 runs. Figure 3 shows the PSDs cor-responding to each run. The final diameters contained in the PSDs for each seed differed by approximately 5% except in the case of the largest diameter (which differed by ∼ 25%). This uncertainty is smaller than the uncertainty on disk models, albedos, and observational debiasing which will be explored more in Sections 4.3.3 and Section 4.4. Additionally, a two-sample K-S test between each of the three random seeds yield p-values greater than 0.6 for all three combinations. Based on this experiment, we believe that the PSD shapes is ro-bust to small changes in the initial conditions, and it is appropriate to take as representative one simulation from each set of parameters. One way to reconcile this result with the results of J. J. Rucska & J. W. Wadsley (2021) is by considering the differences in the numerical resolution. Our fiducial resolution for these simulations is 2560 H−1, whereas J. J. Rucska & J. W. Wadsley (2021) employed a res-olution of 600 H−1. J. J. Rucska & J. W. Wadsley (2021) reports a spread in planetesimal numbers from 14 to 22 in their smallest domain. In order to esti-mate what the IMFs in the simulations in this study would look like had they been performed at 600 H−1,we can look at the number that had diameters larger than 2560 /600 × Dmin for the runs. This corresponds to a spread of 935 to 969 objects. We believe our numbers were much greater for three reasons. First, the phys-ical parameters of the Tau1 Pi05 Z5 G1 run are more conducive to planetesimal formation via the SI than those of J. J. Rucska & J. W. Wadsley (2021) (namely 

Z = 0 .05 vs Z = 0 .02). Second, the smallest domain size of J. J. Rucska & J. W. Wadsley (2021) is half the volume of the Tau1 Pi05 Z5 G1 run, which could eas-ily result in fewer planetesimals. Third, J. J. Rucska & J. W. Wadsley (2021) performs mass distribution anal-ysis at a single time snapshot in the simulation, poten-tially missing objects that formed and subsequently were destroyed/merged at earlier times; our clump-tracking algorithm accounts for this, as outlined in Section 2.2, potentially leading to a larger number of total planetes-imals formed. Thus, while it is still possible that stochasticity ulti-mately can affect the IMF, the increased planetesimal numbers and advanced clump tracking in our simula-tions compared with J. J. Rucska & J. W. Wadsley (2021) suggest that the impact of this effect is negli-gible. That said, it should be kept in mind that we only tested the effect of stochasticity for one combination of physical parameters, and extending this testing to other parameter combinations is left to future work. 4.3.2. Simulation Integration Time 

The length of time over which the simulation is run may also have an impact on the PSDs. While 

ClumpTracker quantifies the masses of planetesimals as soon as they form, as described above, these planetes-imals are not being removed from the simulation. As a result, there is no way to completely rule out the effects of already-formed planetesimals on the later stage evo-lution of the system. For example, these planetesimals, which are more like extended pebble clouds (as discussed above), might accrete solids from the surrounding envi-ronment at a higher rate than if they were fully collapsed objects. This increased accretion of pebbles will not be combined into the planetesimal mass in the IMF, but may rob the local environment of pebbles that would otherwise form additional planetesimals. In short, our PSDs may be not completely reflect the true PSD pro-duced by the SI. Given this limitation, a shorter simu-lation runtime should be prioritized. Conversely, not all shearing box simulations produce planetesimals at the same rate, even after the activa-tion of self-gravity. In order to better enable a robust comparison between observations and our predictions, our simulations must generate a sufficiently large pop-ulation of planetesimals (see Section 3.2.2). Therefore, we must consider both effects: prolonged evolution that may distort the true IMF and run times too short to enable a good comparison with observations. Thus, we must quantify the effect of the planetesimal formation time on the IMF/PSD. In order to do this, we run the ClumpTracker algorithm from the start of planetesimal formation to some end time; we then in-crease that end time and determine whether the PSD changes in a significant way. In Figure 4, we chart the inter-quartile range (IQR) of the Tau1 Pi05 Z5 G1 PSD over different ClumpTracker end times, with the ac-tual PSD distribution shown adjacent. If we were to perform a random selection of an unknown underlying distribution over time, the resulting sample should con-verge in appearance, represented by quartiles and a me-dian approaching a constant value as enough objects are sampled. 16

Disk Model 

As mentioned, the value of Dexp for a given PSD de-pends on the disk model used to convert code to physical units. There are a number of free parameters in our disk model that are not strongly constrained by observations, namely the values of Md, Rc, and q. For example, by al-lowing Md to be as low as 0 .01 M⊙ or as large as 0 .2M⊙,

Rc between 30 AU and 100 AU, and 0 .4 < q < 0.6, MH

can be as low as 9 .57 × 10 26 g or as high as 2 .42 × 10 28 g. That means that the largest clump formed in our fidu-cial simulation, at 7 .73 × 10 −6 code units, could range in diameter from 383 km to 1239 km. For reference, the brightest candidate in recent Cold Classical belt studies is Hr ≈ 3.6 (M. T. Bannister et al. 2018); at an albedo of 0.15, this corresponds to a diameter of 653 km. The unknown disk model parameters is the largest source of uncertainty on the theory side of this work. However, this uncertainty is not expected to affect the PSD shape since it leads to only an uncertainty in the mass scaling used to convert code units to physical units. The disk model choices for the parametrized analysis (Section 3.2.2, where the analysis depends on a disk model) are presented in Appendix B. There is a wide range of both disk masses ( Md) and characteristic radii ( Rc) that equate to an MH that aligns the PSDs with the MPC (see Section 3.2.2). To assess whether these required MH values can be pro-duced by plausible Solar System-like disk models, we evaluate two representative parameter combinations for each PSD: a relatively massive disk with Md = 0 .1 M⊙,from which we solve for Rc, and a Solar System-like char-acteristic radius of Rc = 45 AU, from which we solve for 

Md. We consider a PSD consistent with Solar System-like disk parameters if the Rc = 45 AU model requires a disk mass above that of the MMSN ( ≳ 0.013 M⊙,S. J. Weidenschilling 1977b; C. Hayashi 1981). Three of six PSDs meet this criterion and can therefore be re-produced with Solar System-like disk parameters. En-couragingly, these are Tau01 Pi05 Z5 G1 , which showed strong similarity to the CCKB distribution in Sec-tion 3.2.1, and Tau1 Pi05 Z5 G05 and Tau3 Pi05 Z2 G1 ,which showed strong similarity to all observational fits in Section 3.2.2. 4.4. Observational Uncertainties of CCKBOs 

The CCKB has been observed to exhibit geometric albedos spanning approximately 0.1–0.2, with reported mean values near ∼ 0.16 based on available samples (W. C. Fraser et al. 2014; P. Lacerda et al. 2014). How-ever, these estimates are derived from limited and poten-tially selection-biased samples, and the intrinsic albedo distribution remains uncertain. For this reason, it is standard practice to adopt a fiducial albedo of 0.15 when converting magnitudes to sizes. We follow this conven-tion throughout this work, noting that variations across the observed albedo range correspond to an uncertainty of order ∼ 20% in inferred diameters, while the differ-ence between assuming 0.15 and 0.16 is negligible com-pared to this intrinsic scatter. An albedo that varies with different CCKBOs would present a complication that would be challenging to in-clude in this study. Fortunately, the relationship be-tween albedo and size has been studied for different TNO object populations (E. Vilenius et al. 2012, 2018). Since CCKBOs have very minimal collisional evolution, and have similar surface characteristics as suggested by their spectra (T. Seccull et al. 2021), it is not expected that they possess a significant albedo distribution be-yond a uniform value, so the use of constant albedo is acceptable. Besides albedo, the bulk material density of CCK-BOs could range from 0.30 to 3.74 g /cm 3, correspond-ing to a diameter difference of ∼ 130% (this density consideration does not account for porosity or plan-etesimal shape). Should density depend on planetesi-mal mass, or have some other complicated relationship with size, this would also distort the PSD shape. The standard assumption is uniform density of CCKBOs at 

ρmat = 1 .0 g /cm 3. This choice is supported by the observed density of similar size objects (M. Tatsuuma et al. 2024), and while theory shows a spread in mate-rial densities ( ∼ 0.5 − 1.5g /cm 3 for similar mass objects 

∼ 10 −3 − 10 −4 MPluto ; M. H. Ca˜ nas et al. 2024), the spread is small. Thus, for simplicity we assume constant density; this should be kept in mind when interpreting results. 4.5. Binary Formation Effects

CONCLUSIONS In this work, we simulated the planetesimal forma-tion process via the SI and calculated IMFs and size distributions for different parameters. These predicted size distributions were compared to the observational size distributions of Cold-Classical KBOs. Overall, we found similarities between the simulated and observed size distributions. The main conclusions are as follows: 

• The SI is able to produce a statistically similar shape to the distribution of the largest objects in the CCKB (those within the exponential taper of the distribution) as evidenced by K-S test results. 

• The predicted size distributions produced by the SI contain only large planetesimals in the expo-nentially tapered region of the CCKB distribu-tion. Therefore the small-size end of the distri-bution (dominated by α) and the characteristic tapering size ( Dexp or HB ) of the CCKB are diffi-cult to probe when fitting. Instead, by holding α

and Dexp to observationally fit values and fitting 

β to the PSDs alone, the SI is able to reproduce the β value from observations of the CCKB. The agreement in β values is best for low α values: 

α ≲ 1.5, αM ≲ 0.5, αHr ≲ 0.3. 

• The predicted size distributions show stronger agreement with observations for τ ≤ 0.1, Z < 0.05 (favoring Z = 0 .02 in this study, though solar 

Z = 0 .01 was not tested), and Π < 0.1. 

• The size distributions of planetesimals are robust to small differences in initial conditions with the same physical parameters, when adequate resolu-tion is reached. 

• A toy model including the effect of sub-grid col-lapse and multiplicity on the size distribution sug-gests that while the IMF will be steepened by sub-grid collapse, the effect is sufficiently weak such that other uncertainties (e.g., observational) will dominate. Our results clearly demonstrate that, modulo uncer-tainties, the size-frequency distributions produced by the SI are in broad agreement with observations of the CCKB for the largest planetesimals. More specif-ically, for various parameter combinations we explored, we found agreement between our predictions and ob-servations quantified by at least one, and in some cases multiple, statistical tests. While there are cases in which observations and predictions disagree, our results show that the SI can reproduce the observed size distribu-tion of the CCKB within the range of parameters we have explored. Coupled with previous theoretical and numerical support for the SI from studies of binary col-lapse (D. Nesvorn´ y et al. 2021), our findings support the hypothesis that the SI is the dominant mechanism for forming the CCKB. Future work should compare the small planetesimal limits (i.e., D < 100 km for the disk model used here) of the predicted and observed CCKB distributions and should compare simulations to other planetesimal populations where possible. However, this work puts the SI on even firmer footing as a key process in outer Solar System planetesimal formation. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS We thank J.J.
