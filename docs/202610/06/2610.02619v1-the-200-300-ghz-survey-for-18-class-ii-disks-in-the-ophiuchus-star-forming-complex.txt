ABSTRACT We measured the 198–310 GHz flux densities of 18 Class II protoplanetary disks in the Ophiuchus star-forming complex using the Submillimeter Array (SMA). The ∼200–300 GHz spectral indices (α200 −300 ) of these disks are 2.3 +0 .2

> −0.5

. In this observed frequency range, emission properties of the Ophiuchus disks are very similar to the 48 spatially compact Class II disks in the Taurus-Auriga region observed in a previous SMA survey. Combining the Ophiuchus and Taurus-Auriga samples, the Pearson correlation coefficient between α200 −300 and the flux density at 250 GHz ( F250GHz ) is 0.27 (p = 0 .02). We do not find a convincing correlation between α200 −300 and the R95% radius of the disks. Our interpretation for the α200 −300 values being close to 2 in the spatially compact disks is that at ≳200 GHz frequencies, the continuum emission in these disks is dominated by optically thick dust thermal emission, independent of F250GHz and R95% . As a result, the (sub)millimeter luminosity and the apparent disk size are positively correlated, which has been found in numerous resolved emission surveys of Class II disks. In young clusters with ages of ∼1–2 Myrs, Class II disks with smaller radii or lower (sub)millimeter luminosities are not necessarily those in which the dust mass budget has been consumed or dispersed. 

Keywords: Circumstellar dust (236) — Protoplanetary disks (1300) — Pre-main sequence (1289) — Planet formation (1241) 

1. INTRODUCTION Some recent observational studies have indicated the 

∼100 μm maximum dust grain sizes ( amax ) in protoplan-etary disks (e.g., A. Kataoka et al. 2016; I. W. Stephens et al. 2017; H. B. Liu 2019; G. Guidi et al. 2022; Y.-C. Hu et al. 2025; H. B. Liu et al. 2026). Such modest 

amax values may indicate that the dust particles in the observed regions are fragile (e.g. H. Jiang et al. 2024) or are limited by the bouncing and/or inward migration barrier(s) of dust growth, and thus are hard to coagu-late to larger sizes (for a review see T. Birnstiel 2023). If this is indeed the case, disks may tend to preserve their initial dust mass budget instead of consuming most of it in planet formation. Constraining the overall dust mass

OBSERVATIONS 2.1. Sample selection 

We selected the sample from the 48 young stellar objects (YSOs) observed by S. M. Andrews & J. P. Williams (2007). We only included objects with flux densities greater than 45 mJy at ∼350 GHz frequency (∼850 μm wavelength). To save slewing time, we further excluded the four sources, RNO 90, Wa Oph 6, AS 209 and AS 205, which are separated from the geometry cen-ter of the other sample by >2◦. This yields a sample of 18 Class II disks. With an integration time of one hour, the selected objects can be detected at >10-σ signifi-cance in every 12 GHz intermediate frequency (IF) of the selected SMA correlator settings, thereby meeting our target sensitivity (more details are provided in Sec-tion 2.2). Table 1 summarizes the properties of the host protostars and the disk radii derived in the high angular resolution observations of dust continuum emission. 2.2. The SMA observations 

We conducted an SMA survey of the selected sam-ple (Table 1) between 2022 and 2023 (Table 2). The observations in 2022 were taken in the extended ar-ray configuration; the follow-up observations in 2023 were taken in the compact configuration. Concatenat-ing the extended and compact array configuration ob-servations yielded 0 .′′ 8–1 .′′ 2 synthesized beam full width at half maximum (FWHM) and ∼40 ′′ maximum recov-erable angular scales. The observational logistics, including integration time allocation, target grouping and slewing path optimiza-tion, and the interleaving of target-calibrator scans, were designed following the strategy of C.-Y. Chung et al. (2024). The 18 target sources were divided into three groups based on proximity (Table 1). Targets in a given group were sequentially observed during the 12-minute window between complex gain calibrator scans. This yielded a total calibration duty cycle of 15 minutes. By looping through these three target groups every 45 min-utes, we ensured that every target was visited at least 1–2 times in each track of observations. We observed Titan as the absolute flux calibrator and 3C279 or BL Lac as the passband calibrator at the beginning or end of each observation (Table 2). The observations were operated in dual-receiver mode, in which the local oscillator (LO) frequencies of the two receivers were tuned to 209 GHz and 238 GHz in the tracks of 230 GHz-3b and 230 GHz-2, and were tuned to 270 GHz and 302 GHz in the tracks of 270 GHz-2a and 270 GHz-1 (Table 2). The SMA Wideband Astro-nomical ROACH2 Machine (SWARM; R. A. Primiani et al. 2016) correlator simultaneously covered the ±4– 16 GHz intermediate frequency (IF) ranges in the upper and lower sidebands. The frequency coverages of the observations are summarized in Table 2.

The complex gain phase and ampli-tude solutions for each sideband of each receiver were also separately derived and applied. 3.2. Gain-phase self-calibration 

The self-calibration procedure includes (1) identify-ing target sources (hereafter the self-calibration tar-get sources) that are bright enough for deriving self-calibration solutions in each sideband of the observa-tions (Section 2.2) in short time intervals, (2) producing source models, and (3) solving and applying antenna-based, time-dependent corrections for gain phase. For (1) and (2), using the imaging procedure introduced in Section 3.3, we first produced the preliminary images from the calibrated 254–318 GHz data (Table 2) that were created in the standard calibration procedure (Sec-tion 3.1). We focused on the observations in this fre-quency range because they are rather sensitive to dust emission and have higher angular resolutions than the observations that were taken with the lower LO frequen-cies (Section 2). From the preliminary 254–318 GHz images, we selected the following self-calibration target sources: WSB 31, GSS 39, DoAr 25, GSS 26, WSB 60, VSSG 1, YLW 8, IRS 51, DoAr 44, and AS 206. We used the clean components obtained when we gener-ated the preliminary images as source models. Using the MIRIAD selfcal task (R. J. Sault et al. 1995), we derived the gain-phase self-calibration solutions in 5 minutes intervals. We visually inspected the solutions and flagged the few outliers. Afterwards, we employed the scipy UnivariateSpline routine (P. Virtanen et al. 2020) to interpolate the solutions in the time domain us-ing the cubic spline function ( k = 3), and then applied the interpolated solutions to all target sources. 3.3. Imaging 

We performed naturally weighted, multi-frequency synthesis ( mfs ) imaging using the MIRIAD software package (R. J. Sault et al. 1995). The observations taken in the compact and extended array configurations were jointly imaged. The two-dimensional continuum images were created with 600 pixels in each dimension. The size of a pixel is 0 .′′ 1. Our imaging procedure first created the dirty images by inversely Fourier transforming the calibrated SMA visibilities (Section 3.1, 3.2) using the MIRIAD invert task. From the dirty images, we iden-tified the peaks of the emission. We then deconvolved the dirty images using the MIRIAD clean task for 100 iterations, restricted in the 5 ′′ -wide box regions around the peaks and without setting cutoff levels. This yielded the preliminary residual images. We measured the root-mean-square (rms) noise levels from these preliminary residual images. To produce the final images, we decon-volved the dirty images again using the MIRIAD clean 

task, by setting the cutoff levels to 1.5 times the rms noises. The typically achieved synthesized beam full width at half maxima (FWHM) at the 209, 238, 270 and 302 GHz LO frequencies (Section 2.2) are 2 .′′ 1, 1 .′′ 7, 1.′′ 2 and 1 .′′ 0, respectively; . 3.4. Measuring flux densities

The MCMC samplings were implemented using the publicly available python package emcee (Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013a). The best-fit flux density ( Fν )is taken as the value at the maximum of the posterior probability distribution, while its uncertainty ( σFν ) is defined as one-half of the difference between the 16th and 84th percentiles of the posterior distribution. We as-sumed a nominal absolute flux uncertainty (∆) of 5.5%, which was realistically determined in C.-Y. Chung et al. (2026) based on comparing multiple epochs of obser-vations. The standard error of the flux density ( δFν )is p(σFν )2 + ( Fν × ∆) 2. The results of our flux density measurements are summarized in Appendix B. Some de-tails are given as follows. 

DoAr 24Ea and DoAr 24Eb —These two binary compo-nents were both significantly detected in our SMA im-ages (Appendix A). With a 2 ′′ angular separation, the images of them are confused in our SMA observations at 

≲230 GHz, which makes the Gaussian fitting uncertain. Since the radii of these two binary components are ≲0.′′ 1(Table 1), which cannot be resolved by our SMA obser-vations (Section 3.3), we treated these two binary com-ponents as distinct point sources. To derive their flux densities, we simultaneously fit two point sources to the observations on them at all frequencies. The free param-eters include the flux densities (flux), x-offsets (dx), and y-offsets (dy) of the two point source models. The prior function of flux is uniform within the range of 0–2 Jy and is zero elsewhere. The prior functions of dx and dy are Gaussian functions with standard deviations equal to the synthesized beam FWHM, which are centered at the pixels of the source centers that we determined from the self-calibrated 254–318 GHz image (Section 3.3). 

IRS 37, IRS 39, and IRS 41 —The source IRS 39 was not detected in the image domain at most of the observing frequencies (Table 2) and thus was not considered in our visibility fittings. The 1 .′′ 92 separated companion of IRS 37 (Table 1) was not detected in our SMA observa-tions. Therefore, we treated IRS 37 as an isolated single source. The primary beams of our SMA observations on IRS 37, IRS 39, and IRS 41 overlapped. The pri-mary beam FWHMs and the degrees of overlap depend on the observing frequency. For each of the two sources IRS 37 and IRS 41, we jointly fit the observations that covered the sources within the primary beam FWHMs with circular Gaussian models. For example, when the observations at a certain frequency (e.g., 199 GHz) cov-ered both IRS 37 and IRS 41 in two overlapping pri-mary beams, we simultaneously fit the observations of these two antenna pointings with two circular Gaussian models. The free parameters of each circular Gaussian model include flux density (flux), x-offset (dx), y-offset (dy), and full width at half maximum (FWHM). When evaluating the likelihood function (i.e., when comparing models with the observed complex visibilities), the val-ues of flux were implemented with the primary beam attenuation at the location of the intensity peaks. The prior functions for flux, dx, and dy were similar to those for the point source models we used in the fittings for DoAr 24Ea and Eb. The prior function for FWHM was wide but not flat, which is a Gaussian function centered at 0 .′′ 24 with a standard deviation equal to 4 .′′ 7. In ad-dition, we rejected the MCMC samplers that walked to FHWM <0′′ .

Other sources in Table 1 —Our SMA observations did not detect the companions of DoAr 16, YLW 8, and IRS 51 (Table 1). The 0 .′′ 43 separated binary components of HBC 266 (Table 1) cannot be separated in our SMA ob-servations. Thus, we treated DoAr 16, YLW 8, IRS 51, HBC 266, and the other sources in Table 1 (except for DoAr 24Ea, DoAr 24Eb, IRS 37, IRS 39, and IRS 41) as isolated single sources. For each of these isolated sin-gle sources, to derive flux densities, we fit the visibilities with a circular Gaussian model. DISCUSSION AND SUMMARY We performed SMA observations on 18 selected Class II disks in the Ophiuchus molecular cloud. The flux densities of our selected sources are in the range of 10– 500 mJy at the frequency of 250 GHz. Our observations yield 8 independent measurements of flux densities over the frequency range of 199–310 GHz. Based on these measurements, the derived spectral indices α200 −300 are 2.3 +0 .2 

> −0.5

(Figure 3). We estimated lower limits on the dust masses us-ing uniform-slab dust emission models that incorporate both dust absorption and scattering. We adopted the standard DSHARP dust opacities (T. Birnstiel et al. 2018). Owing to the known degeneracy between dust grain size (or the dust opacity spectral index) and dust temperature, we followed the approach of C.-Y. Chung et al. (2024) and considered two representative combi-nations of dust temperature and maximum grain size, (Tdust , a max ) =(24 K, 3 mm) and (100 K, 0.05 mm). For each case, we generated model flux densities at our eight observing frequencies (Table 2) while varying the dust surface density, Σ dust , from 0.1 to 5.0 g cm −2. We measured the model spectral index, α200 −300 , by fit-ting a power law to the synthetic flux densities. For each source, we identified the minimum surface den-sity, Σ dust ,min , at which the model spectral index be-came lower than the observed value. If this condition was not reached within the explored range, we adopted Σdust ,min = 5 .0 g cm −2, in which case the inferred sur-face density represents a lower limit. Holding Σ dust 

fixed at Σ dust ,min , we then varied the solid angle of the emitting region until the model 250 GHz flux den-sity matched the observed F250 GHz . The corresponding solid angle, Ω min , was combined with the source distance to estimate the dust mass as Mdust = Σ dust ,min Ωmin d2.We performed this procedure independently for the two (Tdust , a max ) combinations to bracket the dust masses inferred for these representative dust properties. Figure 5 summarizes the results of our dust mass es-timates. We exclude IRS 37 from the estimates because its exceptionally low spectral index (Figure 1) indicates a significant contribution from free–free emission. We 10 emphasize that both Σ dust ,min and Mdust inferred with this method should be regarded as lower limits. This is because the disk-averaged α200 −300 reflects a mix-ture of optically thick and optically thin dust emission. The continuum emission does not directly constrain the amount of dust hidden in optically thick regions, and therefore potentially some amounts of dust mass can re-main undetected. For several spatially extended disks, such as GSS 26, DoAr 25, GSS 39, and WSB 31, a mean dust tempera-ture of ∼24 K may be more realistic than 100 K (Fig-ure 5). Even under this assumption, the estimated lower limits on the dust masses are ≳ 100 M⊕ for a substan-tial fraction of our observed sources (Figure 5). These sources may preserve their a significant fraction of the primordial dust mass budget, which is not yet dispersed (e.g., due to inward migration or feedback) or being con-sumed in planet formation. For other sources, the esti-mated lower limits on the dust masses lie in the range of 

≳ 10–100 M⊕ (Figure 5). If these lower limits are not substantially below their actual dust masses, a fraction of their primordial dust mass may already have been dis-persed or incorporated into larger bodies during planet formation, as has been discussed in previous studies of younger sources (e.g., L. Tychoniec et al. 2020). How-ever, because our estimates provide only lower limits, we cannot exclude the possibility that all of the millimeter-luminous ( F250 GHz > 10 mJy when scaled to a distance of 140 pc), ∼1–2 Myr-old Class II disks in our sample have largely preserved their primordial dust mass bud-gets. Finally, given the current measurement uncertainties of α200 −300 , which were largely limited by thermal noise in the SMA observations, the data did not convincingly reveal the correlations between α200 −300 and the R95% 

disk radius (Figures 3, 4). The α200 −300 and the 250 GHz flux density ( F250GHz ) may be positively correlated, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.27 ( p = 0 .02). In other words, in the F250GHz range of the Ophiuchus and Taurus-Auriga disks we compared, there is no evi-dence that the fainter or smaller disks are optically thin-ner (e.g., due to loss of dust mass).
