# Comparison of neural and spline representations for physics-informed learning 

Régis Duvigneau ∗

Université Côte d’Azur, Inria, CNRS, LJAD 

Abstract 

Physics-informed machine learning as recently emerged as a new paradigm for the res-olution of partial differential equations, including functionalities for parametric modeling and inverse design, using neural networks to represent the solution fields. However, several difficulties have been reported concerning the training of such networks, resulting in slow convergence, low accurate solutions and sensitivity to the choice of hyper-parameters. In order to better understand the mechanisms underlying these difficulties, the present work proposes a rigourous comparison of neural networks and B-Splines representations within the same physics-informed learning framework. Four problems with closed-form solutions are numerically studied to establish a rigorous analysis of the accuracy and efficiency of the two representations, in terms of asymptotic convergence rates, sampling requirements, loss function minimization and arithmetic precision needed. 

# 1 Introduction 

The current context of energy transition makes the use of multi-physics simulation and design methodologies critical across all industrial sectors (energy, transportation, building, chemicals, etc.). Continuous advances in numerical simulation methods over the last thirty years have enabled the development of design workflows incorporating numerous cutting-edge methods, such as geometric modeling, mesh generation, numerical resolution of partial differential equa-tions (PDEs), optimization. Although these techniques are now well established, their wide adoption in industry has yet to be achieved, primarily for two reasons: the complexity of im-plementing these workflows and the computational cost of running them. These observations motivate the development of new methods that are simpler to implement and less costly to run. Recently, the emergence of machine learning techniques has enable the construction of approximate models based on data, whether from experimental measurements or pre-existing simulation results, which have been found particularly effective for many applications, when large databases are available. Many architectures have been investigated, from historical multilayer perceptrons [1] to convolutional networks [3, 21], graph neural networks [33] and, more recently, transformers [12, 43] including attention mechanisms and neural operators [22, 24] mapping functions. Among these, the present work focuses on techniques that make direct use of physical models based on PDEs during the training phase, by incorporating the PDE residuals in

These approaches are known, amongst other names, as Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) [27], deep Ritz method [40] or deep Galerkin method [34]. They have enjoyed con-siderable success in recent years, leading to a remarkable number of publications, numerous extensions and applications across all disciplines, as demonstrated in the review article [7]. However, these physics-informed learning approaches cannot be considered as general-purpose solvers yet, such as finite-element method. Indeed, several studies have pointed out strong limitations, in particular the sensitivity with respect to the choice of hyper-parameters such as network architecture, optimizer, loss weights, etc. [20, 37, 44]. The objective of this work is to study more in depth the role of the neural representa-tion itself in these difficulties, by comparing the use of neural networks and B-Splines in a similar physics-informed machine learning framework. B-Splines are a standard representa-tion in geometric modeling [8, 10] and are also used for PDE solving in isogeometric analysis methods [6]. Recently, they have also been employed in machine learning in conjunction with neural networks [32, 36, 39], mainly to facilitate geometry handling. In a very recent work [5], B-Splines have been used directly to parameterize the solution in a physics-informed machine learning formulation. The present work is based on similar methodological ingredients, but is focused on the analysis of the approximation properties of the two representations. The general framework of physics-informed learning is presented in Section 2, irrespective of the representation chosen for the solution. The specific features of neural networks and B-Splines are described in Section 3 and 4. Their properties are examined in Section 5. Aset of four problems with closed-form solutions is considered in Section 6, allowing a rigorous comparison of errors obtained by numerical resolution. Finally, the results are discussed in Section 8, before conclusion. 

# 2 Physics-informed machine learning 

2.1 Principle of residuals minimization 

Physics-informed machine learning aims to simulate physical phenomena by minimizing an error estimate based on model residuals, typically formalized as PDEs. Although the approach is clearly inspired by artificial intelligence paradigms, the use of PDE residuals makes it similar to standard resolution methods like finite-element method. For the sake of simplicity, we consider a generic problem to present the methodology: the physical rules are expressed as a one-dimensional PDE, first-order in time and second-order in space, associated with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions and initial condition. # 3 Neural representation 

The Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) network is used in the PINNs methodology [27] to repre-sent the solution of the problem. It is composed of a set of L layers: one input layer, L − 2

intermediate hidden layers and one output layer. Each intermediate layer l contains a set of 

Nl neurons, each of them being connected to all neurons of the previous layer. The number of neurons in the input layer corresponds to the number of space and time coordinates, whereas the number of neurons in the output layer is the number of unknown fields, as depicted in Fig. (1). Thus, the values associated with the neurons of the first layer are simply the space-time coordinates (x, t ) of a given collocation point. The value of the neuron in the last layer predicts the solution u(θ; x, t ). Each neuron of the internadiate layers is a mathematical operator that applies a non-linear activation function α to the weighted sum of its own inputs plus a bias factor. The evaluation of the space and time derivatives consists in differentiating the output of the network with respect to its inputs. According to the forward evaluation achieved by com-position of the activation function, the most efficient approach to estimate the derivatives is based on a backpropagation algorithm [30], usually implemented using Automatic Differenti-ation (AD) techniques [2]. The derivatives of the loss function with respect to the parameters 

θ (network weights and biases), required for the descent algorithm, are evaluated in the same way.

# 5 Properties of the two representations 

In this section, we compare the properties of the two representations, in the perspective of the physics-informed learning procedure detailed above. 

Support A first major difference between the two representations is the domain of influence of the degrees of freedom. For B-Spline bases, each function is non-zero over p + 1 knot 7intervals, as illustrated by Fig. 2. In contrast, functions generated by MLPs have infinite support since they are compositions of tanh activation functions. Consequently, the influence of each individual parameter θi is located in space and time for B-Spline representations, and not for MLP networks. Thus, some evaluations can be avoided for B-Splines when the collocation points are located outside the support of a given basis function. 

Smoothness Since a C∞ function is usually employed as activation, e.g. tanh , the output of MLP networks are also C∞. B-Spline functions are C∞, except at the knots abscissae where a Cp−1 continuity is obtained by construction, if the knots are distinct [8]. 

Linear independence The B-Spline functions form a polynomial basis, then they are lin-early independent and generate functions depending linearly on the degrees of freedom. The functions generated by the hidden layers of MLP networks are obviously non-linear with re-spect to θ and not independent in general.

Indeed, swapping the weights and biases of a network can result in the exact same output, yielding several minima. 

Approximation capabilities It has been shown that MLP networks have the ability to represent a large variety of functions. In particular, a neural network with a single hidden layer and a non-polynomial activation function can approximate any continuous function to any desired accuracy [16]. Besides, B-Splines are known to have noticeable approximation properties. In particular, the error for approximating a smooth function decays at the optimal rate of O(hp+1 ) in the L∞ and L2 norms [8], where h is the knot interval. 

Computation of derivatives As already explained, the computation of the derivatives for neural networks is achieved using AD techniques, whereas analytic expressions of derivatives can be used for B-Splines. Obviously, the recourse to AD facilitates the implementation, how-ever the evaluation of analytic derivatives is usually far more efficient in terms of computational time and memory use. 

Account for geometry The neural networks consider as input the space coordinates, usu-ally expressed in Cartesian frame for the sake of simplicity. Nevertheless, other frames, e.g. cylindrical or spherical, can be used as well if more convenient. The situation is different for B-Splines, because the basis functions are defined in the parametric domain, which should be mapped to the physical domain by fitting. This mapping makes the expression of the derivatives in the Cartesian frame more complex, because the geometric mapping has to be differentiated as well. Nevertheless, this allows to match the boundary of the physical domain 8to B-Spline extremities, thus facilitating the imposition of the boundary conditions. This point will be especially illustrated in the last testcase. 

Imposition of boundary conditions In the PINNs methodology, the imposition of the initial and boundary conditions is achieved by penalizing the residual term in the loss function, yielding a multi-objective training problem. It has been shown that the antagonism between the different loss terms is a cause of ill-conditioning [20, 37]. In contrast, the properties of B-Splines at the domain extremities, by the use of clamped knot vectors, allow to impose intial and boundary conditions by projection, independently from the training phase. This is especially important, because it reduces the loss function to the PDE residual only and, consequently, facilitates the training. Moreover, the degrees of freedom corresponding to the B-Spline extremities can be removed from the set of trained parameters, yielding a problem of reduced size. 

Sampling For MLP networks, collocation points are usually chosen randomly, according to a given distribution that promotes space filling, e.g. Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS). Re-sampling or mini-batches are commonly employed during the training phase, emphasizing the stochastic nature of the training. Regarding B-Spline representations, a similar strategy could obviously be adopted. However, the specific structure of B-Spline bases allows an alternate choice based on Greville abscissae, which are the mean locations of p consecutive knots in the knot vector gk = 1

It has been shown that this set of points, whose cardinal is equal to the number of functions, allow a stable B-Spline interpolation and yields orders of convergence p and p − 1 for even and odd degrees respectively [17]. The resulting approach relies only on n collocation points per direction and is in fact similar to the isogeometric collocation method [29]. 

Initialization The inital guess of the solution can be critical for the training, since descent algorithms are essentially local optimizers. The initialization of network parameters is usu-ally achieved randomly with a dedicated distribution, e.g. Xavier uniform initialization [15], because it is difficult to provide a guess for the network parameters. As consequence, the robustness of the resolution has to be assessed by performing several runs because different solutions may be found in practice depending on the initialization. On the contrary, the B-Spline solution can be initialized by intuiting a solution guess, since there is a direct rela-tionship between the B-Spline degrees of freedom and the solution, as illustrated in Fig. (3). Following this review of the properties of the two chosen representations, a set of compu-tational testcases are presented and analysed in the next section.

6.1 Numerical setup 

A set of problems with closed-form solution is now solved using both representations. Some numerical parameters common to all computations are detailed below. They have been selected according to the previous discussions and similar studies from the literature. 9Neural representation For MLP networks, one considers a set of different architectures, ranging from one to five hidden layers, composed of 8, 16, 32 and 64 neurons each. The activation function is always tanh . The sampling of the collocation points is achieved using a LHS technique and remains fixed during the whole training. The number of points is 2000 in the domain, plus 100 on each boundary. All boundary conditions are implemented as penalty terms in the loss function, with unitary weights. The network parameters are initialized with a Xavier uniform distribution. For each test, a set of 5 runs is performed to assess the robustness with respect to the initialization and sampling. The Greville abscissae are chosen as collocation points. The B-Splines are initialized with a constant value depending on the problem. We investigate now the influence of the arithmetic precision on the results. # 8 Discussion 

The following lessons can be learnt from these numerical experiments: • Neural networks have the ability to approximate the solution of a PDE problem using just a few parameters . This has been noticed in particular when solving the Burgers equation, whose solution is characterized by a localized variation of value. The absence of compact support is certainly an advantage in this context, permitting a self-adaption mechanism focusing the degrees of freedom where needed. • Contrary to B-Splines, neural networks fail to reduce the error as much as would be desired . In particular, an increase of the number of parameters does not ensure that the error will be reduced accordingly. No asymptotic convergence has been established, whatever the case. • A significant variability in the results using neural networks has been reported, that seems to be of higher amplitude for convection-dominant problems. This variability is 

due to the initialization of the network parameters , as shown by using a deterministic sampling. The ϵ2-norm of error typically exhibits a variability of factor 2 to 3, whereas variations of one order of magnitude can be observed for the ϵ∞-norm. This is a strong practical limitation of the use of neural networks, since one does not want to perform several random runs when solving an engineering problem. • The resolution of the loss minimization problem is far more difficult using neural net-works than using B-Splines. It has been shown that the optimization often lead to a local minimum and that the convergence rate may be very low, even if a quasi-Newton optimizer is employed. As consequences, a long iterative process has to be achieved and deceptive results may be obtained. Consequently, it seems that the error due to the optimization dominates the errors of quadrature and approximation .• The use of double precision can help to increase the efficiency of the training for neural networks, typically reducing the error by one order of magnitude but maintaining a low rate of convergence. • The use of B-Spline bases yields an efficient physics-informed learning procedure, in-cluding a fast convergence of the loss minimization and an established convergence rate as the number of degrees of freedom increases. This proves that the dominant error for B-Splines is the approximation error. Nevertheless, the tensorial nature of B-Splines makes this representation untractable when the dimension of the input (coordinates and other parameters) or the output (physical fields) increases. According to these observations, the bottleneck for physics-informed neural networks lies in the non-convexity and ill-conditioning of the loss function. This yields a slow convergence and a dependancy on the initialization. B-Splines are not affected by these limitations and can exhibit asymptotic convergence rates in accordance with the approximation theory, but suffer 33 from the curse of dimensionality. This lead to the two following questions: how to improve the convergence of the training of neural networks ? How to improve the scalability of B-Spline bases ? Regarding neural networks, several works can be found in the literature that attempt to identify the key factors limiting the convergence and find a remedy (see for instance [20, 28, 38]). As pointed out in this study, there are certainly several factors increasing the stiff-ness of the training: the aggregation of different terms in the loss function, the mix of data values and differential information, the non-localization of degrees of freedom, etc. Several approaches have been proposed to overcome these difficulties, such as adaptive penalty [11], natural gradient descent [26], preconditioning [31], implicitation of boundary data [35], adap-tive sampling [42], etc. However, as far as we know, none of the proposed methods has permitted to establish a clear convergence as the number of network parameters increases. This property is fundamental for engineering applications because it allows to pave the way towards the control of simulation error. Concerning B-Splines, an improvement of scalability and computational efficiency could be achieved by introducing either local refinement strategies, such as LR-B-Splines [4] or TH-B-Splines [14], that allow to focus the degrees of freedom in regions of interest, and adopt low-rank B-Spline representations [13, 25], that may yield a reduction of the dimensionality of the problem. 

# 9 Conclusion 

The objective of this work was to conduct a rigorous comparison between neural and spline representations in the perspective of physics-informed learning. Four PDE problems have been considered and the results have been compared, in terms of l2 and l∞ error norms, with a focus on the error reduction as the number of degrees of freedom increases. Moreover, the roles of the sampling, the arithmetic precision and the optimizer have been studied more in depth for one case. It has been found that the two representations exhibit different properties. On the one hand, neural networks can provide a rather good approximation of the solution with a few parameters, but suffers from difficulties during the training that prevent from achieving a high accuracy. On the other hand, B-Splines allow a rigourous control of errors, but have a low scalability due to their tensorial nature. Therefore, a possible perspective would consist in the hybridization or coupling of both representations to benefit from their respective advantages. 34 References
