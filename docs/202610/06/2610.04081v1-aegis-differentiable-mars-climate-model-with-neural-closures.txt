ABSTRACT 

General circulation models (GCMs) are the primary tool for simulating planetary atmospheres. They play a vital role in understanding Mars’s atmosphere, as fore-casting its unique weather is mission-critical for operations such as entry, descent, and landing. Mars poses unusual challenges for these models, as observations are sparse compared to Earth. In addition, a thin CO 2 atmosphere alongside a radia-tively active dust cycle creates a volatile atmosphere with large diurnal temperature swings and no true terrestrial analog for validation. Existing Mars GCMs, including the LMD PCM, the NASA Ames Mars GCM, and PlanetWRF, are mature and physically detailed but are implemented in legacy Fortran with finite-difference or finite-volume solvers, and they do not expose gradients for calibration or machine learning. Here we present AEGIS, a modular differentiable Mars climate model that couples Mars’s unique atmospheric physics to the Dinosaur dynamical core, with interfaces for neural closures. We showcase stable ten-Mars-year simulations that reproduce the seasonal CO 2 cycle while conserving the total CO 2 inventory, capture realistic large-scale surface-temperature structure, and produce surface pressure that follows Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter (MOLA) topography. Gradients through coupled trajectories agree with finite differences and support physical calibration and neural training. We compare with conventional GCMs, highlighting the framework’s computational efficiency and differentiability. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

General circulation models (GCMs) connect the governing equations of atmospheric motion to the physical processes that shape planetary climate. Established Mars models include the LMD Mars GCM, the NASA Ames Mars GCM, and PlanetWRF (Forget et al., 1999b; Haberle et al., 1993; Richardson et al., 2007). On Mars, radiative heating by dust and CO 2, exchange with a thermally active surface, and seasonal condensation onto polar caps jointly shape circulation, pressure, and frost. A useful model must represent these interactions while allowing individual assumptions to be tested (Kahre & Haberle, 2010). Traditional GCMs contain uncertain parameterization coefficients whose coupled effects can compli-cate tuning and permit compensating errors (Forget et al., 1999b). Parameter sweeps and ensemble calibration are often computationally expensive (McNeall et al., 2020). In addition, the forward-model interface does not generally expose gradients, and maintaining separate adjoint implementations incurs substantial development effort (Giering & Kaminski, 1998). Introducing a learned component can also require substantial changes to the coupling between numerical and physical routines. These challenges motivate models in which physical simulation and gradient optimization share the same implementation (Davenport et al., 2026). Differentiable programming bridges this gap. When a simulation is implemented as differentiable numerical operations, automatic differentiation applies the chain rule through its computational graph to obtain derivatives of outputs with respect to inputs and parameters or learned weights (Baydin et al., 2018). This supports calibration, sensitivity analysis, inverse problems, and training over multiple time steps. In particular, a neural parameterization can be optimized for its effect on a coupled 1arXiv:2610.04081v1 [astro-ph.EP] 2 Oct 2026 trajectory, accounting for feedback between the learned tendency and the resolved dynamics (Rasp, 2020; Kashyap et al., 2024). NeuralGCM demonstrates the potential of this approach for Earth’s weather and climate by coupling learned parameterizations to a differentiable atmospheric dynamical core (Kochkov et al., 2024). Its Dinosaur core solves the hydrostatic primitive equations using spectral horizontal coordinates and a sigma vertical coordinate. The JAX Circulation Model (JCM) uses the same core with an explicit suite of intermediate-complexity physical parameterizations, providing a setting for process-based studies and hybrid modeling for Earth’s climate and atmosphere (Gelbrecht et al., 2023; Davenport et al., 2026). In a different physical domain, JAX MD illustrates how reusable numerical components and neural models can be composed within a common differentiable simulation environment (Schoenholz & Cubuk, 2020). AEGIS shares the Dinosaur backbone with JCM and NeuralGCM, but addresses a different physical regime and adds coupled state variables and operators needed for Mars. In this work, we present AEGIS, a differentiable Mars Climate Model based on the Dinosaur Core. Unlike the Earth-focused configurations of its Dinosaur-based predecessors, AEGIS uses planetary radius, rotation, and thermodynamic scales at the dynamical interface. It adds Mars radiation and surface physics, evolves frost and regolith alongside the atmosphere, feeds CO 2 phase change into the dynamical core’s prognostic log-pressure state, and projects the coupled atmosphere–frost inventory after each step. These additions make mass exchange and surface memory part of the differentiated trajectory. We make three contributions: 1. We extend the Dinosaur core with planetary-unit rescaling, a coupled atmosphere–surface–frost– regolith state, CO 2 mass exchange into prognostic pressure, and an atmosphere–frost inventory projection. 2. We provide modular Mars radiation, surface, transport, and neural interfaces through which physical controls and learned components can enter the same coupled simulation. 3. We verify physical and neural gradients, evaluate Mars fields against reference products, and demonstrate calibration, year-long forward integration, and controlled neural experiments, and showcase them in Section 3. 

2 METHODS 

AEGIS couples a modular Mars physics suite to the Dinosaur spectral dynamical core, carrying the atmosphere, surface, CO 2 frost, and regolith in a single differentiable state (Section 2.1). Automatic differentiation through this coupled time step provides gradients for physical calibration and for training learned components that enter through a common neural interface (Section 2.2). 2.1 ATMOSPHERIC CONFIGURATIONS AND THE DYNAMICAL FRAMEWORK 

AEGIS separates the description of a planetary atmosphere from the numerical machinery used to evolve it. A body configuration specifies radius a, gravity g, rotation rate Ω, specific gas constant 

R, heat capacity cp, and reference temperature and pressure. We use Mars values for the planetary constants (NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, 2025). The dynamical equations consume these values through a common interface. Length and time are scaled by a and (2Ω) −1, respectively, so the nondimensional radius and rotation rate follow Dinosaur’s conventions for each body, removing dependence on Earth-specific default scales while retaining the existing spectral operators. The framework supports a hierarchy of atmospheric configurations. Dynamical configurations isolate resolved transport; physically forced configurations add radiation, surface exchange, mixing, and volatile reservoirs; hybrid configurations add a learned tendency or substitute a learned component at a selected physical interface. Complexity is therefore introduced through explicit components: a body definition establishes the mechanical and thermodynamic constants, while its radiation, condensables, and boundary conditions establish the atmospheric regime. The physically developed configuration evaluated here is Mars; extending to another atmosphere involves supplying the relevant process operators as well as its constants (Hu et al., 2025). Dinosaur advances the hydrostatic primitive equations on a sphere (Kochkov et al., 2024; Davenport et al., 2026). The atmospheric state contains spectral vorticity and divergence, temperature relative 2to a reference profile, log surface pressure, optional transported tracers, and simulation time, on a vertical coordinate σ = p/p s that follows changes in atmospheric mass and the terrain-dependent lower boundary (Richardson & Newman, 2018). Spectral transform models using sigma coordinates have a long history in Mars atmospheric modeling (Nayvelt et al., 1997). The main experiments use triangular truncation T21, a 64 × 32 longitude–latitude transform grid, and 12 equally spaced sigma layers. The coupled state augments the atmospheric variables with surface temperature, pressure-equivalent CO 2 frost, and a vertical regolith temperature profile attached to their geographic columns. We use forward-mode differentiation for the three physical controls and reverse-mode differentiation for neural weights; forward mode efficiently evaluates derivatives with respect to a small number of controls, whereas reverse mode is appropriate for a scalar loss with many parameters (Baydin et al., 2018; JAX Developers, 2024a). Learned components are exposed through two adapters that share one interface: a model is initialized from a random key into a JAX parameter tree, input normalization is fitted on training data only, and a bind call returns a pure JAX function that is passed to the coupled equations when the time step is built. 4 FUTURE EXTENSIONS 

The present demonstrations span coarse-resolution physical simulations, coupled-heating seed, and structured neural component replacement, and they establish the differentiable physical and neural interfaces. The framework already supports the natural next steps: broader observational comparison, repeated neural experiments across seeds, and multi-year equilibration studies, each reusing the same coupled operators and trajectory objectives. The explicit planetary, radiation, and reservoir interfaces provide a route to experiments with changing Martian boundary conditions. Altered surface albedo, imposed heating, or specified changes in volatile inventory could be introduced through these interfaces and evaluated with spatial climate diagnostics. Differentiable objectives could then connect intervention parameters to quantities such as regional temperature, frost persistence, or atmospheric pressure. For larger changes in atmospheric composition and mass, the body thermodynamics, opacity data, condensable processes, and applicable dynamical assumptions would be extended together. 

5 CONCLUSION 

We have presented AEGIS, a differentiable Mars climate model that couples a modular Mars physics suite to the Dinosaur dynamical core and exposes interfaces for learned components. The model represents terrain, radiative forcing, surface and subsurface heat exchange, vertical transport, and the seasonal exchange of CO 2 between atmosphere and frost within a single differentiated trajectory. Its gradients agree with finite differences through coupled trajectories, and gradient-based calibration lowers held-out reanalysis temperature error in every test window. Integrations of up to ten Mars years remain stable, reproduce the seasonal CO 2 cycle and the terrain-driven surface-pressure structure, and conserve the total CO 2 inventory, with a Mars year completing in under 1.5 h on a single GPU. 9Neural heating and radiation interfaces make the same framework usable for training and comparing learned components. AEGIS thus provides a common foundation for physical sensitivity studies, gradient-based calibration, and the development of hybrid planetary climate models.

A MARS PHYSICS OPERATORS 

MOLA elevation data provide the lower boundary (Smith et al., 2001; NASA Planetary Data System, n.d.). The raster is regridded onto the transform grid and converted into spectral orography. Prescribed dust fields can be supplied through seasonal or per-window adapters. These data remain separate from the numerical operators, making boundary-condition experiments straightforward. Radiation combines CO 2 absorption and dust optical properties in a column flux calculation. The correlated-k approach represents nongray gaseous absorption using a reduced distribution of absorp-tion coefficients (Lacis & Oinas, 1991). The main physical configuration uses bundled Ames-derived 12-band correlated-k tables, interpolated in temperature and log pressure, together with two-stream transfer. Solar and thermal fluxes are evaluated at layer interfaces, and atmospheric heating follows from their convergence. Computing atmospheric and surface heating from a common set of interface fluxes makes the column energy exchanges telescope to the top-of-atmosphere balance. This same construction is used for the neural radiation interface. The surface temperature evolves from radiative, sensible, conductive, and latent heat exchange (Forget et al., 1999a; Haberle et al., 2019). A bulk aerodynamic sensible heat flux couples the surface to the lowest atmospheric layer. The surface loss and atmospheric gain use the same flux, with atmospheric heating divided by the layer heat capacity. Surface stress transfers momentum, and Richardson-number-dependent vertical diffusion mixes momentum, heat, and tracers. The vertical diffusion solve uses a batched tridiagonal algorithm (Petrosyan et al., 2011). Dissipated resolved kinetic energy is returned as heat. Dry convective adjustment mixes unstable adjacent layers while conserving their sigma-weighted enthalpy. A 12-layer regolith conducts heat beneath the surface and supplies thermal memory across daily and seasonal forcing. The CO 2 cycle couples temperature to atmospheric mass. B CALIBRATION AND NEURAL -TRAINING PROTOCOL 

Physical calibration and coupled heating are evaluated using twelve prespecified ARCO-MACDA reanalysis windows (Bhattacharya, 2026; Valeanu et al., 2026). Four windows from Mars years 24, 26, 29, and 31 are used for training; four from years 32–33 for validation; and four from years 34–35 for testing. Each split samples four seasonal quarters. Atmospheric initial conditions are constructed from the reference temperature, winds, and pressure. Surface and frost initialization use start-time fields, and the soil initialization uses preceding surface history. The native reference interval of 7397.937 s is integrated with 25 steps of 295.91748 s. Start-time dust and the orbital phase are matched for each window. The training loss averages squared errors in temperature, zonal wind, and meridional wind, after scaling each field by its training-target standard deviation. It uses horizontal area weights, equal sigma-layer weights, and equal window weights. Feature normalization uses initial states from training data. Both fitting experiments perform 50 Adam updates (Kingma & Ba, 2014) with learning rate 10 −3 and gradient-norm clipping at one. Validation selects the checkpoint, including the initial physical baseline among the candidates. Physical calibration adjusts CO 2 longwave opacity, dust longwave opacity, and surface exchange within bounds [0 .05 , 2] , [0 .05 , 2] , and [0 .25 , 4] . Neural fitting retains all three physical multipliers at one. Reported split RMSE is the square root of the mean per-window MSE, matching the figure-generation code.
