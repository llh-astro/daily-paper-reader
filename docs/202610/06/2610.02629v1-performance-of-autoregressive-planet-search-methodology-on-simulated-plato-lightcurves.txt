ABSTRACT 

Aims. This study examines the PLATO mission sensitivity to small transiting planets (radii between 0.5 and 2.0 R ⊕)using Autoregressive Planet Search (ARPS) methodology based on simulated lightcurves. 

Methods. Several thousand simulations of PLATO-like lightcurves from magnetically active stars expected from the PLATO Long Observation Phase South 2 (LOPS2) field with injected transiting planets smaller than 2.0 R ⊕ radius are examined. The lightcurves are analyzed with ARPS that uses autoregressive modeling for filtering of nonplanetary signals and the Transit Comb Filter periodogram for planetary transit detection. False alarms of nonexistent periodic signals are reduced by thresholds on two ARPS parameters, peak periodogram power, and transit duration. 

Results. When applied to PLATO’s simulated first three months observations of bright stars (P1 and P2 samples), at least 50% of Earth-size planets are recovered for periods shorter than 40 d. In two years of LOPS2 observation, 25% are recovered for periods less than 240 d. Methodological implications on detrending, periodograms, False Alarms, vetting, and catalog evaluation are discussed. The utility of a positive predicted value heat map after detection and vetting is demonstrated. 

Conclusions. While the ARPS planet detection procedure is not entirely well-suited for PLATO analysis, this study provides methodological insight into the sensitivity and reliability of small transiting planet detection. 

Key words. methods: statistical – planets and satellites: detection – surveys – techniques: photometric 

1. Introduction 

The discovery of Jupiter-size planets orbiting close to solar-type stars, first by radial velocity variations and then by transit photometry, inaugurated exoplanetary re-search (Mayor & Queloz 1995; Charbonneau et al. 2000). Hot Jupiters are easily found today and nearly 10% of the 

≃ 6000 confirmed planets 1 have radii above 1 R Jup and orbital periods shorter than 10 days despite a much lower unbiased occurrence rate around 1% (Howard et al. 2012). However it is much more challenging to detect Earth-size planets in Earth-like orbits around solar-type stars; only a handful of confirmed planets have radii consistent with rocky compositions and stellar insolations consistent with liquid water on their surfaces. The Kepler 2 and TESS

A major cause of these difficulties has been magnetic ac-tivity signatures that inhibit high precision measurements in both photometric and radial velocity. For transit pho-tometry with the Kepler satellite observatory, intrinsic stel-lar variability for an ensemble of stars roughly doubled the instrumental noise (Gilliland et al. 2011). For the Kepler

The goal is to improve the sensitivity and reliability of official catalogs of candidate transiting exoplanets emerging from dedicated space-based surveys.

The European Space Agency’s PLAnetary Transits and Oscillations of stars (PLATO) space telescope planned for an early-2027 launch is designed to reduce these limitations to ‘detect small planets (< 2 R⊕) around bright stars ( Pmag <11 mag), including terrestrial plan-ets in the habitable zone of solar-like stars.’ (Rauer et al. 2025). It will obtain continuous lightcurves for more than 245,000 target stars lying in a single Long-duration Ob-servations Phase South 2 (LOPS2) field for at least two years (Montalto et al. 2021; Nascimbeni et al. 2025; Montalto et al. 2026). Sensitivity to Earth-like transits requires highly effec-tive statistical techniques to model and remove intrinsic stellar photometric variations without also diminishing the faint and widely-spaced periodic dips from long period, po-tentially habitable, transiting planets. Filtering techniques must be nonparametric without a global statistical model because stellar variability is largely unpredictable with com-ponents on many timescales. Statistical methods for fil-tering include sliding windows (including differentiation), polynomial fitters (such as splines and locally estimated scatterplot smoothing), Gaussian Processes regression with carefully chosen kernels, multiscale wavelet transforms, Fourier filtering, self-organizing maps, independent compo-nents analysis, recurrent and other neural networks, low-dimensional autoregressive modeling (ARMA), and other procedures. Comparisons of some filtering methods are made by Hippke et al. (2019), Canocchi et al. (2023), and Garcia et al. (2024). The periodic transit brightness dips are then sought in the residuals of the detrended lightcurves, most commonly using the Box-Least Squares (BLS) algorithm or its vari-ants (Kovács et al. 2002; Collier Cameron et al. 2006; Ofir 2008, 2014; Mislis et al. 2016; Hippke et al. 2019). However, if filtering involves differentiation of the lightcurve, a transit box shape is transformed to a double-spike and the Tran-sit Comb Filter (TCF) periodogram is used (Caceres et al. 2019b). Tests show that TCF outperforms BLS under many circumstances (Gondhalekar et al. 2023). Heller et al. (2022) and Krenn et al. (2024) quantita-tively examine the sensitivity of PLATO to Earth-sized planets. They introduce both planetary transits and stellar noise based on short-term solar variability into simulated lightcurves based on PLATO instrumental capabilities. Us-ing filtering with the Wõtan procedures (Hippke et al. 2019) and post-filtering transit detection with the transit least-squares algorithm, Heller et al. (2022) find true positive rates near unity for planets larger than 1.2[1 .0] R⊕ with two[three] or more transits in 2–3 yr continuous obser-vation. Krenn et al. (2024) examine four statistical pro-cedures for quantifying correlated noise combined with a BLS-type algorithm that includes limb-darkening in the host star. They find that, for targets brighter than Pmag 

of 10, a transiting Earth analogue can be reliably identi-fied if more than 2 transits are observed; its radius can be measured with about 3% precision. A more extensive com-parison of methods for PLATO transit detection is provided by Talens et al. (2026b). The purpose of the present study is to provide com-plementary calculations of filtering and periodogram pro-cedures for the recovery of transits injected into noisy lightcurves. Here we use statistical techniques adopted from the AutoRegressive Planet Search (ARPS) procedure developed by Caceres et al. (2019b) with extensions by Melton et al. (2024a,b). These statistical procedures have been applied to Kepler (Caceres et al. 2019a) and TESS 

(Melton et al. 2024c) survey lightcurves. We apply these methods to simulations of PLATO lightcurve simulations with different assumptions of stellar photometric variabil-ity (Sect. 2). The goals of the present study are limited as follows: 1. Only ARPS, one of several existing lightcurve analysis systems, is considered for transiting planet detection. It is mathematically different from the planet detec-tion procedures implemented in the PLATO pipeline (Rauer et al. 2025). 2. Only recovery of a single small planet (radius between 0.5 and 2.0 R ⊕) is considered. Large planets and multi-ple planets systems are not treated. 3. The methodology is not designed for detection of single transits (e.g. Cooke et al. 2018). 4. We consider sensitivity to transiting exoplanets in the presence of photon noise, instrumental gaps in the data stream, and modeled stellar variability. Statistical re-moval of blended eclipsing binary contamination is not tested. These are discussed by Montalto et al. (2020) and Melton et al. (2024b) for the TESS mission and by Bray et al. (2023) and Smith et al. (2025) for the PLATO LOP2S field. In statistical parlance, we treat false alarms (when no periodic signal is truly present) but not false positives (periodicity not caused by plan-etary transits is present). Sect. 2 describes the simulations used in this study. In Sect. 3 and Appendix A, we review the ARPS methodology. Section 4.1 presents the expected sensitivity of the PLATO mission from its first quarter of observations; this is ex-tended to 2 years near-continuous observation of the LOPS2 field in Sect. 4.2 and Appendix B. In Sect. 5, we discuss the results of the analysis, relationship to PLATO pipeline methods, and prospects for improvements. Appendix C dis-cusses the role of multivariate feature-based classifiers that can improve the recovery rates obtained here.

3. Autoregressive planet search methodology 

The ARPS procedure for PLATO simulations begins by binning the lightcurves from the native 25-second cadence to a continuous 30 minute cadence. This arranges for ingress and egress changes to be sudden rather than gradual; this improves periodogram sensitivity. Next ARFIMA (AutoRe-gressive Fractional Integrated Moving Average) modeling is applied to the binned lightcurves, a combination of a differentiation operation for filtering followed by a low-dimensional nonlinear parametric modeling of stochastic autocorrelated time series. Commonly known as Box-Jenkins analysis, ARMA-type modeling was developed around 1970 for analy-sis of econometric time series such as variations in the value of stocks. These autoregressive models have been used in a vast range of time series and signal process-ing problems; a nonlinear extension for volatility received the Nobel Prize in Economic Science (Engle 1982). The general use of autoregressive modeling for astronomical time series is discussed by Feigelson et al. (2018) and the specific use for filtering stellar lightcurves for transiting planet detection is presented by Caceres et al. (2019b) and Melton et al. (2024a). We recommend the online textbook by Hyndman & Athanasopoulos (2021) for explanations of autoregressive modeling including the ARFIMA model. The first step (the ‘I’ or ’Integrated’ in ARFIMA) is the differencing operator, the differentiation of the evenly spaced time series where the stellar flux value at time xt

is replaced by xt − xt−1. This is equivalent to removing a narrow running mean filter. For a regularly spaced time se-ries xt, let dt = xt − xt−1 be the differenced time series and mt = 12 (xt + xt−1) be the running mean with win-dow of 2 time units. Then mt = 12 dt + xt−1. One can thus recover the running mean local smoother with window 2 from the differenced local change operator by dividing by 2 and adding the lag-1 offset series. The running mean is the simplest local polynomial smoother; a spline regression typically uses a higher order cubic polynomial. All polyno-mial smoothers will not fully model structure smaller than the window width.

For most real Kepler and TESS stellar lightcurves, and nearly all simulated PLATO lightcurves, the differencing operator removes the nonstationary flux variations due to magnetic activity, stellar rotation, and other secular or quasi-periodic variation. The second step is to reduce short-memory stochastic autoregressive behaviors that are not removed by differen-tiation. The simplest linear model for a stationary, evenly-spaced time series is an autoregressive moving average (the ‘ARMA’ in ARFIMA) model. Here the current flux value is modeled as the sum of linear dependence on p recent previous values of the flux and q recent previously values of stochastic flux changes, ARMA( p, q ). Letting xt be the current value and ϵt = N (0 , σ 2) be a normally distributed error associated with time t, then the ARMA( p, q ) model is 

xt = θ1xt−1 + θ2xt−2 + ... + θpxt−p + ϵt + (1) 

ϕ1ϵt−1 + ϕ2ϵt−2 + ... + ϕq ϵt−q .

The ARMA parameters θi=1 ,...,p , ϕj=1 ,...,q , and σ2 are fit by maximum likelihood estimation for a range of models with a range of integer values of p and q representing mod-els of different order. The optimal values of p and q maxi-mizes a likelihood ratio statistic penalized for model com-plexity. Typically the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) is used for ARMA model selection (Konishi & Kitagawa 2008; Box et al. 2015; Hyndman & Athanasopoulos 2021). The adequacy of the AIC-selected model is established with the theorem-based Ljung-Box test (Ljung & Box 1978) of the residuals. A p-value greater than 0.01 residuals for this tests demonstrates the residuals are consistent with sta-tionary (trend-free) Gaussian white noise and no further modeling is needed. The fitting procedure is thus deterministic with no free parameters such as a choice of kernel function or width. The best fit ARMA(p,q) model has the effect of removing most short-memory stochastic autocorrelated ripples in flux that remain after the differencing operation removed large-scale variations. As the linear ARIMA (AutoRegressive Integrated Mov-ing Average) model sometimes fails to remove autocorrela-tions on longer timescales, we apply a nonlinear ARFIMA model (the ‘FI’ represents ‘fractional integration’) that is equivalent to adding a long-memory power law 1/(f α) com-ponent to the short-memory autoregressive model. The mathematics relating fractional integration to power law ‘red noise’ is presented by Palma (2007). We find that only 

∼10% of fits to PLATO simulations require a long-memory component with α greater than 0.02. A guide to the ARPS software is provided in Ap-pendix A. Figure B.1 in Appendix B shows three examples of ARFIMA modeling of simulated PLATO lightcurves. In two cases, the ARFIMA model was completely successful in removing all simulated stellar variations. In the third case, residual autocorrelation remains in the ARFIMA residuals implying that a more complex model might further reduce autocorrelated noise. Caceres et al. (2019a) and Melton et al. (2024a) find that the residuals of ARIMA and ARFIMA models for most (but not all) Kepler and TESS lightcurves are close to Gaus-sian white noise. A critical finding is that ARIMA-type modeling is not effective for removing sudden, single-bin spikes like stellar flares or transiting planet ingresses and egresses. The differencing operator changes a periodic box-shaped transit signal into a periodic sequence of down-and-up spikes representing the sudden ingress and egress flux changes. These signals are prominent only if the lightcurve cadence is similar to the duration of the ingress, roughly 20–30 min for most exoplanets (Sect. 5.4). The ARFIMA residuals are thus typically stationary and near-Gaussian white noise, but still containing sudden signals like flares and transits. Planet detection then re-quires the sensitive detection of sequences of double spikes in ARIMA residuals. Caceres et al. (2019b) developed the TCF for this purpose. Once an orbital period, transit duration, and phase are chosen, the algorithm passes a matched filter for periodic double-spikes over the lightcurve ARIMA residuals. A periodogram is then constructed with the maximum power at each period; this is similar to the BLS algorithm but without a least-squares calculation. Gondhalekar et al. (2023) compared TCF and BLS sensi-tivities for a variety of simulated and observed lightcurves. They show that BLS is often subject to higher trends, noise and spurious spectral peaks than TCF. The software implementation of the ARPS transit detection procedure − ARFIMA modeling for filter-ing and TCF periodograms − is computationally effi-cient and simple to code. Appendix A provides soft-ware details and guidance for researchers. Relevant code 5

developed by Gondhalekar et al. (2023) is available at Gondhalekar & Feigelson (2023). Computational time on an Apple M4 CPU for a simulated 2-year PLATO lightcurve, binned to 30-min cadence, is about 20 seconds for ARFIMA, 2 minutes for the TCF periodogram (with pe-riods starting at 0.2 day), and less than 1 minute for other calculations. 4. Analysis and results 

4.1. Single quarter PLATO analysis 

For the estimation of PLATO sensitivity of data from one-quarter (90 d) simulated lightcurves, each inner orbit sim-ulation is divided into eight single-quarter segments that are subject to independent ARPS analysis. Figure 3 com-pares the distributions of three simulated planetary param-eters − planet radius, orbital period, and transit duration −

with the results of the ARPS analysis for the one-quaarter simulations. Under certain conditions, ARPS can overesti-mate planetary radii, underestimate planetary orbital peri-ods, and derive transit durations that are too short or too long (Caceres et al. 2019a; Melton et al. 2024b). While the differences between simulated and recovered transiting planet properties seen in Fig. 3 might initially suggest that recovery of small planets will fail, the com-parison of histograms suggest opportunities for discrimi-nating between spurious false alarms and correct discovery of true positives. For example, the rejection of TCF peaks with transit durations shorter than 1 hour and longer than 

≃20 hours should clean away many false alarms. Further-more, experience with TCF performance with Kepler and 

TESS data, as well as TESS -based simulations, suggests that TCF peaks with S/N less than approximately 20 are of-

ten spurious (Caceres et al. 2019a; Gondhalekar et al. 2023; Melton et al. 2024a). Fig. 4 shows our chosen thresholds for two derived pa-rameters: the transit duration associated with the TCF peak and the S/N of the TCF peak. These thresholds seem reasonable, but the cuts are not based on any algorithmic or statistical procedure. Other variables could be used for cleaning the sample, such as TCF transit depth. More effec-tive removal of false alarms obtained with machine learn-ing algorithms trained on many variables is discussed in Appendix C. The result of this cleaning process can be seen by com-paring the left and right panels of Fig. 5: a much higher fraction of ARPS periods accurately agree with the input simulated periods once transit duration and S/N cuts are implemented. For periods shorter than approximately 45 days, about 95% of the TCF spectral peaks are correctly with TCF periods accurate to ±0.06% (1 σ). From 45 to 90 days, decreasing fractions of the TCF peaks are correctly identified. This is partly due to the increasing presence of only one transit (hence no signal in the TCF periodogram) and partly to the paucity of photometric measurements dur-ing widely-spaced transits. In cases where ARPS derives incorrect periods, the TCF period is shorter than the true injected period. In a few cases, ARPS captures the 1/2 alias of the simulated period. We define ARPS ‘recovery’ of simulated planetary tran-sit signals by cases along the 45 deg line in the right panel of Fig. 5 where the TCF peak period lies within ±0.1%of the injected period. Of the 2712 simulations that pass the vetting thresholds, 1644 (61%) have recovered periods within ±0.1% of the injected periods. This represents 26% of the original 1Q simulations. The dependence of ARPS recovery in one-quarter sim-ulations is further elucidated in the histograms shown in Fig. 6. Recovery rate is largely independent of host star brightness, but depends on stellar radius: recovery falls from 45% or more for stars with radii below 1.0 R ⊙ to less than 15% above 1.5 R ⊙. The third histogram shows that more than 50% of injected planets with orbital periods shorter than 20 days are recovered, but the fraction declines to neg-ligible levels for periods exceeding 60 days. Finally, around 40% of injected planets with radii larger than 1.5 R ⊕ are recovered, but the fraction declines to 20% for Earth-size planets and to negligible levels for radii below 0.8 R ⊕.We provide two heat maps for the one-quarter simu-lations to measure ARPS methodology sensitivity to sim-ulated PLATO planets. It tells how sensitive PLATO will be for true planets in a chosen radius-period cell. Figure 8 shows a second heat map giving the ratio of (number recovered with correct period) / (number satisfy-ing detection criteria) in each radius-period cell following application of vetting criteria in transit duration and pe-riodogram peak S/N. This heat map counts (in radius-period cells) successful and failed recoveries of lightcurves in the right panel of Fig. 5. The two heat maps address complementary questions. The TPR heat map tells us how efficiently planets are re-covered across the radius-period space. These values are needed for inferring intrinsic planet occurrence populations from observed transiting samples (e.g. Howard et al. 2012). The PPV heat map tells us how reliable those detections are once the vetting criteria have been applied. This complex issue is not treated here but is discussed in Fressin et al. (2013); Deleuil et al. (2018) and Hadjigeorghiou et al. (2025) and other studies.

5.5. Implications for PLATO mission sensitivity 

Although the ARPS methodology is not the most sen-sitive procedure for PLATO planet detection (Sect. 5.4, Talens et al. 2026b), the analysis here suggests opportuni-ties for improving sensitivity to small planets. Detrending The differencing operator built into ARIMA and ARFIMA modeling, while effective for Kepler 

and early TESS data, is clearly non-optimal for PLATO with its rapid photometric cadence (Sect. 5.4). But detren-ders based on local regression (such as splines and Gaus-sian Processes regression) risk a failure to remove rapid, low-amplitude, stationary stochastic variations that may be present. These can be identified with examining the nonparametric autocorrelation function and applying the Ljung-Box hypothesis tests for short-memory autocorrela-tion (Figs. 2 and B.1). If significant stochastic signals are present, then fitting a low-order ARMA model and remov-ing it from the detrended residuals may improve overall sensitivity to the smallest planets. Periodic signal detection The TCF periodogram, like BLS, is also non-optimal for rapid cadence data, as it is designed for sensitivity to box-shaped transits with sud-den ingress and egress. TCF thus misses information em-bedded in the curved ingress and egress shapes available to techniques such as TLS (Hippke et al. 2019) and CE-TRA (Smith et al. 2025). However, the results here suggest that the periodogram noise characteristics at periods longer than 1/3–1/2 times the light curve duration should be care-fully examined (Figs. 2 and B.2). Trends in periodogram median levels should be removed and noise characteristics should be carefully examined, as discussed by Ofir (2014) and Gondhalekar et al. (2023), particularly if periodogram signal-to-noise ratios play an important role in planet de-tection. Vetting procedures False alarms emerging from the pe-riodogram are reduced here using a simplistic and heuristic thresholds applied to two properties of the signal (Figs. 4 and 9). But it is far more advantageous to tune a multivari-ate machine learning classifier that gives optimized results (Appendix C). The RAVEN system proposed for PLATO is an example of a multivariate machine learning vetting procedure (Hadjigeorghiou et al. 2025). Vetting evaluation After detrending and periodic-ity detection, a planet detection pipeline proceeds with a vetting procedure intended to reduce both statistical false alarms and astronomical false positives. Analysis of simu-lated lightcurves with artificially injected planet signals is needed to produce a heat map of pipeline completeness as a function of planet radius and orbital period (Figs. 7 and 12). Statistically, this is the true positive rate in a classification confusion matrix. This is essential for converting observed detection rates to intrinsic planet occurrence rates. But it is also important to examine the heat map of positive pre-dicted value that measures the false alarm rate after detec-tion and vetting has been performed (Figs. 8 and 13). This is useful for evaluating the false alarm risk of ground-based followup study for PLATO candidate transiting planets. Catalog interpretation The heat maps in Figs.7, 8, 12, and 13 show that PLATO catalog of candidate transit-ing planets emerging regularly from the LOPS2 survey will necessarily have incompleteness that varies with planet size and orbit. But scientific followup and interpretation may require different true positive rates and positive predictive value levels. For example, study of hot Earths might tol-erate more false alarms to get larger samples than those obtained in catalogs designed for high reliability planet oc-currence rate estimation. The principle of False Discovery Rate classification (Benjamini & Hochberg 1995, Appendix C) widely used in other fields encourages using different classification thresholds for different scientific purposes. It is thus beneficial for the PLATO catalog to be accompanied

6. Conclusions 

We have applied the ARPS – AutoRegressive Planet Search – planet detection procedure to simulations of one-quarter and two-year PLATO lightcurves with realistic photomet-ric variations from stellar activity and injected planetary transits. The ARPS ARFIMA model combines a nonpara-metric differentiation-based detrender with a parametric linear short-memory autoregressive component and a non-linear long-memory 1/f α component. This model is derived from longstanding experience with ARMA-based economet-ric time series analysis. The ARFIMA residuals are then examined for box-like periodic variations with the Tran-sit Comb Filter periodogram. This procedure has been demonstrated to give high sensitivity to small planet de-tection in Kepler and TESS datasets (Caceres et al. 2019a; Melton et al. 2024a). ARPS is not ideal for the native PLATO cadence of 25 s as it is most sensitive when the entire ingress and egress oc-curs in a single time bin. The ARPS detection rates can thus be viewed as lower limits to methods designed for rapid ca-dence light curves. Despite this limitation, the results give clear indications of PLATO mission capabilities. For ex-ample, in the first quarter (three months) of data, ARPS analysis of P1 and P2 stars in the LOPS2 field predicts de-tection of a majority of Earth-size planets out to ∼ 40 days, although the detections at longer periods must combat false alarms in the periodogram. In two years exposure, at least 25% of transiting Earths are detected out to ∼ 240 d with almost no false alarms. Recovery rates are not highly sen-sitive to the brightness of the host star but benefit from smaller stellar radii. The discussion here also provides guidance for PLATO catalog construction is several respects such as statistical testing of detrender performance, complications in peri-odogram interpretation, and benefits of machine learning multivariate classification for false alarm and false positive reduction with use of ROC curves and False Discovery Rate corrections.

Appendix B: Detailed ARPS performance on PLATO simulations 

As outlined in Sect. 3, two stages of the AutoRegres-sive Planet Search analysis are performed here: ARFIMA modeling of the lightcurve, and TCF periodogram of the ARFIMA residuals. The best fit ARFIMA( p,q ) models cho-sen with the Akaike Information Criterion (a likelihood ra-tio test with penalty for model complexity) spanned the range of 0 < p < 10 and 0 < q < 10 considered in our analysis. Goodness-of-fit is measured with the Ljung-Box test (Ljung & Box 1978), a generalization of the Durbin-Watson test that evaluates whether significant autocorre-lation is present in the ARFIMA residuals above that ex-pected from Gaussian white noise. We apply the test out to a lag of 10 cadences (5 hours). Figure B.1 shows two cases where the ARFIMA( p,d,q )model provided an excellent fit to the simulated lightcurves, and one case where the solution was inadequate. The 

≃720 day lightcurve in the top two panels has quarter-long variations and a few distinct month-long events be-tween days 170–300. This behavior was successfully fit with a low-order ARFIMA(0,1,2) model and the residuals are consistent with Gaussian white noise. The middle panels simulate a rapidly rotating magnetically active star with starspots that emerge and dissipate. Here a higher-order ARFIMA(1,1,10) model is needed. Although we restricted ARIFA( p,d,q ) models to p ≤ 10 and q ≤ 10 , this model was sufficient to give uncorrelated residuals. The bottom panels show a case with more evanescent spots that was partially treated by an ARFIMA(10,1,0). The residual autocorrelation function shows a good fit out to a lag of 10 cadences but poor control at longer lags. The residual plot shows autocorrelated behavior most clearly around days 320–400. Despite the limitations of this ARFIMA fit, the injected transit signal for a planet with radius 1.1 R ⊕ and period 493.6 day was the dominant spec-tral peak in the TCF periodogram. When periods shorter than about half of the lightcurve duration are considered, TCF periodograms are densely population with noise. When the periodic transit signal is detected (Figure 7 and 12), a sharp spectral peak ap-pears clearly above the noise. Lightcurves folded at the peak periods show that TCF is remarkably sensitive for 2-year lightcurves, recovering the planetary signal when only 

≃ 0.03 % of the photometric observations are in the transit. But for longer periods approaching the lightcurve du-ration, the periodogram quality deteriorates with trends (also discussed by Ofir [2014] for Box-Least Squares peri-odograms) and constant noiseless power levels. A planetary signal is occasionally recovered in this long-period regime but is often missed. The top row shows an outer simulation where a simulated planetary signal is recovered in a portion of the periodogram that is not well-populated with noise. Appendix C: The role of machine learning classifiers in transiting exoplanet catalogs 

From a statistical perspective, the reliable selection of a small number of stars with transiting planets from a large collection of photometric lightcurves is a problem in imbalanced classification based on pattern recognition (Fernández et al. 2018; Galli 2026). Scientifically success-ful classification thus requires very small fraction of both false alarms (FAs) where no periodicity is actually present and false positives (FPs) where periodicities have a non-planetary origin. In the main body of this study, we consider only de-tection sensitivity seeking reduction of FAs using heuris-tic (i.e. not optimized by an algorithm) thresholds on two variables, transit duration and TCF power S/N. Adding a multivariate machine learning classifier is usually viewed as a tool for optimizing discrimination between transits and FPs. But it will also substantially improve discrimination between shallow transits and FAs allowing improvements in small-planet sensitivity. This issue is recently discussed by Ivashtenko & Zackay (2025); Hadjigeorghiou et al. (2025); Robnik et al. (2026). The heat map recovery rates in Figs. 7 and 12 should thus be viewed as lower limits to PLATO’s sensitivity after 1 quarter and 2 years observations, re-spectively. In the parlance of classification methodology, optimized classifiers will reduce both FAs and false nega-tives (FNs), thereby improving TP rate or sensitivity (often called ‘completeness’ in astronomical surveys). Powerful multivariate statistical and algorithmic ap-proaches based on machine learning have been developed in recent decades that can reduce FAs and FNs while simul-taneously reducing FP contamination. Such treatments re-quire: dozens of ‘features’ characterizing the lightcurve be-haviors; large training sets of labeled lightcurves (TPs, FPs, and true negatives or TNs); and well-designed classifier op-timization algorithms often involving decision trees, boot-strap aggregation, and gradient descent methods. These methods, developed over several decades (e.g. Schapire 1990; Breiman 2001; Quinlan 2014; Robin et al. 2011; Chen & Guestrin 2016), fall under the rubric of ensemble classifiers. Among feature-based machine learning procedures, the Random Forest classification approach (Breiman 2001) is most commonly used for transiting planet identification. It has been applied to ground-based, Kepler , K2, and TESS 

mission lightcurves and is a stage of the widely-used NASA-Ames analysis pipeline (McCauliff et al. 2015; Mislis et al. 

> Article number, page 18 E. D. Feigelson, M. Montalto, et al.: ARPS Performance on Simulated PLATO lightcurves

2016; Shallue & Vanderburg 2018; Armstrong et al. 2018; Schanche et al. 2019; Caceres et al. 2019a; Lu et al. 2020; Montalto et al. 2020; Armstrong et al. 2021; Montalto 2023; Melton et al. 2024a,b). For exoplanet applications, adaptations to imbalanced classes may be needed (e.g., Ishwaran & Kogalur 2024; Siriseriwan 2024). Design of training sets is critical: the DIAmante and DTARPS analyses of TESS data show that FP contamination can be considerably reduced by introduction of simu-lated eclipsing binary FPs into the negative training set (Montalto et al. 2020; Melton et al. 2024a). Specialized measures such as comparing in-vs. -out of transit difference images and transit shape classification can also be impor-tant (Bryson & Kunimoto 2025). An important alternative to Random Forest is XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin 2016) proposed for PLATO transit dis-covery by Hadjigeorghiou et al. (2025). Gradient boosting efficiently focuses on a single best classification while Ran-dom Forest will often give a broader range of possible clas-sifications. XGBoost may thus follow the training set more reliably. But if one uses training sets based on empirically confirmed transiting planet distributions that are largely missing small planets at long periods, then more flexible approaches like Random Forest might be preferred. Once a type of ML classifier and a classifier threshold are chosen, trials with different algorithm and parameter choices are compared by confusion matrices, ROC curves, and their associated scalars (such as Accuracy, Precision, Recall, False Discovery Rate, F1 score, Youden’s J, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient, Nakas et al. 2023). The classification stage can follow any time series analysis pro-cedures such as those discussed by Talens et al. (2026b). Fig. 14 of Caceres et al. (2019b) illustrates the power of multivariate classification applied to a transiting planet survey. It shows the ROC curve emerging from an optimized Random Forest classifier for Kepler 4-year lightcurves based on 37 features of the lightcurves, ARPS analysis, and stel-lar metadata. There is strong improvement (from 40% to 98% recovery assuming a FP rate of 1%) in planet detec-tion sensitivity compared to a univariate threshold on the periodogram peak signal-to-noise ratio. This indicates that the heat maps derived here based on heuristic thresholds of two variables are pessimistic and that a classifier-enhanced vetting procedure will give higher percentage of TP recov-ery. Transiting exoplanet catalogs can furthermore be based on different classifier thresholds. As the astronomer chooses a lower [higher] threshold for the classifier probability, more [fewer] new planets are discovered with higher [lower] con-tamination by FPs and FAs. This tuning of the ratio of FPs to TPs is the principle of False Discovery Rate (FDR) in-troduced by Benjamini & Hochberg (1995) and widely used in many fields. Rather than applying a conservative Bon-ferroni correction that minimizes FPs, the FDR framework allows the scientist to choose a ratio that balances TP re-covery at the expense of FN, FP and TN contamination. This choice is based on scientific goals; there is no statisti-cally optimal value. Constructing exoplanetary catalogs based on different classifier FDRs (or equivalently, different thresholds along the classifier ROC curve) can serve different scientific pur-poses. A conservative classifier, for example, would give a small high-purity catalog with fewer eclipsing binary con-taminants but exhibiting reduced ability to discover mem-bers of unusual subpopulations such as long-period Earths. A more liberal classifier might give a larger catalog with higher TP recovery rate and more opportunity for discover-ies of members of unusual subpopulations, but with higher FP and FN rates.
