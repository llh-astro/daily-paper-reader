# Physics is the Best Teacher: Consistency Learning for Time-Invariant Operators of Chaotic Dynamics 

Lufang Chiang 1,2∗† Jiachen Yao 2∗ Thomas Y.L. Lin 3 Anima Anandkumar 21National Taiwan University 2California Institute of Technology 3University of Washington 

Abstract 

Accelerating the prediction of long-term behavior in chaotic systems is crucial in scientific computing. However, existing methods rely on numerical solvers or autoregressive models that advance one small step at a time, which makes long horizons expensive. We instead view this problem as learning the system’s time-invariant evolution operator, which jumps the state across a large time span in a single evaluation. To this end, we derive the consistency equations a time-invariant operator must satisfy, with differential and compositional objectives in physical time. These equations also connect the learned operator to the physics-prescribed instant dynamics, enabling physics embedding in consistency learning. Across five chaotic systems, we find that physics-distilled consistency makes both short-term trajectories and long-term statistics more accurate. The learned operator survives temporal extrapolation and requires one-tenth as many evaluations as autoregressive rollout, offering an efficient route to long-term simulation of chaotic dynamics. 

1 Introduction 

Predicting the long-term behavior of chaotic systems is a core task of scientific computing, from weather forecasting [1–3] to the simulation of fluids [4, 5]. Chaotic systems amplify perturbations exponentially [6, 7], so a numerical integrator has to take steps small enough that the discretization error does not grow much along the trajectory, and the cost of simulations over a long horizon is therefore prohibitive. An operator trained at a fixed step and rolled out autoregressively [8–10] spends as many function evaluations (NFE) as the solver takes steps, and the error compounds across them. A neural ODE or a Continuous Flow Operator (CFO) [11, 12] replaces the fixed step with an integrator over a learned instantaneous velocity, but still trades solver precision against many NFEs. To accelerate the prediction, one can view the problem as learning a time-invariant evolution operator of an autonomous system. For autonomous dynamics ∂tut = v(ut), this operator takes a state and an elapsed time to the future state, i.e., ψ(ut, ∆t) = ut+∆ t. Because ψ depends on elapsed rather than absolute time, the same model applies from every starting time and can jump directly to any requested horizon in a single evaluation. Recent work learns related operators for molecular and physical dynamics [13–15], while one-step generative models use a similar idea to compress many sampling steps into one [16–19]. Across these settings, the shared computational goal is to replace repeated integration with direct evaluation of a learned operator. Learning that operator is especially demanding for chaotic dynamics [20–22]: it must represent strong sensitivity to the input state while remaining consistent with one autonomous law over a continuum of horizons [23, 24]. What remains unclear is how to formulate these consistency conditions systematically in physical time, and how much imposing them buys over fitting trajectory data alone. Fitting trajectory data alone does not ensure that the learned operator follows the same dynamical law across all horizons. A finite set of trajectories directly constrains the operator only at observed states and sampled time intervals, leaving its behavior at intermediate horizons underdetermined. Regression alone guarantees neither condition, so even a model that fits the observed transitions need not represent the flow of an autonomous differential equation [25]. Enforcing these conditions links predictions across horizons and provides supervision beyond the sampled transitions. The consistency conditions give physics a teaching role in learning time-invariant evolution operators. When the governing equations are known, their velocity field supplies the instantaneous velocity needed to construct targets for the differential consistency objectives. Physics thus enters the consistency targets, shifting the methodology from hard constraints and PINN-style equation residuals [27] to the supervision source. Alternatively, the model can supply its own velocity estimate from its learned “average” velocity at zero horizon. Both choices use the same consistency objectives for distillation [26], which lets us ask whether physics is a better teacher than the model itself. In summary, our contributions are the following. • We explore the design space of consistency learning for time-invariant evolution operators by deriving two differential forms and a compositional form in physical time, each yielding a training objective on the averaged velocity field to promote stability. • We instantiate the same differential consistency objectives with either the model or the governing physics as the teacher. Through controlled comparisons, we show that physics distillation consistently outperforms self-distillation. • We compare all the design choices on five representative chaotic systems, inside and beyond the training horizon, and find that the Eulerian form with a physics teacher stands out.

3 Experiments 

Setup. We use five chaotic systems from the dysts catalogue [7], Lorenz, Chen, Rössler, Thomas and Halvorsen, measuring every metric in their Lyapunov times Tλ. Each dataset holds 10 ,000 trajectories of 5Tλ, detailed in Section C, and every method shares one MLP with residual connections, with or without conditioning on time, with hyperparameters tuned by Vizier [28] on the validation split and every cell run over three seeds, detailed in Section E. The baselines are an autoregressive model (AR) that predicts either the next state ( x-pred) or the velocity ( v-pred), and CFO [12], a continuous-time model that predicts instantaneous velocity, trained on a polynomial interpolant of the data points. For a compact overview, Section A lists each method’s defining equation, NFE, and average prediction error. Besides the relative L2 error for short-term accuracy, we report long-term metrics on the distribution the model settles into, detailed in Section D.

Choice of form. Under a physics teacher, Eulerian is ahead of Lagrangian, though the two stay close and most cells of Table 2 do not separate them, and what differs between the two is where F is evaluated. Eulerian evaluates F at the data state, so its target is fixed by the data and does not move while the operator trains. Lagrangian evaluates F at the operator’s own predicted endpoint, so its target is bootstrapped on the current prediction and follows the model’s own error, which may be the harder signal to learn from. The choice of form matters far less than the choice of teacher. 

4 Conclusion 

In this work, we learn the time-invariant evolution operator of an autonomous system from the consistency conditions it must satisfy, with either the model itself or the governing equations as the teacher. Across five chaotic systems, self-distillation carries little supervision and lands on the data-only control, while physics-distillation improves on it in and beyond the training horizon, where the baselines that read absolute time break down. While we lay out this design space, we do not test every choice in it, and leave a direct test of what separates the two differential forms to future work. 4Declaration of LLM Usage

Each dataset holds 10 ,000 trajectories of one chaotic system, stored as tensors of 1001 frames in a three-dimensional state space and split into 9000 training, 500 validation, and 500 test trajectories. We generate all five datasets using the official implementation of dysts [7]. Since we target long-term behavior, every trajectory starts on the attractor, drawn by the on-attractor initial-condition sampler of dysts: it integrates one long reference orbit and takes the starting points from it, so the dataset is a set of overlapping windows of that orbit and inherits its invariant measure. We space the starting points by a fixed interval along the reference, since random draws would repeat some of them. Every window is a segment of the same autonomous flow, so absolute time carries no information about what comes next, and the data determines exactly the time-invariant operator of Section 2. From each starting point, DOP853 [29] then integrates the trajectory, keeping the estimated relative error of every step below 10 −10 . We run each trajectory for five Lyapunov times, so consecutive frames are 0.005 Lyapunov times apart; see Section B.5 for the normalization. We split the trajectories in their order along the reference, so every test trajectory starts more than 155 Tλ after the last training trajectory ends, with the validation trajectories in between.

D.3 Long-term statistics 

In chaotic systems, nearby trajectories separate exponentially, so no prediction stays aligned with the true trajectory beyond a few Lyapunov times [40]. The meaningful long-term target is instead the statistics of the motion on the attractor, so we also evaluate the model’s output in distributional and transition aspects [7, 20–23]. From the 500 test initial states, each model rolls 50 Tλ: AR and CFO roll 10 ,000 native frames and keep every tenth, and the evolution operator rolls 1000 native steps of ∆tmax = 0 .05 Tλ (10 frames), so every model lands on the same 500 × 1000 × 3 array sampled every 10 frames, while the evolution operator runs 10 times cheaper than AR. For the ground truth, the reference is the high-accuracy solution (DOP853 [29]) at the same times, and the first 5Tλ are discarded; the remainder is flattened over trajectories and times into two clouds of 450 ,000 states each, one from the model’s runs and one from the reference’s. The two clouds capture the model’s and the truth’s long-term behavior on the attractor. 14 D.4 Correlation dimension 

The attractor is a fractal, and its correlation dimension measures how the number of neighboring state pairs grows with distance [41]. We write un ∈ Rd for the n-th state of the flattened reference cloud (Section D.3), N for the number of states, and # for the size of a set, and then the correlation sum 

C(r) = #{(n, n ′) : n̸ = n′, ∥un − un′ ∥2 ≤ r}

N (N − 1) (47) scales as rD2 at small r, and the exponent D2 is the correlation dimension. We estimate it by subsampling each cloud to 10 ,000 states and fitting the slope of log C(r) against log r by least squares [42]. from the model’s cloud. The difference catches a model whose runs blur the attractor’s fine structure into a thicker set, even when every state stays close to the attractor.

E Implementation details 

Model architecture. We compare all methods on the same MLP with residual connections, using four blocks of hidden width 128 , and 341 ,379 parameters in total. Each block applies a LayerNorm, a GELU activation, and a linear layer. The output of each block is divided by four so that the time derivative with respect to ∆t does not grow with depth. For time conditioning, the step size ∆t enters every block through AdaLN-Zero [46] on the sinusoidal features [∆ t, sin(2 πf k∆t), cos(2 πf k∆t)] ,with four frequencies fk spaced linearly in [1 , 8] , and a two-layer network with a zero-initialized output layer maps them to a learnable scale and shift. 

Method details. For the AR and CFO baselines, the model either predicts the instantaneous or average velocity ( v-pred), which is multiplied by the time step ∆t, or the next state ( x-pred) directly. For the evolution operator, the network outputs the averaged velocity ¯vθ (u, ∆t) and the operator itself is ψθ (u, ∆t) = u + ∆ t ¯vθ (u, ∆t), so one forward pass covers the whole horizon. 

Hyperparameter tuning. All tuning uses the official implementation of Vizier 3 [28, 47], and selects hyperparameters by the relative L2 error on the validation set. For each task, the data-only control first searches the learning rate and the EMA decay over 12 or 16 trials, then fixes the best pair for every consistency objective, for which we search only the weight w over 6 trials. The AR and CFO baselines search the learning rate alone over 6 trials. All searches run on seed 0, and seeds 

1 and 2 retrain the selected cells unchanged. For the out-of-distribution tasks, we reuse the values tuned on the corresponding in-distribution task without any further search. 

Training. Table 5 lists the training hyperparameters shared by all methods, which follow the official implementation of CFO 4 [12]. The AR baselines train on the fixed one-frame step and CFO on absolute time, as in their original implementations. For the evolution operator, each training batch draws states ut from the training trajectories and step sizes ∆t, with details in Section F. The model selected for evaluation is the checkpoint with the best validation relative L2 error, checked every

21 H Related work 

Learning dynamics from trajectories. Autoregressive models [8–10] and flow-map learning methods [49] learn a fixed-step update on a uniform time grid and roll it out. Neural ODEs [11] and CFO [12] learn the instantaneous velocity instead. CFO fits a spline through each trajectory and matches its time derivative by flow matching, so it trains on irregular time grids without backpropa-gating through a solver. Both families still advance in small steps at inference. 

Accelerating many-step models. Consistency models [16], shortcut models [50], MeanFlow [18], Terminal Velocity Matching [51], and flow maps [17, 26] replace many sampling steps with one or a few by learning the map between two generative times. Spatio-Temporal MeanFlow [13] carries this map into physical time with the governing equations, between absolute start and end times. Hamiltonian Flow Maps [14] instead learn it over the elapsed time from the governing equations alone, in the Eulerian form. Deep-OSG [52], Consistent Vector Flow [15], and a semigroup consistency diagnostic [25] build on the semigroup property. We derive all three consistency conditions of flow maps for one time-invariant evolution operator of an autonomous system in physical time, which unifies these elapsed-time works. We take the velocity from either the model or the governing equations, providing a more general paradigm for distillation and physics-informed learning.
