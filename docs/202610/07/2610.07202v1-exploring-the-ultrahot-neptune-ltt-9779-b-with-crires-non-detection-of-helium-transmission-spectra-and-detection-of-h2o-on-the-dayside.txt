ABSTRACT 

Ultrahot Neptunes reside in the most extreme irradiation regime of the hot Neptune desert and remain challenging targets for atmo-spheric characterization because of their intrinsically low planet-to-star flux ratios. We present high-resolution transit and dayside thermal emission spectroscopy of the ultrahot Neptune LTT 9779 b obtained with CRIRES +, aimed at probing both atmospheric escape and dayside molecular emission. No significant absorption is detected in the metastable He i λ1083 nm triplet during transit, yielding a 3 σ upper limit of 0.45% on the excess helium absorption depth and supporting previous non-detections that suggest weak ongoing atmospheric escape. For the dayside emission spectra in the K band, our cross-correlation analysis reveals an H 2O absorption signal and a tentative CO feature, with the combined signal reaching a detection significance of 4 .8σ. Both the CRIRES + and previ-ous JWST data favor a non-inverted temperature structure, providing no evidence of a thermal inversion on the planetary dayside. We further perform a joint atmospheric retrieval combining the CRIRES + high-resolution spectra with JWST /NIRISS SOSS secondary-eclipse observations. The joint retrieval yields a low H 2O abundance, while the CO abundance remains weakly constrained. The inferred planetary equatorial rotational velocity, veq = 4.9+4.4 

> −3.0

km s −1, is consistent within 1 σ with the synchronous rotation velocity expected for a tidally locked planet ( ∼ 2.8 km s −1). However, the large uncertainties prevent us from placing strong constraints on the planet’s rotation state. Future phase-resolved CRIRES + observations with broader orbital-phase coverage and higher signal-to-noise ratios would improve the abundance constraints and probe the atmospheric dynamics of this rare ultrahot Neptune.

1. Introduction 

With the growing number of discovered exoplanets, several dis-tinct demographic structures have emerged. Among them, one of the most intriguing is the “hot Neptune desert,” a region where Neptune-sized planets are markedly absent at orbital periods of 

≲ 4 days (Szabó & Kiss 2011; Mazeh et al. 2016). Its location in the period–radius and period–mass planes is illustrated in Fig. 1. The small number of planets in this region cannot be explained solely by observational biases (Lecavelier Des Etangs 2007; Beaugé & Nesvorný 2013). Instead, it is widely interpreted as evidence that strong evolutionary processes actively reshape the population boundaries of close-in planets. Several physi-

At such extreme irradiation levels and such a small orbital separation, the at-mosphere of LTT 9779 b could be subject to vigorous photo-evaporation and Roche-lobe overflow, as suggested by its re-stricted Jeans parameter, Λ = GM pmH/(kBTeq Rp) ≈ 27, its grav-itational potential, Φ = GM p/Rp ≈ 4 × 10 12 erg g −1, and its Roche-lobe filling factor, Rp/RRoche ≈ 0.5 (Vissapragada et al. 2024). Therefore, under such extreme irradiation and tidal forcing, LTT 9779 b is a particularly compelling target for transmission spectroscopy of the He i λ1083 nm triplet, which has proven to be one of the most sensitive tracers of atmo-spheric escape across both space- and ground-based telescopes (Seager & Sasselov 2000; Oklopˇ ci´ c & Hirata 2018; Spake et al. 2018; Nortmann et al. 2018; Allart et al. 2018). In addition, its extreme irradiation may significantly af-fect the planet’s atmospheric thermal structure. First, the equi-librium temperature of LTT 9779 b is approximately 2000 K (Jenkins et al. 2020). Moreover, the follow-up Spitzer and Tran-siting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) observations have measured dayside brightness temperatures of 2300 ± 140 K at 3.6 μm and 1800 ± 120 K at 4.5 μm (Crossfield et al. 2020; Dragomir et al. 2020), which indicate a complex vertical ther-mal structure in its atmosphere. Such temperature levels are comparable to those of typical ultrahot Jupiters (UHJs, 

Teq > 2000 K), which, owing to their extreme stellar ir-radiation, are predicted to exhibit thermal inversions created by atomic or molecular absorbers such as iron, TiO, and VO (Hubeny et al. 2003; Fortney et al. 2008; Lothringer et al. 2018). These thermal inversions in UHJs have been com-monly detected through chemical emission features across both the optical and the near-infrared bands using ground-based telescopes. For example, in the optical, Fe i emis-sion lines have been seen in KELT-9b (Pino et al. 2020; Kasper et al. 2021), WASP-33b (Nugroho et al. 2020; Cont et al. 2021; Herman et al. 2022), WASP-189b (Yan et al. 2020), MASCARA-1b (Scandariato et al. 2023; Guo et al. 2024), and HAT-P-70b (Guo et al. 2026). In the near-infrared band, emis-sion from molecules such as CO or H 2O has also been observed in several UHJs such as WASP-18b (Brogi et al. 2023; Yan et al. 2023), WASP-33b (Yan et al. 2022a; van Sluijs et al. 2023; Finnerty et al. 2023; Choi et al. 2025), and MASCARA-1b (Holmberg & Madhusudhan 2022; Ramkumar et al. 2025). No-tably, space-based observations have also revealed emission fea-tures in exoplanet spectra, for example in WASP-121b with the Hubble Space Telescope (HST; Evans et al. 2017) and in WASP-18b with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST; Coulombe et al. 2023). Nevertheless, robust detections of strato-spheric thermal inversions have so far been largely limited to UHJs orbiting A- and F-type stars. To date, no ultrahot gas gi-ant orbiting a G-type star has shown unambiguous evidence of a thermal inversion, despite theoretical expectations that such inversions should be possible under su ffi ciently intense irradi-ation (Lothringer & Barman 2019). In this context, LTT 9779 b, an ultrahot Neptune around a G-type star, o ff ers a rare opportu-nity to test whether Neptune-sized planets can sustain Jupiter-like thermal inversions under extreme irradiation around cooler solar-type hosts. More generally, atmospheric studies of exoplanets have tra-ditionally focused on close-in hot Jupiters and UHJs, whose large atmospheric signals make them favorable targets for char-acterization. Neptune-sized planets, however, remain consider-ably more challenging owing to their smaller radii, lower at-mospheric scale heights, and the possible presence of clouds or hazes. With the advent of JWST, atmospheric characterization of Neptunes and sub-Neptunes has begun to transition from a handful of individual detections to more systematic investiga-tions. Space-based low-resolution spectroscopy has revealed at-mospheric features in several such planets, providing evidence of molecular species such as H 2O, CH 4, CO 2, and even SO 2

(Madhusudhan et al. 2023; Holmberg & Madhusudhan 2024; Welbanks et al. 2024; Beatty et al. 2024; Gressier et al. 2025). In contrast, detections of molecular signals in Neptune-sized exoplanets using ground-based high-resolution spectroscopy remain relatively scarce. One example is the reported sig-nal of H 2O and NH 3 in the atmosphere of HAT-P-11b ob-tained with GIANO at the 3.6 m Telescopio Nazionale Galileo (Basilicata et al. 2024). Several additional Neptune-sized plan-ets, including GJ 436b (Grasser et al. 2024; Peláez-Torres et al. 2026b; Finnerty et al. 2026), GJ 3470b (Dash et al. 2024), and WASP-166b (Lafarga et al. 2023), as well as sub-Neptunes such

as GJ 1214b (Peláez-Torres et al. 2026a; Nortmann et al. 2026) and GJ 3090b (Parker et al. 2025), have also been investigated with ground-based high-resolution spectroscopy, although most studies have yielded only tentative detections or upper limits. Expanding this sample is therefore essential for complementing JWST observations and for probing atmospheric composition, dynamics, and diversity across the Neptune and sub-Neptune population. So far, LTT 9779 b has been the focus of extensive atmo-spheric investigations through transmission and emission spec-troscopy, as well as reflected-light observations. Common trac-ers of atmospheric escape, such as the He i λ1083 nm triplet and Hα, showed no detectable signals in ground-based transit obser-vations obtained with the Warm INfrared Echelle spectrograph to REalize Decent high-resolution spectroscopy (WINERED) on the 6.5 m Clay /Magellan II Telescope and the Echelle SPectro-graph for Rocky Exoplanets and Stable Spectroscopic Observa-tions (ESPRESSO) at the Very Large Telescope (VLT), respec-tively (Vissapragada et al. 2024; Ramírez Reyes et al. 2025). These non-detections could indicate a weak mass-loss rate and point toward a high-metallicity atmosphere. Furthermore, high-resolution reflected-light observations with ESPRESSO in the four-unit telescope mode did not detect TiO features and fur-ther suggested the presence of high-altitude silicate clouds in the atmosphere (Vaughan et al. 2026). Moving to space-based observations, previous transit observations with the HST Wide Field Camera 3 (WFC3) detected only weak spectral features (Edwards et al. 2023). Consistent with these findings, recent transmission spectra obtained with the JWST Near Infrared Im-ager and Slitless Spectrograph (NIRISS) also revealed muted molecular features and favored an atmosphere with extremely high metallicity (20–850 × solar) and millibar-level clouds at the terminator (Radica et al. 2024). The dayside atmosphere has likewise been explored through phase-curve observations. Phase-curve observations with JWST /NIRISS in the single ob-ject slitless spectroscopy (SOSS) mode revealed a weak H 2O ab-sorption feature (Coulombe et al. 2025). At longer near-infrared wavelengths, recent phase-curve observations obtained with the JWST Near Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec) using the G395H grating showed that the obscuring e ff ect of high-altitude clouds becomes less significant, revealing a carbon-rich atmosphere dominated by CO and CO 2, with retrieved mixing ratios of 

∼ 10 −1 and ∼ 10 −4, respectively, together with weaker H 2Oabsorption features (Ashtari et al. 2026). Their atmospheric re-trievals further favored a near-unity carbon-to-oxygen ratio and a metallicity exceeding 500 × solar. The unusually high geometric albedo of LTT 9779 b has been investigated through several independent observations. Based on CHaracterising ExOPlanet Satellite (CHEOPS) secondary-eclipse measurements, Hoyer et al. (2023) reported an extremely high geometric albedo of Ag = 0.80 +0.10 

> −0.17

, ranking LTT 9779 b among the most reflective exoplanets known. Subsequent JWST NIRISS /SOSS phase-curve observations confirmed the planet’s high geometric albedo ( Ag = 0.50 ± 0.07; Coulombe et al. 2025) and revealed a pronounced longitudinal asymmetry, with the western hemisphere exhibiting a higher reflectivity ( Ag,west =

0.79 ±0.15) than the eastern hemisphere ( Ag,east = 0.41 ±0.10). A subsequent reanalysis of the ESPRESSO reflected-light dataset detected a broadband planetary signal at a signal-to-noise ra-tio (S /N) of ∼ 5.4 and inferred a geometric albedo of Ag =

0.88 ± 0.25, with enhanced blue reflectivity consistent with e ffi -cient scattering or high-altitude clouds (Borsa 2026). Although the CHEOPS and ESPRESSO measurements yield higher cen-tral values than the JWST result, the di ff erences are modest (∼ 1.6σ and ∼ 1.5σ, respectively, based on the reported un-certainties) and do not indicate a significant discrepancy. These measurements were obtained over di ff erent wavelength ranges and rely on di ff erent approaches to estimating the reflected-light contribution. In particular, the CHEOPS albedo was in-ferred assuming a negligible thermal contribution, whereas the JWST phase-curve analysis separated reflected light from ther-mal emission. The wavelength-dependent reflectivity indicated by ESPRESSO further highlights the importance of accounting for di ff erences in bandpass when comparing the reported albe-dos. Here, we present high-resolution transit and emission spec-troscopy of LTT 9779 b obtained with the upgraded Cryogenic Infrared Echelle Spectrograph (CRIRES +), with the latter yield-ing the first ground-based detection of dayside thermal emission from a Neptune-sized exoplanet. No significant helium absorp-tion features are detected during a single transit event. In con-trast, the dayside emission spectra reveal the presence of H 2Oand provide tentative evidence of CO. By further performing a joint atmospheric retrieval with JWST NIRISS /SOSS data, we confirm the presence of a weak H 2O absorption in the at-mosphere of LTT 9779 b. Our results demonstrate the feasibil-ity of using ground-based high-resolution spectroscopy to probe ultrahot Neptunes and highlight the complementarity between ground-based and space-based facilities for infrared atmospheric characterization. The paper is organized as follows. In Sect. 2, we describe the observations and data reduction. In Sect. 3, we introduce the methods for the dayside molecular search and atmospheric retrievals. In Sect. 4, we present and discuss the results of the transmission spectroscopy, dayside molecular search, and at-mospheric retrieval. Finally, we summarize our conclusions in Sect. 5. 

2. Observations and data 

> 2.1. Observations

The CRIRES + instrument is a cryogenic, cross-dispersed, high-resolution echelle spectrograph covering the near-infrared band, installed at the 8 m-class Unit Telescope 3 of the VLT (Dorn et al. 2023). As part of our guaranteed time observations with CRIRES +, we observed the thermal emission spectrum of LTT 9779 b on 9 August 2022 between 05:56 UT and 08:40 UT, as well as the transmission spectrum on 12 August 2023 between 06:07 UT and 08:28 UT. An ABBA nodding pattern was applied to both our obser-vations. In this pattern, the target was observed at two di ff er-ent positions along the slit (A and B), which allows for an e ffi -cient removal of the sky background and detector artifacts. As illustrated in Yan et al. (2023), in the data reduction procedure, we treated the A and B spectra as two independent datasets and combined them in the final cross-correlation results. The thermal emission observations cover the orbital phases between 0.29 and 0.43, where most of the dayside of the planet is visible to the ob-server before secondary eclipse. The transmission observations cover a full transit event with orbital phases between -0.09 and 0.03. We used the Y1029 wavelength setting, covering 950 nm to 1120 nm, for the transmission observations to search for the He i

λ1083 nm triplet. The K2166 wavelength setting, with a wave-length coverage between 1921 nm and 2472 nm, was used for the thermal emission observations, as this range is sensitive to some major molecular spectral lines (e.g., CO and H 2O). The Y1029  

> Article number, page 3 A&A proofs: manuscript no. aa62567-26

wavelength setting consists of nine echelle orders with two nar-row gaps within each order, yielding 27 wavelength segments in total. The K2166 wavelength setting includes seven echelle or-ders with two narrow gaps within each order, yielding 21 wave-length segments in total. We used the 0 .2′′ slit of CRIRES +,corresponding to a nominal resolving power of R ∼ 100 000. For the thermal emission observations, the adaptive-optics (AO)-corrected point spread function (PSF) was narrower than the slit, resulting in an e ff ective resolving power higher than the nominal slit-limited value. This super-resolution e ff ect has also been reported in recent CRIRES + studies (Nortmann et al. 2025; Boldt-Christmas et al. 2026; Cont et al. 2026). We estimated the resolving power by fitting each wavelength segment separately with molecfit , obtaining values ranging from approximately 

R = 95 000 to 160 000 across di ff erent segments. We there-fore adopted a representative resolving power of R = 120 000 throughout this work. The airmass and the mean S /N per pixel during the observations are illustrated in Fig. A.1. 

2.2. Raw data extraction 

The raw spectra were reduced by the European Southern Ob-servatory (ESO) CRIRES + data reduction software pipeline 

cr2res , including dark subtraction, flat-field correction, echelle order localization, and wavelength calibration. Bad pixels were masked after the raw data reduction, and wavelength calibra-tion was performed using a uranium-neon lamp together with a Fabry–Pérot etalon. The time series was organized into pairs of consecutive A and B nodding positions, yielding 33 pairs for transmission spectra and 38 pairs for thermal emission spectra. Then the pipeline was run on each pair, in which A and B spec-tra were subtracted from each other. Subsequently, the pipeline produced one-dimensional spectra, along with associated uncer-tainty estimates for each wavelength segment and nodding po-sition. We discarded the first and last 20 pixels for every wave-length segment to mitigate edge e ff ects from the detector. During our observations, the PSF was smaller than the slit and not perfectly centered, resulting in wavelength shifts of a few km s −1 between the spectral data points and the pipeline-delivered wavelength solutions. These o ff sets typically di ff er be-tween the A and B nodding positions due to imperfect alignment with the slit (Cont et al. 2024). To account for the wavelength shifts, we used the molecfit software (Smette et al. 2015) to fit telluric absorption features and derive improved wavelength so-lutions for the A and B spectra, respectively. We adopted the first nodding pair of the transmission spectra in the molecfit calcu-lation. In contrast, for the thermal emission spectra, we used the third nodding pair due to its higher S /N. The resulting wave-length solution was then applied to all exposures. To assess the additional drifts between di ff erent exposures and examine the temporal stability of wavelength solutions de-rived from molecfit , we further calculated the radial velocity (RV) drifts between the median spectrum and individual expo-sures of the time series using the cross-correlation technique. Fig. A.2 shows the distribution of RV drifts across all exposures and wavelength segments. For both the transmission and thermal emission observations, all the RV drifts are < 0.6 km s −1, with most clustered around 0 .2 km s −1. Since these shifts are smaller than the velocity width of a single CRIRES + resolution element (∼ 2.5 km s −1), the wavelength solutions from molecfit are suf-ficiently stable for our analysis, and we therefore did not apply further corrections to di ff erent exposures. 2.3. Data reduction 

To extract the faint signal of the exoplanet atmosphere, we must remove the telluric absorption, the stellar spectrum, and any con-tribution from instrumental e ff ects. These data reduction proce-dures are described in the following subsections and were ap-plied consistently to both the thermal emission and transmission observations. 

2.3.1. Pre-processing the spectra 

(1) Normalization. During the observations, the flux levels of in-dividual exposures varied, primarily because of variable slit losses caused by changes in seeing and airmass-dependent atmospheric extinction, with additional contributions from instrumental e ff ects such as variations in the blaze function. To account for these variations, all spectra were normalized to a common continuum level. We first divided the wave-

> Article number, page 4 S. Liu et al.: Exploring the ultrahot Neptune LTT 9779 b

length range into 100 equal-width bins and selected the 90th percentile flux within each bin as a representative point on the continuum. A second-order polynomial was then fitted to these points to model the continuum. For every wavelength segment and every frame, the spectrum was divided by its fitted continuum. (2) Clean up the spectral matrix. In this step, we first calcu-lated a master spectrum by taking the mean of all spectra along each pixel column, then computed the standard devi-ation for each pixel column, following the same procedure as in Yan et al. (2023). We subsequently obtained the empir-ical S /N of each pixel column by dividing the master spec-trum by the standard deviation. Based on this empirical S /Nspectrum, the lower S /N pixel columns were set to NaN val-ues. For both of our observations, about 10% of the pixel columns were masked. This procedure ensures that strong telluric lines are e ff ectively masked without requiring prior knowledge of their positions. Notably, normalization has to be performed before this step since continuum variations can significantly a ff ect the computed standard deviation across pixel columns, ultimately resulting in an inaccurate estimate of the empirical S /N. (3) Sigma clipping. To correct bad pixels, we further performed 3σ clipping on the time evolution of each pixel column and then replaced the bad pixels with the mean values of each pixel column. 

2.3.2. Removal of telluric and stellar lines 

To date, two main methods have been developed to correct the stationary telluric and stellar lines in the spectral matrix. One approach involves accurately modeling the telluric spectrum us-ing tools such as molecfit , which uses a line-by-line radia-tive transfer model together with atmospheric profiles from the Global Data Assimilation System to generate synthetic transmis-sion spectra of the Earth’s atmosphere across the observed wave-length range (e.g., Hoeijmakers et al. 2018). Another approach employs detrending algorithms such as SYSREM (Tamuz et al. 2005), which iteratively identifies and removes systematic trends shared across the spectral matrix while accounting for the uncer-tainty of each data point. In this work, we adopted SYSREM , as it has been demonstrated to be a robust tool in high-resolution spectroscopy (Birkby et al. 2013; Snellen et al. 2014). More-over, Maguire et al. (2024) showed that SYSREM outperforms 

molecfit for close-in planets ( P = 2 days in their simula-tions), making it particularly suitable for our short-period target LTT 9779 b. 3. Methods 

3.1. Search for atmospheric species on the dayside 

To investigate the presence of chemical species on the dayside, we performed the cross-correlation analysis to extract the weak planetary spectral lines (Snellen et al. 2010; Brogi et al. 2012;  

> Article number, page 5 A&A proofs: manuscript no. aa62567-26

Birkby et al. 2013). This analysis computes the cross-correlation function (CCF) between the template and the residual spectra, coherently combining the many weak molecular lines that are shifted due to the Doppler e ff ect into a detectable signal peak. We searched for dominant molecular species in the K band, such as CO and H 2O, as well as other molecules including CO 2, OH, CH 4, and HCN, although these latter species are not expected to be abundant at such high temperatures. We also searched for Fe, which has been detected in some UHJs with CRIRES + (e.g., Ramkumar et al. 2023; Lesjak et al. 2025). 

> 3.1.1. Model spectra

The model template was constructed following a procedure sim-ilar to that described in Yan et al. (2020) and Cont et al. (2024). Following the retrieval framework detailed in Sect. 3.2.1, we defined a pressure grid of 51 layers sampled logarithmically from 10 −8 to 10 2 bar and used a ten-point parametric temper-ature–pressure ( T -p) profile. We adopted the best-fit T -p profile derived from the joint retrieval (see Sect. 3.2.3) to generate the final model spectra. The volume mixing ratios (VMRs) of H 2O and CO used for the template calculation were adopted from the best-fit val-ues corresponding to the maximum posterior probability in the CRIRES +-only retrieval (Sect. 4.4). The corresponding mean molecular weight (MMW) was calculated by assuming that the remaining atmosphere consists of H 2 and He in solar proportions (Asplund et al. 2009). However, when generating the individual templates of CO 2, OH, CH 4, HCN, and Fe, whose abundances are not tightly constrained by the CRIRES +-only retrieval, their VMRs and the corresponding MMWs were computed using 

FastChem (Stock et al. 2018) under the assumption of chemi-cal equilibrium, adopting a carbon-to-oxygen ratio of C /O = 1and a metallicity of ∼ 500 × solar as reported by Ashtari et al. (2026). These abundances, together with the temperature-, pressure-, and wavelength-dependent opacities, were then used in the radiative transfer calculations to generate the synthetic spec-tra. The high-resolution opacity data adopted in this study are obtained from the following sources: CO opacity data from Li et al. (2015), H 2O line list from Polyansky et al. (2018), CO 2

data from Yurchenko et al. (2020), OH line information from Brooke et al. (2016), CH 4 opacity from Hargreaves et al. (2020), HCN from Harris et al. (2006), and Fe from Kurucz (2018). Finally, we ran the radiative transfer code petitRADTRANS 

(Mollière et al. 2019) to generate the high-resolution ( R = 10 6)model spectra for the corresponding chemical species. The template fluxes generated by petitRADTRANS corre-spond to the planetary emission, Fp. To be consistent with the data reduction procedure, we first assumed a stellar blackbody spectrum ( Fs) and regarded the observed spectrum as Fp + Fs.Then we normalized the model spectra by dividing them by Fs

to obtain the stellar-normalized model spectrum: 1 + Fp/Fs. We applied the same Gaussian filtering used in Sect. 2.3.2 to remove the planetary continuum, and the normalized spectra were subse-quently convolved with the Gaussian instrumental profile at the observational resolving power and with the rotational broaden-ing profile, both described in Sect. 3.2.1. The final model spectra used in the cross-correlation analysis are shown in Fig. 5.

> 3.2. Framework of atmospheric retrieval

Over the past few years, atmospheric retrieval techniques tai-lored to high-resolution spectroscopy have undergone rapid development (e.g., Brogi & Line 2019; Gibson et al. 2020; Yan et al. 2020). Using retrieval frameworks, statistical con-straints on atmospheric properties are obtained by comparing parameterized model spectra with observations. In this work, the atmospheric conditions of LTT 9779 b are quantitatively investigated following the approach of Cont et al. (2024) and Palle et al. (2025).

3.2.1. Joint retrieval 

Given that the molecular signals detected in the cross-correlation analysis of the CRIRES + data are not highly significant, we adopted a joint atmospheric retrieval framework that com-bines ground-based high-resolution spectroscopy with space-based low-resolution observations. The inclusion of the JWST NIRISS /SOSS data, whose spectral coverage overlaps with the CRIRES + K-band region, provides complementary information on the continuum level and absolute flux scale, thereby en-abling tighter constraints on the atmospheric properties. For the NIRISS /SOSS data, we adopted the secondary-eclipse spectrum reported by Coulombe et al. (2025). In addition, we used the CRIRES + residual spectra after the seventh iteration of SYSREM ,as this iteration yields the strongest CCF signal when combining CO and H 2O (Fig. 6). > 3.2.3. High-resolution retrieval with fixed parameters

To further explore the capability of CRIRES + high-resolution spectroscopy to constrain the atmosphere of a Neptune-sized planet, we performed an atmospheric retrieval using the high-resolution data alone. In addition to H 2O and CO included in the joint retrieval, we also incorporated CO 2, OH, CH 4, HCN, and Fe as additional free chemical species to investigate whether the CRIRES + data alone could place any meaningful constraints on their abundances. However, the S /N of the single high-resolution observation is insu ffi cient to independently constrain all free pa-rameters. These param-eters were adopted from the best-fit model corresponding to the maximum a posteriori (MAP) solution of the joint retrieval. 4. Results and discussion  

> 4.1. Transmission spectroscopy of He i

The goal of the transmission observations of LTT 9779 b using the Y1029 wavelength setting is to search for possible absorp-tion in the metastable He i λ1083 nm triplet at air wavelengths of 1082.909 nm, 1083.025 nm, and 1083.034 nm. For illustration purposes, instead of treating the A and B spectra as two inde-pendent datasets, we combined them by sorting in the order of phase and interpolating the molecfit wavelength solution of B onto A. We discarded one spectrum of A due to data loss around 1083 nm, most likely resulting from a pipeline reduction arti-fact, while all other exposures remained una ff ected. In total, we retained 21 in-transit spectra across all observations, based on the first and fourth contact times (T1 and T4) calculated from the parameters listed in Table 1. We performed the full data reduction procedure (Sect. 2.3) on the observed transmission spectra in the observer’s frame. We found that after three iterations, the SYSREM algorithm could ef-fectively remove both stellar and telluric lines around the He i

λ1083 nm triplet. We also inspected the strong telluric OH emis-sion line at 1083.1322 nm (air wavelength; Oliva et al. 2015), but found no significant emission in our data. Therefore, no masking was applied to this region. The residual spectral ma-trix was subsequently shifted into the stellar rest frame, ac-counting for the barycentric Earth RV ( vbary ) and the stellar systemic velocity ( γ = −10 .6 ± 1.0 km s −1, from Gaia DR2; Gaia Collaboration et al. 2018). The final transmission spectral matrix is shown in Fig. 3. There is no significant signal along the expected He i absorp-tion track. No influence from the OH emission line is apparent in our data, and the deep Si and H 2O absorption features are eff ectively masked and removed, respectively, by our data re-duction procedure and do not a ff ect the in-transit spectra. Next, we shifted the residual matrix into the planetary rest frame using the semi-amplitude, Kp = 225 km s −1 (Table 1). We then com-puted the error-weighted mean in-transit spectrum, where each spectrum was weighted by its inverse variance, to produce the one-dimensional transmission spectrum (Fig. 4). Again, no sig-nificant absorption is observed around the He i λ1083 nm triplet. We further rebinned the one-dimensional planetary trans-mission spectrum into 5-pixel bins (0.017 nm), and the result-ing binned spectrum is presented in Fig. 4. The transmission spectrum has a standard deviation of 0 .15%, leading to a 3 σ

upper limit of 0 .45% for helium absorption. This result is con-sistent with that of Vissapragada et al. (2024), who reported a 3σ upper limit of 0 .20% from observations with the WINERED spectrograph ( R ∼ 68 000) on the 6.5 m Clay /Magellan II Tele-scope. This places LTT 9779 b closer to the temperature regime of typi-cal hot Jupiters, for which no robust evidence of thermal in-versions has been reported to date (Madhusudhan 2019; Snellen 2025). Several cloud-related processes could contribute to this cooler, non-inverted atmosphere. First, Radica et al. (2024) pro-posed a positive feedback mechanism in which clouds increase the planetary albedo by reflecting incident stellar radiation. The resulting reduction in absorbed stellar flux cools the atmosphere, which in turn facilitates further cloud formation. Second, prelim-inary HELIOS calculations for LTT 9779 b suggest that, for the adopted cloud properties, clouds warm the atmospheric layers below the cloud deck while cooling those above it. This behavior is consistent with the e ff ects of enhanced long-wavelength cloud opacity predicted by semi-analytical cloudy-atmosphere models (Heng et al. 2012). By modifying the vertical temperature gradi-ent, this radiative e ff ect may weaken or suppress a thermal in-version driven by high-altitude short-wavelength absorbers. Fi-nally, condensation or cold trapping may deplete iron and other atomic metals from the upper atmosphere, removing some of the principal short-wavelength absorbers thought to drive ther-mal inversions in ultrahot giant planets (Lothringer et al. 2018; Parmentier et al. 2018). The combined e ff ects of enhanced re-flection, cloud-induced radiative redistribution, and the depletion of gaseous optical absorbers may therefore make the atmosphere of LTT 9779 b cooler and less favorable for the development of a thermal inversion than would otherwise be expected. 5. Conclusions 

In this work, we presented high-resolution CRIRES + transit and dayside emission spectroscopy of the ultrahot Neptune LTT 9779 b. During the transit observation, we searched for ab-sorption in the metastable He i λ1083 nm triplet and found no significant signal. From the combined transmission spectrum, we derived a 3 σ upper limit of 0.45% for the helium absorption depth, consistent with previous ground-based non-detections and suggesting that the planet is unlikely to be undergoing strong, extended H /He-dominated atmospheric escape at present. For the dayside observation, we performed a cross-correlation analysis in the CRIRES + K-band spectra to search for molecular emission or absorption signatures. We found an absorption signal of H 2O with a S /N of ∼ 4.5 and a tentative sig-nal of CO. The combined signal of H 2O and CO reaches a S /Nof ∼ 4.8. The location of the signal in the Kp-∆v map is broadly consistent with the expected planetary trail. To further assess the robustness of the high-resolution signal, we performed atmospheric retrievals combining the CRIRES +

data with JWST NIRISS /SOSS secondary-eclipse observations. The joint retrieval yields a non-inverted T -p profile and a rela-tively low H 2O abundance, consistent with the weak water fea-tures seen in both the low- and high-resolution data. In addition, the retrieved Kp is consistent with the theoretical orbital value within the uncertainties, further supporting the interpretation that the high-resolution signal arises from the dayside atmosphere of LTT 9779 b. A CRIRES +-only retrieval with the T -p profile, Kp,and ∆v fixed to the joint-retrieval values independently recovers the H 2O signal, while the evidence of CO remains marginal. Overall, our results indicate the presence of weak H 2O ab-sorption in the dayside atmosphere of LTT 9779 b and suggest the absence of a strong thermal inversion in the pressure range probed by the observations. This picture is consistent with a cloudy, high-albedo atmosphere, where enhanced reflection may reduce the absorbed stellar energy. Cloud-induced radiative ef-fects may also modify the vertical temperature gradient in a way that disfavors the development of a strong thermal inversion. Our study demonstrates that ground-based high-resolution spectroscopy can probe molecular signatures in Neptune-sized exoplanets, even when the planet-to-star flux ratio is extremely small. More importantly, these results highlight the complemen-tary strengths of joint retrievals combining ground-based and space-based observations. Although the continuum information is removed from the high-resolution spectra during normaliza-tion, ground-based spectroscopy remains highly sensitive to the relative depths and contrasts of individual molecular lines and provides direct constraints on the planetary orbital velocity and possible atmospheric dynamics. In contrast, space-based low-resolution observations retain the continuum information and are therefore better suited to constraining the broadband spec-tral shape, reflected-light contribution, and thermal structure of deeper atmospheric layers. However, the current constraints from the CRIRES + data are still limited by the S /N of a single pre-eclipse observation.
