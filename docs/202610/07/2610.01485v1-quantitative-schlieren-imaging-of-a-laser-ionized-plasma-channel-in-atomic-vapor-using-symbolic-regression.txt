# Quantitative schlieren imaging of a laser-ionized plasma channel in atomic vapor using symbolic regression 

G. Demeter a, ∗

> aHUN-REN Wigner Research Centre for Physics, Budapest, Hungary

Abstract 

The plasma channel of the AWAKE plasma wakefield acceleration experiment is created by laser ionization of rubidium vapor in a 10 m long vapor source. Verifying the properties of the channel — the radius of its fully ionized core and the width of its boundary sheath — that are vital for ensuring good-quality wakefields is important as is validating numerical models of ionizing pulse propagation. Near-resonant schlieren imaging can detect the plasma channel in the low-density vapor, but there is no direct inversion to obtain channel parameters from the images. We use symbolic regression to find compact analytical formulas that connect measurable properties of the schlieren signal to plasma profile parameters. Using databases of simulated signals generated with three different plasma edge-profile families, we obtain formulas with one and two fitting constants to predict the equivalent channel radius with an accuracy below twice the camera pixel size and formulas to predict the sheath width. Re-evaluating previous measurements with these formulas, we confirm a theoretically predicted power-law relationship between the channel radius and the energy of the ionizing laser pulse. The formulas can be adapted to new experimental conditions by refitting the constants on a modest set of new samples, providing a lightweight, interpretable alternative to evaluation with deep neural networks. 

1. Introduction 

Schlieren imaging is a sensitive optical technique for visualizing spatially varying refractive index fields in transparent media [1]. A collimated probe beam is deflected where transverse refractive index gradients are present; a mask placed at the focal plane of a lens blocks the undeflected beam and passes the deflected portion. The second lens thus images only this deflected portion onto a camera. Originally developed for aerodynamics and fluid dy-namics, where it has been used to visualize shock waves, turbulent flows, and heat transfer [1], the technique has also found application in the study of laser-produced plasmas [2, 3, 4, 5, 6], and has

been tested for imaging atomic excitation [7] and laser-generated plasma columns [8] in rubidium va-por. The AWAKE experiment at CERN is a proton-driven plasma wakefield accelerator, aiming to ac-celerate electron bunches to hundreds GeV energy in a single plasma stage [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. The plasma channel required for wakefield generation is created by the photoionization of rubidium va-por along a ∼10 m long vapor source by a focused, short-pulse laser. Because the homogeneity of the plasma density in a long channel of sufficiently large radius is essential for the quality of the proton-bunch self-modulation that drives the wakefield, as well as for the particle acceleration process, it is im-portant to verify the properties of the plasma col-arXiv:2610.01485v1 [physics.plasm-ph] 1 Oct 2026 umn. In particular, the radius of the channel core within which the plasma density is constant, and the width of the plasma sheath (the transition re-gion at the plasma boundary) are important param-eters. Numerical simulations of the ionizing pulse propagation [14, 15, 16] make predictions about these quantities that should be compared with ex-perimental measurements to verify the numerical model. In turn, the verified numerical code can be used to predict the length limits of plasma channel creation in a given ionizing laser setup. When an intense laser pulse ionizes a gas or va-por, it changes the refractive index of the medium along its path, so the plasma channel it creates can be imaged by a transverse probe beam that samples this refractive index change across the channel cross section. Such measurements are complicated by the low density of the rubidium vapor (of order 10 14 –

10 15 cm −3), which makes the plasma optically thin and its direct imaging challenging. Near-resonant schlieren imaging, that uses a probe beam tuned close to the rubidium D 2 resonance line, exploits the anomalous dispersion of the residual neutral va-por to obtain a large refractive index signal even at these low densities, making the plasma column de-tectable as a local reduction in neutral-atom density [7, 8]. The key challenge is then to extract quantita-tive plasma channel parameters from the observed schlieren image. Because the schlieren signal de-pends on the phase integral of the probe through the entire column cross-section, modified by the spatial filtering of the mask, a direct analytical in-version is not possible. Previous approaches to this inverse problem in the context of AWAKE have in-cluded training deep neural networks (DNNs) on large databases of simulated schlieren signals [17]. While DNNs can predict plasma parameters with high accuracy, they require construction of very large training databases ( ∼ 10 6 samples), expen-sive training by high-power GPU clusters, and full retraining whenever experimental parameters such as probe wavelength or vapor density change con-siderably. A second approach [8] assumed a single Gaussian-edge plasma profile and identified, from numerically computed sample sets, that signal peak properties are well approximated by bilinear func-tions of the plasma core radius and sheath width; this yields a compact description with eight fitting constants per set of experimental parameters. Symbolic regression is a machine learning tech-nique that searches the space of analytical expres-sions — most often with evolutionary algorithms — for compact formulas that describe a dataset, balancing prediction accuracy against formula com-plexity [18, 19, 20]. In contrast to DNNs, which en-code the learned input–output relationship in an opaque set of millions of network weights, sym-bolic regression yields explicit closed-form expres-sions that can be inspected, interpreted physically, and used in error propagation. Because the class of candidate models is much more constrained, it typically requires substantially smaller training datasets than DNNs, at the price of being practical only for a modest number of input variables. In this paper we take a more general and sys-tematic approach to the schlieren inverse problem than we employed before, using symbolic regres-sion. We characterize the plasma column by two profile-agnostic parameters: the equivalent radius 

Req , defined through the area under the radial ion-ization probability profile, and a parameter char-acterizing the width of the plasma sheath. We generate databases using a mixture of three dis-tinct edge profile shapes (Gaussian, sigmoid, and cosine) and a random set of parameters governing the plasma core location, radius and sheath layer width. Symbolic regression then discovers compact analytical formulas, with a small number of free constants, that link observed schlieren signal prop-erties directly to these plasma parameters. Once determined for a given set of experimental condi-2tions, the formulas for the plasma radius can be adapted to new conditions (e.g. a different vapor density or probe wavelength) by refitting those con-stants alone on a modest database of new samples. We describe the sample generation procedure, the signal analysis strategy, the resulting formulas, and discuss their accuracy, limitations, and advantages relative to previous approaches. 

2. Schlieren imaging of the plasma column 

The schlieren setup used for AWAKE plasma channel imaging is described in detail in [8], and we only summarize the aspects most relevant to the present analysis. A probe beam at λp = 780 .311 nm (in vacuum), tuned close to the rubidium D 2 res-onance at λD2 = 780 .241 nm, traverses the va-por source transversely. Near the resonance, the anomalous dispersion of the neutral rubidium vapor contributes a refractive index change δn ∼ 10 −4–

10 −3, which is three to four orders of magnitude larger than the plasma dispersion contribution at the same density. When the ionizing laser cre-ates plasma, the local population of the rubidium ground state is reduced, lowering the refractive in-dex in the plasma region and imprinting a spatially dependent phase shift on the probe beam. The choice of probe wavelength is a compromise: the probe must be tuned close enough to resonance to obtain sufficient refractive index sensitivity, but far enough to avoid excessive resonant absorption in the vapor. The optimal detuning also depends on the vapor density, in part through the collisional broadening of the atomic line [21]. The probe beam passes through two 75 cm focal length lenses arranged in a 4f configuration [22], with a circular mask of diameter Dm = 1 .5 mm placed at the back focal plane of the first lens. This acts as a high-pass spatial filter, blocking the unde-flected probe and transmitting light that has been deflected by the plasma column density gradient. The mask diameter is also subject to a trade-off: a mask that is too wide reduces signal amplitude and signal-to-noise ratio, while one that is too small may let some undeflected light pass and reduce image contrast. A gated, image-intensified camera records the resulting schlieren image, triggered 100 ns after the ionizing pulse with a gate duration of 100 ns. This timing is chosen so that atoms excited but not ionized by the laser return to the ground state by spontaneous decay before the image is taken, while plasma recombination (on a 1–10 μs timescale at these densities) does not yet alter the plasma den-sity appreciably. Since the plasma column is uniform along the 3ionizing laser propagation direction x on the length scale of the probe beam diameter ( w = 3 .1 mm FWHM), the x dependence of the image reflects only the probe beam intensity profile, and the two-dimensional schlieren image can be reduced to a one-dimensional signal s(y) by averaging a narrow horizontal strip of the image along x. The re-sulting 1D signal exhibits two prominent peaks at positions y1 and y2 on either side of the plasma col-umn center, arising from the phase gradient at the upper and lower boundaries of the plasma. Light diffracted from the upper and lower edges of the circular mask interferes in the image plane, super-imposing a periodic fringe pattern with spatial fre-quency fy ≈ Dm/(λpl) (where l = 75 cm is the dis-tance between the mask and the second lens) on the two-peaked signal structure. This interference dis-torts the peak shape and is particularly detrimental for narrow plasma channels, where the fringe period can be comparable to the peak separation and the interference amplitude comparable to the peak am-plitude itself. 3.2. Signal fitting and predictor variables 

To apply symbolic regression, each signal must first be condensed into a small number of pre-dictor variables, from which we attempt to infer the plasma channel properties Req and the sheath width. This is in contrast to the DNN approach of [17], where the full 1024-point raw signal vector served as the network input; a small set of infor-mative predictors is essential for the regression to search the space of candidate formulas efficiently, and it keeps the resulting formulas interpretable. Most signals in the database display the charac-teristic structure discussed above: two large peaks with the interference pattern superposed, the mod-ulation due to the interference being most signifi-cant for small core radii r0. The peaks are some-what asymmetric, and the asymmetry is more sig-nificant for sharper plasma edges (narrow sheaths). These observations suggest fitting the signal with a double-peaked model function that allows dif-ferent width parameters on the two sides of each maximum. Among the model functions we tested — symmetric and asymmetric double Gaussians and double Lorentzians — the asymmetric double Lorentzian gives the best fit quality to both the simulated and the experimentally observed signals:

3.3. Predicting Req 

Symbolic regression was applied to both the N =5 · 10 14 cm −3 and N = 7 · 10 14 cm −3 datasets to discover formulas predicting Req from the predic-tor variables {∆, W, W in , W out , W dif f }. We used the PySR package [20] to perform the symbolic re-gression. PySR allows the user to decide what com-plexity is attached to various operators, functions and constants in the formulas. Various complexity settings were explored, as well as the inclusion of various nonlinear functions (power, square root, ex-ponential, log). Predictor sets of independent quan-tities {∆, W, W dif f } and {∆, W in , W out } were tried, as well as the full set {∆, W, W in , W out , W dif f }.This is useful in our case, because PySR may attach different complexities to the same formula if derived from different sets, whereas for a human observer they may be of equivalent complexity even in mixed form. Because of the non-deterministic evolution-ary algorithm, it is also useful to run the search several times, as different formulas may (and will) eventually emerge from the search. As a loss func-tion we used the mean absolute error, denoted σ

throughout, the same measure used in [17]. So which of the formulas returned should we use as the final “predictor” for Req ? There are several 6considerations. First, we may plot the decrease of the loss on the dataset used for fitting (Fig. 3). The plot has a long tail where the loss decreases with complexity only marginally, so it seems obvi-ous to select formulas that are just after the last major decrease of the loss function, that being in some way an optimum with respect to loss and com-plexity together. In the absence of the same formula appearing in differ-ent density runs, we may test the cross-transfer of formulas found for the two densities: if a formula found for N = 5 performs similarly well, with re-calculated constants, on the N = 7 control data (or vice versa), it should be preferred over formulas that do not transfer well. Another consideration is interpretability. Even if we do not discover a formula which excels uni-versally compared to all other variants of similar complexity, it may happen that some structure in some of the formulas can be given clear physical meaning. 3.4. Predicting the sheath width 

Predicting the sheath transition width proves harder than predicting Req . From the two can-didates (dP/dr )−1 proved to be more predictable. When searching for formulas attempting to predict 

W91 and separating the performance with respect to the three plasma profile functions, we repeatedly found large performance gaps. This was not the case for (dP/dr )−1, the quantity we use exclusively in what follows. Symbolic regression over the same dataset returns multiple formulas of similar com-plexity and prediction accuracy for (dP/dr )−1, but with very different functional forms. Plots of the loss functions shown in Figs. 5 a) and b) show that, while for N = 5 the loss converges to σ < 0.03 ,the N = 7 fits eventually plateau at roughly three times this value, confirming that the sheath width is inherently harder to predict from these predic-tor variables at the higher density. The loss of predictability is fundamentally tied to the refractive index, i.e. the optical density, through the phase difference accumulated by the probe beam. The refractive index depends strongly on the vapor density because the optical linewidth broadens due to collisional broadening. By tun-ing the probe wavelength slightly farther from reso-nance, the refractive index at the higher density can be matched to that at the lower density. The symbolic regression approach offers several practical advantages over the methods used pre-viously for evaluating schlieren imaging measure-ments [17, 8]. The DNN approach of [17] requires a very large training database ( ∼ 10 6 samples) and a complex neural network with 10 6–10 7 trainable pa-rameters (weights). The required numerical effort is sizeable: it necessitates several days of process-ing time on modern, high-power GPU clusters. No human input is required in deriving predictor vari-ables from the signals, since the input to the DNN is the signal vector itself, but one has to experi-ment with various network architectures and sizes, each with its own training run, and add noise to the samples to make the predictions robust to ex-perimental noise. The resulting trained networks predict the plasma parameters very accurately, but a full re-training run is required whenever the ex-perimental parameters change by more than a few percent. (It must be noted that the DNN ap-proach was tested only for a single plasma density, 

N = 5 × 10 14 cm −3, and only for the less general, single Gaussian sheath profile (Eq. 1a).) Further-more, in [17] plasma parameters were predicted si-multaneously by the same DNN, so to obtain com-parable relative accuracy the parameters had to be scaled to roughly the same magnitude. In contrast, the current symbolic regression pro-cedure requires a training database of just 10 3 sam-ples; sample generation takes about two minutes and the regression itself about 20 minutes on an ordinary desktop computer. Once functional forms have been derived for a range of densities (or other varying experimental parameters), adapting to new conditions requires only a quick re-fit of 1–3 con-stants on a modest new database. The resulting for-mulas are also fully explicit, which facilitates cross-checking and error propagation. On the other hand, human input (and experimentation) is needed in de-termining the few parameters of the signal that are used for plasma parameter prediction. In [8], a Gaussian plasma profile was assumed, symmetric peak parameters were derived from the signals, and it was observed that, to a fairly good approximation: 

∆ = M12 r0t0 + M1r0 + M2t0 + BW = Q12 r0t0 + Q1r0 + Q2t0 + P (12) Here ∆ is identical to the one used in the present work, and W is an amplitude-weighted mean peak width derived from the filtered signal without curve fitting. The approach was then to use the fit-ting database to fit the 8 free parameters of the model (separately for both plasma densities) and invert the relationships to find the plasma parame-ters r0, t 0. This approach is numerically light, just like symbolic regression, but it uses 8 fitting con-stants, and is more restrictive in that it assumes a single plasma profile. Furthermore, it required considerable experimentation and human work to find the approximate relationship Eq. 12 which still does not exploit the information obtainable from the asymmetry of the peaks. The final test for the method is using it to eval-uate real experimental data and comparing the re-sults with values expected from numerical simula-tions. 4. Summary 

We have presented a symbolic regression ap-proach for evaluating near-resonant schlieren im-ages of the laser-ionized plasma channel of the AWAKE experiment. The plasma column is char-acterized by two profile-agnostic parameters —the equivalent radius Req and the sheath width 

(|dP/dr |max )−1 — and databases of simulated schlieren signals are generated using three differ-ent families of plasma edge profiles. Each signal is condensed into five predictor variables by fit-ting an asymmetric double Lorentzian to the two-peaked signal structure, with low-pass filtering ap-plied when the interference caused by the mask dominates the signal. Symbolic regression with the PySR package then yields compact analytical formulas connecting the predictor variables to the plasma parameters. For the equivalent radius, a purely linear for-mula with a single adjustable constant predicts 

Req with a mean absolute error of about twice the camera pixel size, and a slightly nonlinear formula with two constants performs even better. Both forms recur across regression runs for the two va-por densities studied, with constants re-optimized for each density. The sheath width is inherently harder to predict, especially at the higher vapor density, where enhanced collisional broadening of the atomic line degrades predictability; re-tuning the probe wavelength slightly farther from reso-nance restores the accuracy and even allows the direct transfer of the formulas found for the lower density. Compared with the deep neural network approach of [17], the method requires a training database smaller by three orders of magnitude and runs on an ordinary desktop computer in well under an hour, while yielding fully explicit, interpretable formulas; compared with the bilinear model of [8], it needs fewer fitting constants and does not as-sume a single specific plasma edge profile. Finally, we applied the method to experimental data, re-evaluating schlieren measurements taken near the end of the AWAKE vapor source. Plasma radius values obtained with the present method corrobo-rate the theoretically predicted power-law relation-ship between Req and the ionizing pulse energy, with fitted constants close to those obtained from 12 simulations.
