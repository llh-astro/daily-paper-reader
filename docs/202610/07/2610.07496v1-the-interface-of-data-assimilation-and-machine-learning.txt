# 1 Introduction 

Data assimilation (DA) is the process of combining forecasts from a model with observations in order to optimally estimate the state of a system. This is critical for chaotic systems, such as the atmosphere, since if observations are not continually assimilated the model will quickly lose skill. DA is routinely performed (usually every 6 hours) at operational forecasting centres around the world. In this chapter we will discuss the interface of machine learning (ML) and DA. This is still an emerging and quickly developing field, and this chapter tries to give an overview of some of the main topics and methods. We only overview methods in a cursory way, with pointers to the wider literature. A more mathe-matical and comprehensive overview of the topics discussed here is provided in Bach et al. [2026c], and some of the exposition here is based on the latter. See also the review papers of Cheng et al. [2023], Bocquet [2023]. 

# 2 Data assimilation fundamentals 

A classic textbook on DA from a meteorological perspective is Kalnay [2002], and is still one of the best books on the subject. Other recommended references that take a more mathematical perspective are Law et al. [2015], Asch et al. [2016], Sanz-Alonso et al. [2023]. A good review paper on DA in the geosciences is Carrassi et al. [2018]. We use here the notation of Ide et al. [1997], which is common in the meteorological and oceanographic literature. An important topic in DA is that of uncertainty quantification, which is naturally expressed in a Bayesian framework. Within this framework DA is known as filtering and smoothing . In this chapter we will generally focus on the problem of state estimation , which instead tries to obtain a point estimate. However, many of the methods discussed below can be extended to the Bayesian problem of estimating the posterior, and we will point to further resources when this is the case. Hybrid ensemble–variational methods combine the strengths of both approaches, and usually have the best performance [Carrassi et al., 2018]. 

# 3 Machine learning for data assimilation 

In what follows, we will consider the training of ML algorithms abstractly as an optimisation problem for parameters θ. In the presentation of the algorithms, we will not discuss specific ML architectures in order to maintain generality. θ could represent, for instance, the weights and biases of a neural network. We also avoid discussing specific optimisation methods, instead presenting cost functions J(θ) to be minimised with respect to θ. In many of these cases, optimisation will require automatic differentiation 

to compute gradients of the cost function. Automatic differentiation is an important component of many ML software libraries. In some cases discussed below, the methods will require the gradients of the forecast model, the observation operator, and/or the DA algorithm, which may pose practical difficulties for implementation. 

# 3.1 Using data assimilation to learn a model 

Probably the most widespread application of the combination of ML and DA is in learning an ML forecast model, or learning a model error correction to an existing physics-based model. 

3.1.1 Learning a forecast model using reanalysis 

Reanalysis is widely used to train ML forecast models. This is, indeed, how most of the ML weather models discussed in Chapter 4 were trained. Why train on reanalysis? Consider the alternatives: 1. Why not train directly on observations? Observations are typically sparse and noisy, and irregular in space and time. Reanalysis functions as a physics-based interpolator, being generally more accurate than the observations themselves, and available at every point on the model grid at regular time intervals. 2. Why not train directly on model output? If we train on physics-based model output, we cannot do better than the model itself, and would inherit any inaccuracies it might have. We add two caveats to these points. Although reanalysis will generally have less model bias than raw model output, it will still have some remaining bias. This has led to attempts to train directly on observations (see section 3.6). On the other hand, training on model output can be useful if one is looking for a computationally cheap surrogate rather than trying to capture as faithfully as possible the system’s true dynamics. Consider a reanalysis dataset {xa(ti)}ni=0 , defined for times t0, . . . , t n. 3.1.2 Learning a model error correction using analysis increments 

Another way to use reanalysis is to learn model error corrections to improve an imperfect physics-based model. As noted above, reanalysis generally has less model bias than raw model output, since the model forecast is “nudged” towards the observations, which are usually assumed to be unbiased but noisy. These nudges towards the observations, that is AI( ti) = xa(ti) − xf(ti), are called analysis increments . A simple schematic is shown in Figure 2. The idea of this method [Danforth et al., 2007, Farchi et al., 2021] is to learn systematic patterns in past analysis increments to improve the model, even when observations are not available. The whole analysis increment will not be predictable without the observations, but some patterns might be. 3.1.3 Online methods 

The previous two methods relied on there being a database of past analysis states produced using a physics-based model. These methods are called offline , in the sense that DA does not need to be applied during the training process once this database is available. This is advantageous in terms of computational efficiency, since performing DA repeatedly is typically very expensive. The disadvantage 6of these methods is that analysis/reanalysis does not totally remove the impact of model error, as previously discussed. An alternative class of online methods incorporate data assimilation into the training process [see Bach et al., 2026c, Chapter 8 for an overview]. These methods can be used both for learning a forecast model, learning a model error correction, or learning an unknown model error covariance Q (for more on the latter see Tandeo et al. [2020]). More recently, the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) is testing a system wherein an offline training stage is followed by online tuning with DA [Farchi et al., 2025]. 

# 3.2 Applying data assimilation to a machine learning model 

Once an ML model is trained, in order to use it for forecasting, we must apply DA to it. Ostensibly, this involves replacing M with M ML in any DA algorithm, such as those discussed in Section 2. If the ML model is a close enough approximation to the true system dynamics, i.e., M ML ≈ M , then it can be shown that the estimated trajectory will stay close to the hypothetical trajectory estimated using the true dynamics. Furthermore, even if the ML model is unstable for long rollouts, a common problem, frequent DA can prevent blow-up [Adrian et al., 2025]. See Chapter 6 in Bach et al. [2026c] for further theoretical results. In practice, it is important for DA that the ML model accurately capture the error growth char-acteristics of the system, as well as the covariance structure between variables. Platt et al. [2023] consider enforcing the former during the training process. Application of DA to global ML atmospheric models has thus far yielded mixed results. In experi-ments with synthetic observations, Adrian et al. [2025], Li et al. [2024], Kotsuki et al. [2025] achieved reasonable results, although it was found that the ML models did not always accurately capture error covariances. Experiments with real surface pressure observations proved less successful, with poor es-timation of cross-variable covariances [Slivinski et al., 2025]. Other works with more simplified models have also demonstrated the potential of DA with ML forecast models [Chattopadhyay et al., 2022, Penny et al., 2022]. The use of ML forecast models in DA has several potential advantages compared to the use of physics-based models. Reduced computational expense is an attractive prospect, since DA is among the most computationally expensive tasks in operational weather forecasting centres. Additionally, ML forecast models may offer other advantages for variational and ensemble methods, as we now outline. A potential advantage of using ML models with 4DVar is that, since these models are usually implemented using ML libraries that allow for automatic differentiation, the linear tangent model can be obtained relatively easily, or the 4DVar cost function differentiated directly [Tian et al., 2026, Solvik et al., 2025]. However, Tian et al. [2026] notes that the linear tangent model of ML models can exhibit unphysical noise that may cause robustness issues. A potential appeal of using ML models for ensemble DA, on the other hand, is that due to their being typically computationally cheaper to run once trained, one can potentially afford much larger ensembles than otherwise feasible with physics-based models. Such large ensembles, if they accurately 7captured the time-evolving probability distribution of the system, could potentially improve ensemble DA. Methods that combine physics-based with ML ensembles have also been proposed [Chattopadhyay et al., 2023, Bach and Ghil, 2023, Bach et al., 2024, Voort et al., 2025].

Recall that autoencoders (see Chapter 1) learn a low-dimensional latent space for a given dataset. An active area of research has been to perform DA in such a latent space [Melinc and Zaplotnik, 2024, Pasmans et al., 2025], for high-dimensional systems such as the atmosphere. One might want to assimilate in the latent space for two reasons: 1. DA is costly in high dimensions. If the assimilation step can be performed in a lower-dimensional latent space, the computational cost can be significantly reduced. 2. DA methods, as discussed in Section 2, typically make Gaussian assumptions. Variational autoen-coders impose Gaussian structure on the latent space, so Gaussian DA methods can potentially be more accurately applied here than in a non-Gaussian full space. Suppose one has trained an autoencoder on time-series of the system of choice, resulting in an encoder E and decoder D. Then, to perform assimilation in the latent space at time ti, we compute the latent space representation of the forecast as 

zf(ti) = E(xf(ti)) .

For an ensemble forecast, the encoder would be applied to each ensemble member, obtaining a latent space ensemble {zf 

> j

(ti)}mj=1 . We then define the latent space observation operator to be 

H′ = H ◦ D;that is, to get from a point in the latent space to one in the observation space, one first applies the decoder to return to the full space, and then the observation operator. Now, one can apply a DA algorithm to the latent space forecast zf(ti) and observation y(ti), with observation operator H′, to obtain the latent space analysis za(ti). To obtain a full space analysis one can apply the decoder,

We have not yet addressed how to obtain the latent space forecast covariance matrix. In the case of the EnKF, this can be directly estimated from the latent space ensemble. For 3DVar, the background covariance can be estimated from consecutive latent space states [Melinc and Zaplotnik, 2024]. 

# 3.5 Generative data assimilation 

We have thus far focused on the problem of state estimation: obtaining a good point estimate of the system state at a given time. Filtering, as discussed in Section 2, is the problem of instead estimating a probability distribution over possible states. Specifically, one wants to estimate the 

filtering distribution ,

P(xt(ti)|{ y(tj )ij=0 }); that is, the conditional probability of the state at ti given all the observations from time t0 to time ti.An emerging area of research applying ML to DA is to approximately sample this distribution using generative ML. Specifically, several approaches have used diffusion models as priors for generative DA [Rozet and Louppe, 2023, Bao et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2025]. Diffusion models are discussed in Chapter 6. One approach is to first obtain a generative model that can approximately sample from the probabil-ity distribution P(xt) by training on either simulated time-series or reanalysis. Once this unconditional model is trained, there are various approaches to sample from the approximate distribution conditioned on observations. While such methods seem promising, the issue of how to perform conditional sampling with diffusion models in a principled and tractable way is a difficult one [Zhao et al., 2025]. Methods for conditioning that have been used for generative DA include diffusion posterior sampling [Rozet and Louppe, 2023] and CoPaint [Yang et al., 2025]. Another approach is to directly train a conditional diffusion model, with the disadvantage that the trained model is specific to the observation network used in training. [2024], have thus far not reached the skill of leading physics-based models nor ML models trained on reanalysis.
