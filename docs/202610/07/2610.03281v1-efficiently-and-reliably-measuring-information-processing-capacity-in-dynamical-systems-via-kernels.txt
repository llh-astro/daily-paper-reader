## Efficiently and Reliably Measuring Information Processing Capacity in Dynamical Systems via Kernels 

Alessio Benavoli, ∗ Felix Binder, † and Cangxiong Chen ‡

Trinity College Dublin, Dublin, Ireland. 

(Dated: October 5, 2026) Information Processing Capacity (IPC) is a powerful system-agnostic metric for quantifying a dynamical system’s computational capability and is widely used in quantum reservoir computing. However, standard IPC suffers from severe computational limitations. Computing IPC involves estimating how well the system can reconstruct, via linear regression, an (in principle) infinite family of orthogonal polynomial functions of delayed inputs. In practice, this evaluation must be made finite, so we need only to consider polynomials up to a chosen maximum degree. Moreover, the computational complexity for computing IPC grows combinatorially with the considered maximum degree and delay. Here, we introduce Kernel-IPC (KIPC), a scalable framework that uses kernel methods to compute the total IPC without explicitly enumerating basis functions. This allows us to account for the full (infinite) basis (without degree truncation). We also provide a null-hypothesis test for statistically comparing the KIPC of two dynamical systems. We validate KIPC on an echo state network and demonstrate its utility for quantum reservoir computing on a disordered spin chain. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

The Information Processing Capacity (IPC) is asystem-agnostic metric to quantify the ability of a dy-namical system to perform arbitrary transformations on an input signal. Originally proposed in [1], the evaluation of the processing capacity is based on discrete inputs and discrete system states. The idea is brilliant and simple. Assuming the dynamical system has the fading memory property, [1] shows that the input-output map belongs to a fading memory Hilbert space. Therefore, since any function in this space can be represented as a linear com-bination of its basis functions, a way to evaluate the IPC of a system is to assess its ability to reconstruct elements of this basis via linear regression from its internal states. Thus, the IPC can be interpreted as the total number of linearly independent functions of the input signal that the system can compute. This provides a powerful frame-work for comparing the IPC of different dynamical sys-tems. As a system-agnostic metric, it offers a more prin-cipled alternative for quantifying informational capacity than evaluating performance across a suite of standard benchmark tasks (e.g., NARMA, delay line, etc.). In [1] it is shown that the elements of this basis can be chosen as orthogonal polynomials (e.g., Legendre polyno-mials) of delayed input signals. The reason is related to the Volterra-series representation of fading-memory sys-tems [2]. Extensions of IPC have been discussed in [3–

Instead, [4] derives a connection between the IPC and the performance of the dynamical system acting as a reservoir to solve a specific benchmark task. Finally, [5] introduces the innovation capacity to extend IPC to deal with a noisy reservoir. IPC is particularly fundamental in quantum reservoir computing (QRC). Because QRC is a rapidly evolving field actively exploring diverse hardware platforms [6– 8] and architectural designs, IPC provides a standard-ised task-independent framework to systematically eval-uate and compare how different physical dynamics and reservoir configurations convert quantum resources into functional computational capacity. Indeed, a substantial body of literature on QRC relies extensively on IPC to evaluate and compare: (i) different QRC architectures; (ii) alternative strategies for embedding the input signal into quantum states; (iii) different read-out approaches; (iv) the impact of measurement back-action: [9–31] IPC is powerful but also has some serious drawbacks .The IPC cannot be computed for all elements of the ba-sis of the Hilbert space of fading-memory functions, as this basis is infinite-dimensional. To keep the computa-tion feasible, IPC must be truncated by setting a max-imum degree and delay for the evaluating polynomials (but the number of such basis functions grows combina-torially with the polynomial degree and the maximum de-lay). Consequently, standard IPC calculations are prac-tically limited to systems with lower nonlinearity and de-lays. This issue is further exacerbated by standard early-stopping heuristics, which halt calculations of IPC terms (which are summed to compute the total IPC) within a given degree once consecutive terms yield negligible ca-pacity. Because the calculation of IPC terms is performed in an arbitrary order (typically graded-lexicographical or-der), early stopping prematurely skips later terms which, despite coming later in the lexicographical order, may be arXiv:2610.03281v1 [quant-ph] 2 Oct 2026 2easier for the system to reconstruct and, therefore, con-tribute to the total IPC. Therefore, in papers using IPC, the computation of total IPC is often accompanied by a warning stating that the reported total-IPC may be underestimated due to truncation, see for instance [29, Appendix B]. In this paper, we address this issue by showing how kernels can be used to compute the total IPC without explicitly enumerating all basis functions, and so avoid-ing the drawbacks discussed above. This enables us to compute the IPC without imposing an upper bound on the polynomial degree and, consequently, to capture all possible nonlinearities. As a result, we obtain an efficient and scalable approach for estimating the IPC of highly nonlinear dynamical systems, which we call Kernel-IPC (KIPC). Additionally, we provide a way to statistically compare the KIPC of two dynamical systems using a sin-gle null-hypothesis test. This solves another prominent issue with the standard IPC, which instead requires a separate hypothesis test for each element of the basis function, which can lead to a high false discovery rate due to spurious correlations. We demonstrated these im-provements in practice using an Echo State Network as a test-bed dynamical system. Finally, we showed how the efficiency and reliability of KIPC can be leveraged to drive research in QRC, focusing specifically on a disor-dered quantum spin chain reservoir.

In [1, Supplementary, Sec. 3.3], this restriction is jus-tified by the following implicit assumption.

In [1, Supplementary, Sec. 3.3], the authors moti-vate Assumption 1 on the basis of two empirical observations: (1) in the systems considered experi-mentally, capacity generally decreases as the poly-nomial degree increases; and (2) for physical sys-tems with fading memory, capacity decreases, on average, as the delay increases.

exhibits only low-degree nonlinearities, while Sb

is highly nonlinear (and so it performs poorly on linear memory tasks, e.g., fitting the elements of the basis B1). The total information capacity C tot 

cannot distinguish between these two reservoirs, although it would be natural (also in view of Assumption 1) to prefer the system Sa.

Issue 3: Due to the use of finite statistics (i.e., a finite time series of length nT ) which introduces spuri-ous correlations, capacities cannot be estimated re-liably. To mitigate the effects of these numerical errors, which affect the value of the total capacity when τmax and d are large, [1] suggest perform-ing hypothesis testing to remove IPC terms with small values. Under an independence assumption (which is, in fact, violated [1]), they show that the total capacity is a sum of χ2-distributed vari-ables. Consequently, they define a threshold r via 

P (χ2 > r ) = p with p = 0 .0001 and then use the threshold r to eliminate all small IPC terms (assuming they are due to spurious correlations). They then double this threshold to account for the fact that the total IPC is not the sum of indepen-dent χ2 terms. This procedure is heuristic and, more importantly, does not adjust for multiple test-ing (Bonferroni-like corrections) – the number of tests becomes large when τmax and d are large. This means that the total IPC will mostly of the case be inflated due to spurious correlations when τmax and 

d are large. 

Issue 4: In the computation of total IPC, the choice of the particular orthogonal basis of polynomial is de-termined by the distribution of u, for example, Leg-endre polynomials are used if u is uniformly dis-tributed in [ −1, 1]. However, the distribution of u

may be non-standard and unknown and, therefore, the IPC is not optimally calculable with the formu-las (2).

> V. CONCLUSIONS

In this work, we have introduced a kernel-based ap-proach, the kernel-based information processing capac-ity (KIPC) to measure the information processing ca-pacity metric of a dynamical system. Compared to the total-IPC metric introduced by Dambre et al. [1], our approach resolves several key limitations. First, we can compute the information capacity without imposing an upper bound on the polynomial degree, therefore ac-counting for all possible non-linearities. Moreover, this computation can be performed efficiently. Through a permutation-based test, we also show that we can sta-tistically compare the KIPC of two dynamical systems using a single null-hypothesis test. This solves another prominent issue with the standard IPC, which instead re-quires a separate test for each element of the basis func-tion. We demonstrated these improvements in practice using an Echo State Network as a test-bed dynamical system. Finally, we showed how the efficiency and relia-bility of KIPC can be leveraged to drive research in quan-tum reservoir computing (QRC), focusing specifically on a disordered quantum spin chain reservoir. While KIPC confirmed several previously known properties regarding the IPC for this QRC architecture, it also allowed us to discover an interesting phenomenon: a misalignment be-tween the maximum of the linear KIPC component and the nonlinear KIPC components. This finding warrants further investigation. As future work, we plan to replace the permutation testing we use for determining the distribution of the null-hypothesis with a parametric approximation of the null distribution computed analytically from its moments and, therefore, requiring only a small computational overhead compared to KIPC (permutation testing in-stead requires computing the KIPC nsamples times). This is similar to the approach used in kernel-based indepen-dence testing, where a Gamma distribution is employed to approximate the null distribution [38]. Another valu-able improvement would be to replace the null-hypothesis significance test with a Bayesian framework to more eas-ily distinguish between statistical and practical signifi-cance, similar to the approach in [42].
