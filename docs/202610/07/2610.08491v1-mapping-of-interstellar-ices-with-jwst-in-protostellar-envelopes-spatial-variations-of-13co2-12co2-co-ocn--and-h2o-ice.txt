ABSTRACT 

Context. The composition of planets and their atmospheres is largely determined by the environment in which the planet accretes material. This environment is pre-determined during early stages when the protostar is still forming and impacting the complexity of the material in the envelope. Charting the structure of protostellar envelopes is therefore crucial for understanding how the chemistry is linked to the physical processes occurring in stellar nurseries, and JWST o ff ers a unique opportunity to perform spatial mapping at sub-arcsec resolution. 

Aims. We aim to explore spatial variations of the main ice constituents in the envelope of the low-mass protostar L1527 and the high-mass protostar IRAS 20126 +4104 using JWST observations. 

Methods. We use JWST NIRSpec IFU observations to extract spectra at each pixel in the IFU of 13 CO 2,12 CO 2, CO, OCN − and H 2O ice features. We create optical depth and spatial abundance maps to study the distribution of the ices and use the 13 CO 2 ice feature, a known thermal tracer, to identify regions of high temperatures where the ices are thermally processed. 

Results. Our findings show that the distribution of ices in both L1527 and IRAS 20126 +4104 is not uniform, with both envelopes showing localized temperature zones and substructures. In L1527, CO 2 ice segregation and OCN − enhancement with respect to CO 2 are observed in the warm central disk lane close to the protostar. In IRAS 20126 +4104, the warm temperature zones are observed mostly towards the shocked region in the NW outflow where dense material is interacting with the precessing jet as well as close to the central source. The CO 2 ice is segregated at these locations and we observe sublimation of CO ice, as well as strong gaseous CO rotation vibrational lines, especially in the shocked NW outflow. Close to the central protostar we see an additional enhancement of OCN − with respect to CO 2 that also points towards elevated temperatures and crystalline H 2O ice is also detected at a few locations that coincide with shock knots in the outflow. 

Conclusions. In both low mass and high mass protostar the findings indicate that the distribution of ices and their physical structure are linked to the temperature structure of the envelopes. The envelope of IRAS 20126 shows a more complex structure, where interacting material results in localized regions of high temperatures and denser regions that harbor cooler ices. L1527 in contrast appears to be a more structured system with heated warm ices observed mostly towards the central disk regions. 

Key words. Astrochemistry, Protostars, Interstellar Ices, Infrared Spectroscopy 

1. Introduction 

The creation of worlds involves an intricate sequence of events, each governed by numerous processes that shape the chemi-cal makeup and structure of the planetary systems (Mumma & Charnley 2011; Öberg & Bergin 2021). During the prestellar and protostellar stage, the initial ingredients of planetary matter are established with the formation of interstellar ices, the chemical seeds of more complex chemistry (Herbst & van Dishoeck 2009; Caselli & Ceccarelli 2012; Boogert et al. 2015). Physicochemi-cal processes triggered by the accreting system can subsequently transform this initial composition. Indeed, radiation and heating from the infant star as well as shocks from jets and outflows can create temperature gradients and localized warm regions in the envelope and disk. This in turn can impact the distribution and chemical complexity of the material (Evans 1999; Schöier et al. 2002; Dunham et al. 2014; Murillo et al. 2018; Tobin & Sheehan 2024). The young system as a whole can also be a ff ected by its local environment. Massive stars, for instance, are known to form in bustling neighborhoods that are subjected to strong UV-fields and high-energy particles that easily penetrate clouds and circumstellar medium, a ff ecting the chemistry of the envelope and disk. In addition, these re-gions have a high star population density, which often leads to young stellar companions that interact dynamically (Zinnecker & Yorke 2007; Sana et al. 2012; Duchêne & Kraus 2013; Motte et al. 2018), thus having additional consequences on both the physical structure and chemistry of the individual systems. In contrast, the conditions in low-mass stellar nurseries are gener-ally less violent than their high-mass counterparts (Beuther et al. 2025), which could lead to di ff erences in the chemical structure of their envelopes. This raises two questions: to what extent is the chemistry a ff ected by these protostellar processes and are there observable di ff erences in the distribution of matter inside the envelopes of these systems? Previous observational facilities have enabled detailed char-acterization of the molecular inventory in these young systems in both gas- and ice-phase (Jørgensen et al. 2020; van’t Ho ff &Bergner 2026; Ceccarelli et al. 2023; Rocha et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2024). Gas-phase observations and modeling studies in particular have shed light on the morphology and kinematics of protostellar systems that directly a ff ect circumstellar matter. In addition, these studies were successful in extracting temper-ature profiles and establishing the location of snowlines in var-ious protostellar envelopes (Jørgensen et al. 2002, 2011; van ’t

Ho ff et al. 2018a; Hsieh et al. 2019). In the solid state, infrared observatories have shown that protostellar systems are teeming with ices that vary in relative abundance depending on the line of sight (Gibb et al. 2004; Öberg et al. 2011; Boogert et al. 2015). In addition, their structure and composition can be altered by thermal processes such as segregation and sublimation of the highly volatile species (Pontoppidan et al. 2008; Öberg et al. 2011; Brunken et al. 2025). Although these studies collectively provide a multifaceted view of the overall architecture of protostellar systems and the available material for planet assembly, the link between the enve-lope structure and the spatial distribution of the numerous chem-ical species remains largely unexplored. This is especially true for interstellar ices, where spatial mapping in clouds and pro-tostellar envelopes has only recently become feasible with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) at high (subarcsec) angu-lar resolution and spectral (Tyagi et al. 2025; Petrashkevich et al. 2026). Before JWST, ice mapping studies were mainly focused on infrared sources behind dark clouds and envelopes and were limited to a number of species such as CO and CH 3OH that could be observed from the ground (Pontoppidan et al. 2004; Pontop-pidan 2006; Sonnentrucker et al. 2008). The new technology provided by the JWST NIRSpec Inte-gral Field Unit (IFU) now o ff ers an opportunity to investigate the location and physical structure of ices in the envelopes around protostars spanning a range of masses and luminosities (Yang et al. 2022; Brunken et al. 2024a; Slavicinska et al. 2024; Nazari et al. 2024). The spatial distribution and ice abundances of H 2O, CO and CO 2, the main ice constituents, as well as ionic species such as OCN − can be determined for the first time in young sys-tems and be directly connected to the physical structure of the envelope. Moreover, the high spectral resolution allows us to study variations in ice band profiles and hunt for thermal indi-cators (Brunken et al. 2024a, 2025), enabling the identification of temperature zones in protostellar envelopes from a new per-spective. Segregation of CO 2 ice, sublimation of CO ice, and enhancement of OCN − ice with respect to other species can all point to warmer environments (Boogert et al. 2000; Pontoppidan et al. 2008; Brunken et al. 2025; Nazari et al. 2026). By combin-ing these new ice studies with previous findings from gas-phase observations, we can obtain a comprehensive view of the struc-tures and chemical abundance in protostellar envelopes, which are important for our understanding of planet formation since the planet composition is determined by the material it accretes and, therefore, its formation location. In this work, we present JWST NIRSpec IFU spatial maps of the low-mass protostar L1527 (L = 3 L ⊙, M = 0.45 M ⊙) and the high-mass protostar IRAS 20126 +4104 (hereafter IRAS 20126) (L = 10 4 L⊙, M = 12 M ⊙ ) as part of the JOYS + (Beuther et al. 2023; van Dishoeck 2025) and Investigating Protostellar Accre-tion (IPA) collaborations (Federman et al. 2024; Narang et al. 2024; Rubinstein et al. 2024) (see also Appendix A for additional source properties). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first ice mapping study of a high-mass protostar. The observations in-clude the H 2O stretching mode (3 μm), the 12 CO 2 ν3 stretching mode (4.27 μm), the 13 CO 2 ν3 stretching mode (4.39 μm), the OCN − stretching mode (4.60 μm) and the 12 CO stretching mode (4.67 μm) (Figure 1 right Panel). The analysis is focused on NIR-Spec observations because they provide extended scattered light emission against which the ice absorption can be observed, un-like the dust thermal emission that is obtained with MIRI which is more compact. The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2 we present the observations and methods. In Section 3 we pro-vide the analysis of the maps and the results are discussed in Section 4. Finally, in Section 5 we provide a summary and the concluding remarks. 

2. Observations and methods 

> 2.1. Observations

The NIRSpec and PRISM observations of L1527 were taken as part of the JOYS + Cycle 1 GO program (PI: E.F. van Dishoeck, ID: 1960) and the data consist of NIRSpec (2.87 - 5 μm) and PRISM spectra observed using the G395H modes ( R = λ /∆ λ

= 2700). The IRAS 20126 data were taken as part of the IPA Cycle 1 GO program (PI: T.Megeath, ID: 1802) using the NIR-Spec G395M mode ( R = λ /∆ λ = 1000). Both settings use the integral field unit. Further observational details, data reduction methods, information on the spectral extractions, source proper-ties and coordinates are provided in Section 2.2, Table A.1, and in references (Brunken et al. 2024a,b; van Gelder et al. 2024; Fe-derman et al. 2024; Rubinstein et al. 2024; Narang et al. 2024; van Dishoeck 2025; Devaraj et al. 2026). 

> 2.2. Spectral extractions

To obtain spectral information, we utilize a python script that loops over each pixel in the IFU and extracts a spectrum be-tween 2.87 - 5.27 μm. Each pixel is 0.1 × 0.1 arcsecond and we use this spatial information to convert the maps to astronomical unit scales assuming distances of 142 and 1550 pc for L1517 and IRAS 20126, respectively (Tobin et al. 2012; Cesaroni et al. 2023). For both L1527 and IRAS 20126 the pixels have good S/N and undersampling does not appear to be an issue. In fact, the ice features extracted on a pixel-by-pixel basis look similar to the ice features obtained from 3 λ/D cone aperture extractions centered on the infrared source (Brunken et al. 2024a,b, 2025). The signal does decrease towards the outermost pixels and we therefore refrain from doing any analysis with pixels towards the edge of the IFU field of view.

> 2.4. Spatial maps

Peak optical depth maps are created for 13 CO 2, OCN − and CO by determining the optical depths for each pixel at the peak wave-lengths 4.38 μm, 4.60 μm, and 4.67 μm, respectively. The col-umn densities maps are subsequently calculated by integrating under the ice features and using N = R τν dν/A, where R τν dν is the integrated optical depths under the absorption feature, and A

is the corresponding band strength of the vibrational mode. For 13 CO 2, CO, and OCN − we use the band strengths 9.98 

× 10 −17 , 1.1 × 10 −17 and 1.5 × 10 −16 cm molecule −1, respec-tively, taken from Hudson et al. (2025); Gerakines et al. (2023, 2025). These band newly measured band strengths di ff er from those used in Brunken et al. (2024b) who calculated column den-sities with band strengths taken from Gerakines et al. (1995) and van Broekhuizen et al. (2005). The new band strengths di ff er from the previously used one by a factor 1.15, 1.27 and 0.87 for 13 CO 2, 12 CO, and OCN −, respectively. For details on the error budget we refer the reader to Brunken et al. (2024b) and Brunken et al. (2025). Since the 4 μm spectral region is polluted by gaseous CO lines, we opted to fit a gaussian curve over the OCN − ice band to obtain the column densities. We allow the FWHM to vary between 23 to 26 cm −1 and set the boundary for the peak position between 2165.60 and 2165.64 cm −1 before fitting the ice feature at each pixel using a simple optimization routine. From the column density maps, we produce CO 2/CO, CO 2/OCN − and OCN −/CO ratio maps. Finally, we study the spatial variation of the short-wavelength pure 13 CO 2 peak at 4.38 μm with respect to the long-wavelength peak at 4.39 μmof 13 CO 2 by taking the maximum optical depth at the two fixed positions and computing the ratio. The maps serve as an indica-tion of the segregation of CO 2 ice (Boogert et al. 2000; Brunken et al. 2024a, 2025). 

3. Analysis 

> 3.1. L1527

The 3.8 μm continuum map of L1527 is presented in Figure 1. The dark lane shows the near edge-on disk in which the cen-tral protostar is enshrouded. Emission from the central source is seen in scattered light illuminating the outflow cavities that stretch in the east and west directions (Tobin et al. 2008; van’t Ho ff et al. 2023), seen also in mid-infrared atomic and molec-ular lines (Drechsler et al. 2026; Devaraj et al. 2026). Example spectra at various positions in the envelope are shown on optical depth scale from pixel positions A to G for 13 CO 2, OCN − and CO ice. We ran a simple 1D spherical envelope radiative transfer model using RADMC-3D (Dullemond et al. 2012) to obtain the 40 K, 60 K, and 100 K lines. The parameters used in the mod-els are provided in Table A.1. We note that although both L1527 and IRAS 20126 have disks (Tobin et al. 2012; Cesaroni et al. 2025), this is not included in the model. As shown by Sturm et al. (2023), disks can cause scattering e ff ects that a ff ect the path of the photon, but given the large mass of the envelope dur-ing these early stages, the photon travels a longer path through the envelope from the point of last scattering, and thus most of the ice absorption is coming from the envelope rather than the disk. The exclusion of disks and outflow cavities in our models also results in spherical contours that are not entirely represen-tative of the envelope and disk scenario. For the purpose of this study however, they serve as an approximation of the various temperature zones and are su ffi cient to support the observations.

3.1.3. OCN −

In Figures 2 and A.1 panels III we present the column density and peak optical depth maps of the 4.60 μm OCN − ice feature. In the final row of Figure 4 we present the spectra of the 3 μmH2O ice feature on flux scale taken at a number of positions in the envelope. 4. Discussion 

The results in Section 3 indicate that there is a significant vari-ation in the distribution of CO 2, CO, H 2O and OCN − ices in the envelopes of both L1527 and IRAS20126. The temperature structure of these two envelopes is well documented in the liter-ature, which is imperative for interpreting these findings, since ices are highly susceptible to their local environment. Ices form during the cloud stage through the adhesion of atoms and small molecules to the surfaces of cold dust grains, after which they evolve in tandem with the protostellar system. Hydrogen-rich molecules such as H 2O, NH 3 and CH 4 are the first to form due to the high abundance of hydrogen atoms dur-ing the translucent cloud stage (Öberg et al. 2011; Boogert et al. 2015). CO 2 is also produced in this H 2O-dominated ice via the HO-CO complex (Oba et al. 2010; Ioppolo et al. 2011; Garrod & Pauly 2011). This epoch is closely followed by the heavy freeze-out stage, where CO-dominated ices grow rapidly to form an apolar ice mantle (Pontoppidan 2006). A fraction of this CO ice is converted into CO 2, and hydrogenation of CO also leads to the formation of larger molecules such as CH 3OH (Cuppen et al. 2024). The result is a stratified ice structure, where the molecules are intimately mixed in the di ff erent ice layers. Naturally, when these ices are processed by either thermal or UV radiation, the di ff erent species will react according to their intrinsic properties or undergo further chemical reactions to form other molecules. The highly volatile CO ice, for instance, will partially desorb from the apolar ice mantle. Further heating will cause restructuring of the molecular bonds and segregation of molecules such as CO 2, which produces inclusions of pure CO 2

ice within the mantle, as well as crystallization of the ices. These structural changes are then reflected in the infrared ice absorption bands with CO 2 showing a double peak profile at 2.70 μm , 4.39 μm and 15.2 μm (Boogert et al. 2000; Pontoppi-dan et al. 2008; Poteet et al. 2013; Brunken et al. 2024a, 2025) and crystalline water showing a characteristic peaked profile at 3 μm (Fraser et al. 2001). These unique spectral features are sig-natures of ice processing and can o ff er vital clues about the local environment. In the following sections, we examine the struc-tural di ff erence between L1527 and IRAS 20126 and explore a possible link between the architecture of the two systems and the ongoing ice chemistry. 4.1. Ice distribution in L1527 

L1527 is a young Class 0 protostar located at a distance of about 142 pc (Torres et al. 2007; Tobin et al. 2012). The central proto-star has a luminosity between 2 - 3 L ⊙ (Tobin et al. 2008; Kris-tensen et al. 2012) and is enshrouded in a near edge-on disk ( i

= 85 ◦) that is undergoing Keplerian motion (Tobin et al. 2008; Ohashi et al. 2014; van’t Ho ff et al. 2023). The envelope con-tains about 1 M ⊙ of material, a fraction of which is currently accreting onto the protostar. Finally, a bipolar outflow cavities are observed in the form of reflection nebulae on the east and west of the central source, which are carving out large cavities in the envelope material (Tobin et al. 2008, 2012). Due to its close proximity, L1527 o ff ers an ideal setting for investigating the physical structure of young low mass proto-stars. A study by Tobin et al. (2012), showed clear asymmetries and variability in the outflow cavities where one lobe appears to be brighter than the other, and the authors attributed this to a probable misalignment of the central disk. More recent stud-ies by Drechsler et al. (2026) and Devaraj et al. (2026) revealed asymmetric atomic emission coming from the outflow, with an enhancement observed in the east lobe, which points to an asym-metric mass accretion process. This asymmetry is also observed in Figure 1 where the eastern outflow appears brighter in the in-tensity map. Devaraj et al. (2026) also reported the presence of a high velocity ionized jet coming from Detailed modeling of the temperature structure was also conducted by van ’t Ho ff et al. (2018b) and van’t Ho ff et al. (2023) who derived Tdisk ∼ 20 -40 K. The authors showed that 13 CO and C 18 O gas emission are detected throughout the disk, while N 2D+, which is destroyed by CO, is not detected, thus concluding that the disk is warm enough to prevent CO from freezing out. In addition to various other molecular tracers, they also detect SiO emission west of the central source, alluding to the presence of a jet. In Figure 7 we present an illustrated schematic of the envelope around L1527. The main gas tracers and the main ice findings of the study are highlighted at di ff erent locations in the envelope. The ice maps in Figure 2 imply a higher abundance of ices in the disk region and overall less ices towards the outflow cav-ities, as expected. More importantly, the appearance of the pure CO 2 ice feature at 4.38 μm shows that CO 2 is segregating in the regions close to the disk lane. The peak grows towards the cen-ter of the envelope and appears to trace the cavity walls on both sides of the disk (Figure 2 panel IV). This indicates that these regions have high temperatures that prompt ice heating, and is consistent with the location where van’t Ho ff et al. (2023) de-tects warm ( ≥ 60 K) H 2CO 3 2,2 - 2 2,1 emission. Although the gas and dust do not necessarily have to be coupled, in these dense regions it can be assumed that the warm temperatures measured in the gas phase translate into a warm region overall that heats the ices. In fact, Figure 1 shows that the location where the pure CO 2 peak becomes prominent falls around the 60 K snowline, the typical temperature ranges where CO 2 would normally seg-regate (Ehrenfreund et al. 1998). The OCN − ice column densities also appear to be enhanced in the disk lane (Figure 2 panel III). Figure 2 panel IV shows high segregation of CO 2 ice at these locations, indicating that ob-served enhancement of OCN − could be due to elevated temper-atures. This is consistent with the study by Nazari et al. (2026) who showed that the OCN − ice feature can be enhanced up to a factor 2 - 3 in warmer environments (see also van Broekhuizen et al. (2005)). Finally, Figure 2 panel II indicate an opposite trend for CO where the CO ice appears to be largely present in the cavity walls but is deficient in the disk. Although the warm disk scenario presented by (van’t Ho ff et al. 2023) could explain the lack of CO ices in the disk lane, we must also consider the weak continuum in these regions, as mentioned in Section 3 that would cause the CO ice band to hit the noise level (Figure 1). There-fore, while we do not completely discard the possibility that the lower CO ice column densities in the disk lane are due to ice desorption, we also cannot say conclusively that this is the only reason given the weak signal in this region. 4.2. Ice distribution in IRAS 20126 

IRAS 20126 is a known Class 0 high mass protostar that has been studied in both gas and ice phase (Cesaroni et al. 1997, 1999; Federman et al. 2024; Brunken et al. 2024a; Slavicinska et al. 2024; Brunken et al. 2025). The system is located at 1.6 kpc (Moscadelli et al. 2011) and has a luminosity of 10 4 L⊙ (Cesa-roni et al. 2023). The central star is embedded in a Keplerian disk (Cesaroni et al. 2005) that together with the protostar drive pow-erful molecular outflow. Two jets have been observed in SiO, H 2

and CO emission expanding in the NW and SE directions and their morphology suggests that they could be undergoing pre-cession (Shepherd et al. 2000; Cesaroni et al. 2005; Lebrón et al. 2006; Caratti o Garatti et al. 2008). [Fe II] emission revealed the presence of compact knots and substructures in the outflows (Federman et al. 2024) and two companions are observed north and south of the outflow (Federman et al. 2024, and references  

> Article number, page 9 of 13 A&A proofs: manuscript no. main

therein). Finally, Cesaroni et al. (1997) used C 34 S to show that the disk is embedded in a 40 K molecular clump. Figure 8 shows a schematic of the morphology of the IRAS 20126 system, in-cluding both gas-phase results and the main findings of the ice observations. Warm ices are detected throughout the entire envelope of IRAS 20126. This can be concluded from the presence of the 4.38 μm pure CO 2 peak observed at all locations in the envelope (Figure 4). However, it is possible to distinguish between loca-tions of higher temperature. Figure 5 panel I for example shows that the ices in the southern region of the envelope appear to be enhanced and that the ratio between the short wavelength and long wavelength peak of the 13 CO 2 ice feature is lower in this region compared to every other location in the envelope (Panel N). There is also a lack of gaseous CO forest line at this location. This suggests that the ices are generally less heated at this loca-tion. A dense region of high extinction where the material is gen-erally cooler could be a possible explanation for the colder ices. Another possible explanation is disk-shadowing, where the disk shields the central protostar, casting shadows on the envelope, ultimately resulting in regions with lower temperatures (Murillo et al. 2015). However, to confirm this scenario, observations of deuterated species such as DCO + are needed, since their produc-tion is enhanced under these cooler conditions. The possibility that these colder ices could be originating from the 40 K molecular clump that Cesaroni et al. (1997) ob-served in C 34 S emission was also considered. In the laboratory CO 2 segregation usually occurs between 50 - 90 K (Ehrenfreund et al. 1998; Dartois et al. 1999) but under astrophysical condi-tions the longer time scales mean that thermal e ff ects can take place at lower temperatures (Boogert et al. 2008). At 40 K the ices could therefore be warm enough to begin the process of dis-tillation and segregation, resulting in the double peak 13 CO 2 pro-file, while CO remains partially trapped in the ice mantle along with the less volatile species. As shown in Figure A.3 (right panel) however, the region where we detect the colder ices does not coincide with the location of the molecular clump. The appearance of strong pure CO 2 peaks at 4.38 μm and 4.27 μm in the NW outflow (at for example positions E and F), together with the strong gaseous CO line forest indicate signif-icant ice heating and ice segregation at these locations (Figure 4). We also note the low abundance of CO ice at these loca-tions (Figure 5 panel I) and the enhancement of both CO 2 and OCN − relative to CO in this region (Figure 6 ) that suggest sub-limation of CO ice due to higher temperatures. These elevated temperatures are likely the result of interaction between dense envelope material and the NW jet, which in turn warms the en-vironment and heats the surrounding material. Indeed, Cesaroni et al. (2025) traced the jets in both SiO and H 2CO and showed that the SE H 2CO jet appears to be cooler and less dense, while the NW jet is still passing through denser material. In Figure A.3 (right panel) we show an RGB JWST NIRSpec and ALMA composite image. The DCN map (Cesaroni et al. 2025) traces regions of dense material and the contours show the locations of the NW and SE SiO jets (Cesaroni et al. 2025). It is evident from the DCN map that the NW part of the envelope contains dense material close to where the molecular outflow is observed with NIRSpec and where the SiO jet is located. Furthermore, Shep-herd et al. (2000); Cesaroni et al. (2005); Lebrón et al. (2006); Caratti o Garatti et al. (2008) demonstrate that the jet is precess-ing and curving upward in the NW direction. Therefore, we can conclude that the NW region of the envelope is undergoing sig-nificant disturbance, likely creating a localized region of elevated temperature and possibly radiation that could be heating the ices. Another region of high temperatures is seen close to the cen-tral source as shown in Figure 5 panel IV. The 13 CO 2 is also not only segregated but we also observe enhancement of the OCN −

relative to CO 2 (Figure 6 panel II) which again agrees with the findings in Nazari et al. (2026). Why this same enhancement is not observed in the NW warm region is however unclear. Fi-nally, we detect crystalline water in the east envelope which in-dicates high temperatures at these locations ( > 100 K). Tyagi et al. (2025) also observed crystalline water in the intermediate mass source HOPS 370 in the vicinity of a shock knot, showing that these high temperatures are not uncommon in these massive sources. 

> 4.3. High-mass vs. Low-mass

From the previous sections, we note a few notable di ff erences between the envelopes of the low-mass protostar L1527 and the high-mass protostar IRAS 20126. The geometry of L1527 is simpler compared to IRAS 20126 and the heating of ices appears to occur in a more structured manner compared to the high-mass counterpart. Ice segregation occurs gradually as a fuction of de-creasing radius with a high temperature zone around the disk regions as shown with the thermal tracer 13 CO 2 and the slightly enhanced OCN −. IRAS 20126, on the contrary, has a complex system that consists of outflow and jets that interact with the envelope medium. Heated ices are detected in front of the out-flow cavities with particularly high temperature zones observed where the jet and outflow interact with envelope matter. One possible explanation for the heated conditions in the en-velope of IRAS 20126 could also be its variability. Indeed, pre-vious studies by Massi et al. (2023); Szymczak et al. (2024) and Cesaroni (2026) have shown that IRAS 20126 is a vari-able source, likely due to episodic accretion bursts. These pe-riodic luminosity outbursts can flash-heat the ices on very short timescales, contributing to the elevated temperature zones ob-served in the ice maps. Furthermore, source geometry and sub-structures in the envelope can further cause non-uniform heating during outburst which could explain the non-uniform distribu-tion of ices that are seen. Substructures in the envelope can also result in regions of high extinction where colder ices can survive, as seen in the southern regions of the envelope. This non-uniformity observed in IRAS 20126 could also be tied to the natal environments of the massive star, which can dif-fer from that of low-mass systems. Beuther et al. (2025) for in-stance argues that radiation and ionization pressure, occurring al-most uniquely in high-mass star-forming regions, can impact the environment of the star. Moreover, as noted before, the presence of companions (Figure 4) that drive additional outflows could be interacting with the envelope material of IRAS 20126 and al-tering its morphology. Finally, we also consider the age of the two systems where the slightly younger IRAS 202126 (Caratti o Garatti et al. 2008) could still have remnants of the pre-stellar core that responsible for the substructure and non-uniform heat-ing that are being observed. Figures 7 and 8 therefore o ff er a simplified interpretation of the two systems, but more studies are needed to further under-stand the architecture of these envelopes, with ices providing a complementary view of the gas and dust studies. This is espe-cially the case for high-mass sources.

Our main findings suggest that the distribution of ices in protostellar envelopes is directly tied to the temperature profile of the system and that substructures can exist for both low mass and high protostars. 

– The ices in L1527 are enhanced in the central regions where the disk is located, with lower column densities being mea-sured towards the line of sight that traces envelope material in front of the outflow cavities where material is being carved out. 

– Segregated CO 2 warm ice is observed in L1527 in the vicin-ity of the disk and appears to trace the cavity walls. This points towards a warm region that also coincides with the warm disk layers observed in gas-phase studies. Enhance-ment of OCN − relative to both CO and CO 2 is observed at these warmer locations which is consistent with previous studies that showed that OCN − can be enhanced up to a fac-tor 2-3 after its formation. 

– The distribution of ices in the high mass protostar IRAS 20126 is less structured compared to L1527. In addition, segregated ices are seen in the warm region close to the central as source as well as enhancement of OCN −

– A region of relatively colder ices is detected in the southern region of the envelope of IRAS 20126. One possible explana-tion is disk shadowing but observations of deuterated species are needed to confirm this scenario. 

– Notable di ff erences are observed between the envelope struc-ture of the low-mass protostar L1527 and the high-mass pro-tostar IRAS 20126. The there is a gradual heating of the ices in L1527 as a function of distance to the centra source with, higher segregation observed in the central disk region. The geometry and structure of IRAS 20126 on the other hand is far more intricate with warm ices detected in regions where outflow and jets interact with envelope medium, and as a re-sult heat the surroundings. Dense regions of high extinction could be introducing additional substructres in the envelope of IRAS 20126 where ices are better shielded and resulting in colder ices. Our study shows that the physical structure of protostellar envelopes is intimately linked with its ice chemistry. The dis-tribution of ices in particular appear to be co-spatial with the temperature structure of the envelopes. The envelopes around high-mass protostar are far more complex than their low-mass counter parts and further studies focused on mapping are needed to better understand these systems and to search for similarities and trends.
