ABSTRACT 

Gaia will soon provide a census of cold giant planets. However, an astrometric signal contains the superimposed reflex motion of all companions, so planetary signals cannot be treated individually. We therefore investigate Gaia’s planet-detection capabilities for 1-planet and 2-planet systems, with special attention to systems near 2:1 mean-motion resonance. Gaia can detect 2-planet systems, although the lower-S/N planet requires a higher S/N threshold, especially when the outer planet is more massive and near 2:1 commensurability. When fitting a 1-planet model to a circular 2-planet resonant system, the resulting fit shows biases in eccentricity (up to e = 0.8), mass (up to 100%), and inclination (up to 50 deg). The bias is maximised when the planets’ astrometric signatures are similar, since the combined orbital signal deviates most from a single Keplerian orbit. Many of these systems can be identified as having 2 planets using Bayesian model selection. Still, in some cases the second planet is hidden and undetectable due to its S/N, leading to these biases for the outer planet’s orbital parameters. When the outer planet is roughly over three times as massive as the inner planet, orbital approximations allow us to recover the astrometric resoeccentric degeneracy from the bias. When the planets are in dynamical resonance, apsidal precession observed over a long enough baseline (e.g. DR5) can contribute to breaking the degeneracy at short orbital periods. Overall, these biases might significantly affect constraints on planet formation, migration, and evolution models from Gaia population-wide demographics. 

Key words: astrometry – exoplanets – planets and satellites: detection – celestial mechanics 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Since the discovery of the first giant exoplanet (Mayor & Queloz 1995), the known landscape of exoplanet populations has expanded and diversified considerably. The now thousands of confirmed exo-planet systems have been pivotal in informing planet formation, mi-gration and evolution models. Specifically, the Kepler (Borucki et al. 2010; Thompson et al. 2018) and now TESS (Ricker et al. 2015; Guerrero et al. 2021) missions have provided valuable information on transiting planets close to their host star. Longer period planets, however, are much harder to detect through the transit method, due to their low transit probability and long orbital periods. It is hence harder to study these systems on the level of population demograph-ics. Core accretion giant planet formation models (e.g. Pollack et al. 1996) predict that giant planets form at distances of around 10 au and migrate inwards. However, we still struggle to constrain the ex-act mechanisms that limit the planet mass once the phase of runaway gas accretion is reached (e.g. Mizuno 1980; Rafikov 2006; Bailey & Zhu 2024). Consequently, the current observed semi-major axis and eccentricity distributions are difficult to explain with formation and evolution models (e.g. Jennings et al. 2018; Bitsch et al. 2020). Observations are thus essential to better inform these models. We also suspect that distant gas giants, analogous to Jupiter and Saturn in mass and semi-major axis, have had a major influence in shaping the form of the early solar system planets (e.g. Tsiganis et al. 2005;

Various detection techniques cover complementary regions of mass-period parameter space. For example, microlensing covers the large orbital separation detection space. Only a limited number of systems ( ∼ 200) have been observed so far (Mroz & Poleski 2024), and the demographics from certain of these observations indicate a negative power law (Cassan et al. 2012) or a broken power law (Suzuki et al. 2016) distribution in planet-star mass ratio for mi-crolensed planets, with a particularly large occurrence of super-Earths on Jupiter-like orbits (Zang et al. 2025). It is much harder to establish the same for semi-major axis, given that microlensing is only capable of measuring a projected separation. Currently, our best knowledge of cold giant planet demograph-ics comes from radial velocity (RV) surveys such as the California Legacy Survey (Fulton et al. 2021), and the Mayor et al. (2011) survey analysed by Fernandes et al. (2019). These surveys find an increase in giant planet occurrence rate up to a few au, after which they find a tentative decline, with a limited amount of data beyond 5 au. Planets beyond this separation are harder to find due to the longer baselines required to accurately constrain their properties. In agreement with the aforementioned microlensing results, the mass function is found in Fernandes et al. (2019) to be a power law, al-though with a less steep gradient. Current RV samples estimate the mean eccentricity of such cold giant planets beyond the ice line to be around 0.23 (Kane & Wittenmyer 2024; Alqasim et al. 2025). The Gaia mission (Bernstein & Bastian 1995; Casertano et al. 1995; Gaia Collaboration et al. 2016) will provide a step-change in the exploration for the detection-space of cold giant planets. With a final observation baseline of over 10 years, it will accurately constrain the masses and orbital architectures of planets with semi-major axes up to ∼10 au, where they are currently thought to form. Predictive studies such as Perryman et al. (2014) and Lammers & Winn (2025) suggest that Gaia will discover 10 000 to 100 000 planets by the end of the current decade. Astrometric time series of each star are currently unavailable, pending Data Release 4 (DR4) in December 2026, but results from DR3 show that Gaia should reach its predicted precision of a few tens of 𝜇 as. To prepare for the release of DR4, many studies have considered the detectability criteria for a single planet (e.g. Ranalli et al. 2018), but the case of multiple planets is often omitted. Giant planets are not predicted to form in isolation, and young systems such as PDS 70 (Haffert et al. 2019) or HR 8799 (Marois et al. 2010) demonstrate this explicitly. Amongst the limited number of studies that investigate Gaia’s multi-planet detection capabilities, the study by Sozzetti et al. (2001) showed that Gaia can detect mul-tiple planets in the specific system of 𝜐 Andromedæ. Casertano et al. (2008) then generalised the argument and showed that Gaia can suc-cessfully detect most two-planet systems with well separated orbital periods, disregarding any resonances. The study by Wu et al. (2016) suggested limited recovery capabilities for systems near resonance, but greatly simplified the data recovery from Gaia. Unlike transits, where contamination of the signal from other plan-ets in the systems is limited and reasonably well characterised (e.g. Zink et al. 2019), astrometry is a dynamical measurement of the evo-lution of the system photocentre and is hence affected by all planets at all times. On top of completing the giant planet population de-mographics, we need to improve our understanding of multi-planet system architectures. This need is motivated by the observation that many close-in systems are in resonance or near-resonant (Fabrycky et al. 2014; Winn & Fabrycky 2015), with a higher resonant fraction at earlier times (e.g. Dai et al. 2024). Furthermore, resonances could be a natural outcome of giant planet formation and disc dispersal (e.g. Greenfield & Owen 2026). Planets close to resonance (i.e. near com-mensurability) will generate a highly correlated signal. This signal will lead to a fixed repeating photocentre pattern that may likely im-pact the detectability of multi-planet systems (Yahalomi et al. 2025). Similar degeneracies have been found in the RV, where signals are similarly correlated (Anglada-Escudé et al. 2010). Motivated by the need to uncover multi-planet systems in Gaia data, we develop a robust framework for astrometric planet detection, including a Bayesian model preference test to determine whether a 0, 1 or 2 planet model 1 is the best fit to the available data. Previous studies have used Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to fit the astrometric and orbital parameters of the planet (e.g. Lammers & Winn 2025). This also applies to the Gaia DR4 fit-ting pipeline, which uses a periodogram and MCMC joint approach to characterising exoplanets. This method has advantages and dis-advantages. Recasting orbital parameters in Thiele-Innes constants creates a linear problem to solve in the orbital angles, hence making MCMC fitting routines much faster. Nested sampling, however, has its own significant advantages. Firstly, it is more accurate when it comes to model selection. Most previous studies adopt a frequentist approach to model selection, through the Δ𝜒 2 metric (e.g. Perryman et al. 2014), and even using the Bayesian information criterion as in Ranalli et al. (2018) has its limitations. These limitations arise from the nature of the previous metrics, which solely rely on the maximum likelihood to be calcu-lated. If the posterior distribution for the parameters is wide, skewed or even non-unimodal, this information fails to propagate into the preference criterion. Fitting in terms of orbital angles is non-linear, but this non-linearity is unimportant given the flexibility of nested sampling.

3.3 Comparison to previous work and detection thresholds 

We aim to provide a global characterisation of the detectability ca-pabilities of our pipeline. To do this, we first run a parameter space study for the planet mass and semi-major axis (assuming a 1 𝑀 ⊙ star). We generate a grid of 200 masses logarithmically spaced between 0.1 and 10 𝑀 Jup and 200 semi-major axes also logarithmically spaced be-tween 0.1 and 100 au. These systems are face-on, all with 𝑒 = 0.01. We convert each orbit into an astrometric signal, using the recipe described in Section 2.3. We then run our nested sampling pipeline to retrieve each signal, and present the resulting Bayes factor in Fig. 4 for a single planet compared to no planetary signal. We consider that the planet is successfully detected if the Bayes factor provides decisive evidence in favour of the single-planet model. There is a sharp turnaround in detectability at ∼ 3 au and ∼ 5 au (∼ 5 and ∼ 11 years) for the DR4 and DR5 data, respectively, corre-sponding to the time covered by each of the final two Gaia mission data releases. This turnaround point separates the detection space in half by the semi-major axis. On the small semi-major axis side of the detection space we note that the boundary between the detectable and non-detectable regions is very clearly defined, and follows roughly a 𝑚 p ∝ 𝑎 −1p trend for low semi-major axis on the left hand side of the plot. This trend is well understood (e.g. Perryman et al. 2014; Ranalli et al. 2018; Lammers & Winn 2025) and is explained by the inverse relationship between the two quantities in Eq. 1. Matching the simulation data to the previous equation, we can deduce a S/N per astrometric point detectability threshold for DR4 and DR5 of 0.89 and 0.65 respectively, for the case of this specific region of space, and for face-on orbits. To establish a correspondence with the literature, we also recompute the grid study with randomised inclinations and cos 𝐼 sampled uniformly between -1 and 1. When randomising incli-nations, we find the expected increase of S/N detectability thresholds to 1.01 and 0.77 for DR4 and DR5 respectively. In the plots, we note the difference in detection capabilities between DR4 and DR5, as seen from the detectability contours. We also show results for an identical DR5 grid at 100 pc, and as predicted the parameter space is more restricted. We find very similar S/N per astrometric point thresholds between the 100 pc and the 16 pc simulations. On the larger semi-major axis side of the detection space, the planet is harder to detect since Gaia is only able to collect data over a portion of the orbit, and the orbit is more poorly constrained. As the mass gets larger, the signal gets larger and our pipeline is able to confirm planets up to larger separations. This region should be treated with care because as we show in Section 3.4 the inclusion of a linear acceleration term in the model without a planet might be preferred to a single planet fit. At larger semi-major axes, DR5 has a net advantage over DR4, allowing us to detect planets with periods up to twice as long for identical planet masses, and probe down to masses that are at least twice as low. In Fig. 5, we show the fractional error on semi-major axis and mass for both DR4 and DR5 data. We see that for most planets within the detection region and with semi-major axes smaller than 7 au, we are able to constrain the planet’s mass to better than 20%. Beyond 7 au, we lose precision because we only detect a fraction of the orbit. We also notice vertical regions of constant semi-major axis where the error is larger, alternating between overestimation and underestimation of the mass. We relate this to the harmonics of other periodic signals, such as the Earth’s revolution and the Gaia observation cadence, precession rate and rotation, biasing the retrieval at those specific frequencies. Within these strips, higher mass planets are more precisely measured. We bring attention to the shape of the contour and the slim vertical strip at 1 au, suggesting improved detection capabilities. In reality, these detections are false positives, as shown in Holl et al. (2023) and El-Badry et al. (2024). We also consider the error on the semi-major axis in Fig. 5. We note vertical regions of improved or hindered retrieved precision, directly corresponding to the vertical strips in mass highlighted previously.

3.4 A linear acceleration: the case of long period planets 

When considering the results in Fig. 4, we are drawn to the long period planets detected at high significance, especially beyond 10 au. Given the Gaia mission lifetime of roughly 10 years, these planets would be observed for a fraction of their orbit only. For instance, a planet at 30 au around a solar-mass star would only be observed for 6% of its orbit. To confirm that the signal is indeed from a planetary companion, we need to detect an elliptical periodic reflex motion. The upper limit traces the S/N detectability of the planet that is harder to detect (here the innermost planet, in red). The outer (most detectable) planet here acts as a proxy to connect with our conclusions for single planet systems. We want to know whether or not the single planet detection criterion also applies to each individual planet in a 2 planet signal, or if the joint signal requires adjustments to the criterion. We find that the S/N detectability threshold for these planets is still in relatively good agreement with the theory S/N ∼ 1. The less detectable inner planet often requires a higher S/N threshold to detect, due to the additional parameters in the fitting model. The case of planets in 2:1 commensurability with high planet masses is particularly interesting (lower left panel). We see that there is a bump between 1 and 4 years where the 2 planet system is not de-tectable, stopping the red line from providing a meaningful constraint beyond 1 au. In reality, when considering planets in exact dynami-cal resonance as in Section 4.6, we find that the edge fit by the red line completely disappears in favour of all systems being detected as a single planet system. We explain this discrepancy by considering that at short periods, the planets experience more conjunctions over the observational baseline. If they are not in dynamical resonance, the longitude of the conjunction will drift and the signal from each individual planet can be distinguished. In resonance, the longitude of the conjunction librates and hence for each orbit the orbital shape repeats itself with the same features at the same orbital longitudes, hence hiding the observational signature of the inner planet. This is a clear case where the single-planet detection criterion fails in a significant manner. To draw a more robust systematic conclusion for the 2-planet S/N detection criteria, we present 4 histograms in Fig. 11. We present distributions of S/N detection threshold and slope 𝑠 over all 49 con-figurations of mass and period ratios for the 2 planets. We start with the S/N detection threshold. In the upper left panel of Fig. 11, we find that the dominant planet in the signal almost consistently has a S/N detection threshold between 0.5 and 0.75. For the less dominant planet, we find that 0.5 < S/N < 2. This preference is due to the added parametric complexity of a 2-planet model. We can further ask whether there is a criterion that leads to higher or lower detectability thresholds in the lower signal planet. There is no significant dependence of the secondary planet S/N threshold on the mass ratio. However when the planets are closer to 2:1 commensurability, as represented in white in the lower left panel, this S/N threshold is larger. On the contrary, when the planets are away from resonance, as represented in blue in the same panel, the S/N threshold values are lower (<1.5), but still above the threshold value found for the dominant planet. We run an Anderson-Darling test on the two distributions, and find them to differ with a confidence of 90 %, showing weak evidence that resonances play a role in hindering multi-planet detections. Further analysis in the following sections will show stronger numerical and analytical evidence for this hindering effect. The second metric, the inferred slope of the power law, is a proxy for how accurately the model in Eq. 1 represents the S/N thresholds. From the top right plot, we see that this slope for the dominant planet is bounded by −1.2 < 𝑠 < −0.9, close to its theoretical value of -1. For the case of the secondary planet, the bound upper value can deviate up to -0.5. Finally, in the bottom right plot of Fig. 11, we find that, for the less detectable planet, most deviations in the slope are carried by systems with larger outer planet masses. Overall, and unsurprisingly, we find that the lowest S/N planet will be consistently harder to detect. The largest deviations in the detectability criteria are found when the planets are close to 2:1 commensurability, and when the outer planet is more massive than the inner planet. 

4.5 Biases due to multiple planets and resonances 

When fitting a single planet model to a two planet signal, we would ideally expect to recover the planet with the strongest S/N. In prac-tice, the extra signal due to the second, less detectable planet is at risk of contaminating the fit for a single planet. We hence aim to characterise the extent of this contamination on the significance of any single planet detection, and more importantly on the inferred orbital parameters for this planet. We focus on the retrieved orbital parameters obtained by fitting a single-planet model to the two-planet signals in our grid study. Both planets in the data have near zero eccentricity, hence this should also be reflected in the single planet fit. In Fig. 12, however, we find that this is not the case. We select the simulations where the planets are equal in mass, and show the retrieved eccentricity for the 1 planet fit, across the different period ratios for DR5. When the planets are near commensurability for a 2:1 resonance, there exists a range of outer planet periods between 0.6 and 10 years where the inferred eccentricity is consistently found to be greater than 0.6. When the planets are further away from resonance, this feature does indeed seem to become less relevant across the parameter space as a whole. When we consider planets with 1.9:1 or 2.1:1 period ratios, the high eccentricity region lower limit slowly moves to 1 year and for 1.7:1 or 2.3:1 period ratios, this high eccentricity region still persists but is only relevant between periods of 4 and 7 years. We confirm that this feature is indeed a systematic error by looking at the error on the eccentricity, as shown in Fig. 13. We find a strong overlap between the region of high eccentricity previously found and the region of low error, below 0.1. We hence conclude that the posteriors are narrow around these large, unphysical eccentricities, and that the solver finds a valid high eccentricity orbit, hence the planet with the lower S/N does indeed contaminate the single-planet fit in a significant way. A highly eccentric orbit has a large aspect ratio (i.e. semi-major to semi-minor axis ratio), but these 2-planet signals do not present enough elongation along the effective major axis. Hence the fit adds stretching of the aspect ratio through an effective inclination to balance out the overshooting due to the large eccentricities. The eccentricity bias is more sensitive to the time series measurements and hence is more pronounced, but the spatial extent of the orbit must also be considered here, through the inclination. This might provide a route to quantifying the impact of this degeneracy at the population level, as one would naturally expect the inclinations of real exoplanet systems to be isotropically distributed on the sky or, alternatively, to be linked to the spin axis of the star. The results shown in Figs. 16, 18, and 19 raise an important question: when only a single planet can be statistically detected, is it possible or not to determine whether or not its eccentricity is real or is the effect of a second undetectable planet? The answer to this question is far from obvious, and RV follow-ups might help break the degeneracy. However, these follow-ups will require a lot of observational effort. We present here an idea that could help identify potential targets that demonstrate hints of eccentricity-mass-inclination bias. We notice in Fig. 12 that the eccentricity bias drops when the outer planet period is longer than the observing baseline, in this case, the 10 years of DR5. In Fig. 20 we draw the same plot with the eccentricity bias, but this time only considering the time series available in DR4. We find that this outer edge lies at an outer-planet period of approximately 5 years. Then, comparing the eccentricities for all systems with periods between 5 and 10 years, we expect the effective eccentricity from DR5 to be large and hence biased, whereas the effective eccentricity from the DR4 data would be close to 0. This idea relies on the caveat that beyond periods of 5 and 10 years we are still able to accurately detect and characterise planets with DR4 and DR5 respectively. This is possible, as seen in the discussion of single planet fits but will induce large uncertainties in the retrieved orbital parameters. To explore the idea, we run another series of tests. 5 DISCUSSION AND FURTHER WORK 

From the results presented in this study, we see tentative evidence that the planet-planet interactions and apsidal precession resulting from strong secular interactions and enhanced by the resonance play an additional role in the data, and cannot be completely neglected. For systems like GJ 876, we have seen that the baseline of DR5 covers more than one precession cycle for the real system. We hence moti-vate the need for more detailed modelling of such systems. Including an N-body fit or a proxy for planet-planet interactions might allow us to statistically infer the presence of these planet-planet interactions. For short period planets, the pericentre longitude change might lead to along-scan positional deviations on angular scales greater than the Gaia astrometric precision over 5 to 10 years. Additionally, even if the libration amplitude is on the order of degrees, in extreme cases like GJ 876 it is known that its libration period is on the order of a year (Correia et al. 2010) and we can still expect potentially measurable delays in pericentre passage due to this libration, which can cause significant residuals for a two Keplerian model. These variations can motivate the need for an analogous photodynamical modelling tech-nique used in transits (e.g. Carter et al. 2012), which could lead to identifying librations or hints of librations and contribute to iden-tifying true resonant systems amongst the giant planets found by Gaia. However, we want to highlight a few caveats to this method. Unlike transit observations, Gaia samples sparsely and on a scan-law-determined cadence, so the modulation must neither alias against the scanning law nor be so slow that only a small fraction of a cycle falls within the 5 to 10 yr baseline. Additionally, GJ 876 is an outlier regarding resonance detectability. For most resonant systems, this analysis would not be fruitful, and would come at a significant com-putational cost. A rigorous adjustment of the prior space for both interacting and non-interacting models is hence necessary to provide meaningful Bayes factors that identify deviations. However, it is still worth exploring in future work. The various biases identified in this work will have a significant impact on the demographics from Gaia. It is therefore important to quantify these biases. The least significant of them all, the inclination bias, can already inform us on what fraction of systems are subject

To further adjust the de-mographics, it is necessary to find a mathematical model that goes beyond the resoeccentric degeneracy and encapsulates the biases at large effective eccentricities, in both cases where the second planet is either detectable or undetectable. With this model, adjusting the giant planet occurrence rates on a population scale hence becomes possible. Finally, we want to address various important technical points that were neglected in the detection pipeline. The modelling of the error is assumed to be random normally distributed uncorrelated noise, and hence ignores the various scan angle biases identified in Section 3.2. To obtain meaningful detections from actual DR4 data would require to integrate scan-angle harmonics into the different model comparisons, as well as a jerk term on top of the linear acceleration term already implemented. These effects are already included in the detection pipeline presented in Baycroft et al. (2026), where the scan angle signal identified in Target 4 of Section 3.2 is correctly recovered. These additions will significantly reduce contamination by false positives due to unresolved binaries, or any other scan angle related signals. Additionally, DR4 orbit fits from the pipeline will also have to be compared with models fixing a circular orbit, to confirm that the planets are indeed observed on eccentric orbits as opposed to a spurious eccentricity arising from the data S/N or uneven sampling cadence. All of the previous adjustments will have to be made to the pipeline in order to apply it rigorously to real data, on top of increasing the number of live points to achieve credible and systematic detections. 

6 SUMMARY 

We used the N-body code REBOUND to simulate astrometric data as measured by Gaia for giant multi-planet systems at au distances. We then developed a planet detection pipeline based on nested sampling to fit different planetary architectures, including a purely astrometric solution with and without a linear acceleration, a 1-planet model and a 2-planet (non-interacting Keplerian) model. Our pipeline was ver-ified on the published time series Gaia data from the BH3 detection, where we accurately recovered its parameters. Using our pipeline we recover previously established 1-planet de-tection criteria with Gaia. We also investigated 2-planet detection criteria with a grid study, with particular attention to systems near and far from 2:1 resonance. We sought to identify each scenario where 2-planet detections are statistically impossible and how a sec-ond undetected planet would affect the retrieved orbital parameters for the other planet. We found for single planet detections that we are able to detect shorter period planets (P < 10 yrs) when they have an astrometric S/N per transit greater than 1.01 and 0.77 for DR4 and DR5 baselines, respectively. Including a linear acceleration, we find a sharp cut-off in system detectability for planets beyond 6 au and 10 au for DR4 and DR5 respectively. We found that Gaia is capable of detecting 2-planet systems, using synthetic GJ 876 data as an example. Investigating a grid of 2-planet systems spanning a range of planet mass and period ratios, we found that the lower S/N planet is harder to detect. In DR5, this planet is detected at S/N per astrometric point threshold between 0.5 and 2, as opposed to a range of 0.5 to 0.75 for the dominant planet. We also found that planets closer to 2:1 commensurability are harder to find, and systems with a more massive outer planet show larger deviations from the theoretical detectability power law. When fitting a 1-planet model to data from a 2-planet system in 2:1 resonance, we found that we recover the dominant planet in the signal, with significant eccentricity, mass and inclination biases. These biases become more important as the astrometric signatures of both planets get closer ( 𝑒 bias = 0.8 and 0.4 for mass ratios of 0.8 and 2.5 respectively). When the planets are closer in mass, in most cases both planets can be recovered without bias. However, when one planet becomes statistically undetectable the bias persists. When the outer planet is at least three times as massive as the inner planet, the bias tends towards the previously known resoeccentric degeneracy, where 1-planet and 2-planet solutions are degenerate. We found that placing the planets in dynamical mean-motion resonance extends the parameter space for this degeneracy down to shorter periods. Finally, using the example of GJ 876, we found that apsidal precession can break the eccentricity bias if the system is observed for a long enough baseline, i.e. that of DR5. Overall, if giant multi-planet systems are common the Gaia cata-logue might end up being plagued with single planet systems with anomalously large eccentricities. A thorough re-analysis of the DR4 data might be necessary to confirm if certain targets, especially those with large residuals, are better explained as resonant 2-planet sys-tems. The severe mass bias (between 10% for mass ratios of 2.5 and 100% for mass ratios of 0.8) will have a significant impact on the inferred giant planet demographics from the Gaia catalogue. This result will bias the observational constraints used to test planet-formation models if not corrected, as we might falsely expect there to be fewer systems in 2:1 resonance from the Gaia data, compared to the real distribution of these planets. To identify the degree of contamination in the data, a follow-up idea could be to compare the population-wide distribution of inclinations (including the bias) to the distribution expected from geometry only, taking into account the harder detectability of edge-on systems.
