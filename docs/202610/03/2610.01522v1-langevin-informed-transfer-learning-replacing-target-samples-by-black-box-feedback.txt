## Abstract 

Many scientific and machine learning systems, from molecular dynamics to diffusion models and beyond, are governed by stochastic dynamics with low-dimensional structure, evolving on slow timescales. However, target trajectories, used to identify and interpret such dynamics, are often inaccessible: only biased or static samples that explore the underlying manifold are available. We introduce Langevin-Informed Transfer Learning (LITL), a framework for recovering target Langevin dynamics from biased source samples using only black-box feedback. LITL learns the leading spectral structure of the target infinitesimal generator and the projected drift through Dirichlet representation learning, enabling kinetic recon-struction in spectral form and slow-manifold gradient field estimation. We further introduce a spherical variant well suited to steering normalized latent representa-tions commonly used in learning systems toward desired objectives. We establish finite-sample guarantees for eigenvalue, eigenfunction, and projected drift estima-tion in Sobolev norms, thereby ensuring generalization of these quantities and their first-order derivatives. Empirically, LITL recovers physical transition timescales from biased molecular simulations, builds kinetic structure from static samples of generative models, reconstructs spherical symmetries of physical systems, and en-ables post-hoc latent steering of trained neural networks under black-box feedback. Together, these results position spectral operator learning as a practical framework for recovering stochastic dynamics under distribution shift and unlock applications across machine learning and the physical sciences. 

## 1 Introduction 

Many systems across science and modern machine learning evolve through stochastic dynamics shaped by a small number of slow collective modes. In molecular dynamics, these modes govern metastable states and transition pathways [51, 5]; in modern machine learning, they relate to semantic structures in generative [63] and transformer models [23, 30]. In both contexts, Langevin-type 

> Preprint. arXiv:2610.01522v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026

dynamics underlie these stochastic gradient flows, further being backbone of Bayesian sampling, diffusion models, and latent generative processes [10, 40, 53, 54, 58]. Across these domains, the key object controlling long-timescale behavior is the infinitesimal generator , whose dominant spectral structure encodes geometry, kinetics, and transition pathways between metastable configurations of the underlying dynamics. Understanding such systems requires recovering this spectral structure, a task intractable with classical methods due to the curse of dimensionality that calls for data-driven approaches. In practice, however, the target dynamics are often inaccessible: one may only observe 

static or biased samples together with black-box evaluative feedback , while target trajectories, gradients, or direct dynamical observations remain unavailable. So, a fundamental challenge persists: 

How can one recover a target system’s dynamics without access to its trajectories? 

This setting arises naturally in biased molecular simulations [36], generative models producing equilibrium samples without kinetics [37], physical systems where potentials are reconstructed from measurements [28], and post-hoc latent steering problems with purely evaluative black-box feedback. Existing transfer-learning and adaptation methods predominantly rely on retraining, fine-tuning, or direct optimization of parametric objectives [44, 39, 20]. In contrast, recent spectral approaches in dynamical systems focus on learning transfer operators or infinitesimal generators from trajectory data [24, 31, 13, 7, 34], but do not address transfer under distribution shift or recovery of target dynamics from static biased samples. Moreover, existing generator-learning methods at best provide guarantees only in L2-type geometries and all fail to recover projected drift fields [32, 8, 62, 16, 33], limiting controlled reconstruction of target dynamics and kinetic transfer. We introduce Langevin-Informed Transfer Learning (LITL) , a framework for recovering and trans-ferring target Langevin dynamics from biased source samples using only black-box feedback. Rather than addressing transfer learning in full generality, LITL focuses specifically on transferring stochas-tic dynamics by recovering the target generator’s slow spectral structure together with a projected drift on a learned slow manifold. The method combines importance weighting, Sobolev/Dirichlet representation learning, and generalized eigendecomposition to reconstruct physically meaningful dynamics from static or biased data without requiring target trajectories, differentiable objectives, or retraining. In this sense, LITL reframes transfer learning as transfer of stochastic dynamics: by learning the generator geometry rather than task-specific parameters, it enables post-hoc steering through geometry-aware Langevin flows in latent spaces. This perspective is particularly compelling for modern machine learning systems operating on normalized embeddings and diffusion-like latent dynamics, where semantic organization is intrinsically geometric and metastable. In this setting, we further introduce a streamlined spherical formulation enabling efficient gradient-free transfer on normalized latent manifolds. To relate LITL to existing transfer learning methods, see App. A. On the theoretical side, we establish finite-sample guarantees for eigenvalue, eigenfunction, and projected-drift estimation in Sobolev norms. Empirically, LITL recovers physical transition timescales from biased molecular simulations, extracts kinetic structure from static GenAI samples, reconstructs spherical dynamics of cobalt nanoparticles from black-box potential evaluations, and enables post-hoc fairness-aware latent steering without retraining. Together, these results position spectral operator learning as a practical framework for recovering and transferring stochastic dynamics under dis-tribution shift, while highlighting broader implications for machine learning systems governed by Langevin-type dynamics. 

Contributions. This paper makes three main contributions. (i) Methodology: We introduce LITL, a framework for recovering and transferring stochastic dynamics from biased samples using black-box feedback. (ii) Theory: To our knowledge, we provide the first finite-sample guarantees for eigenvalue, eigenfunction, and projected-drift estimation in Sobolev norms. (iii) Applications: We demonstrate effectiveness across molecular modeling, physical systems, and post-hoc alignment tasks.

Spherical LITL. Unlike classical Langevin dynamics in Euclidean space, which requires a confining potential to admit an invariant distribution, Langevin dynamics on a sphere admits a well-defined invariant measure even in the absence of a potential. This provides a principled, geometry-respecting way to steer the system while avoiding the instability and mode collapse that can arise in unconstrained Euclidean settings. In particular, if the source dynamics is pure diffusion on the sphere ( U ′ = 0)the uniform measure is its invariant distribution. This is crucial in our setting since static samples from the reference distribution are readily available by direct normalization of isotropic random vectors , avoiding long trajectories simulation or stochastic integrators tuning. In this context, transfer learning from the black-box potential is performed by short-horizon gradient updates on the sphere, 

∇Sd−1 U (x) = ( I−xx T)∇U (x), thereby driving the system toward the wells of potential energy. This approach is motivated by the prevalence of normalized embeddings and cosine-based geometry in modern AI systems, models latent dynamics as spherical diffusion with a locally constructed, human-induced potential, enabling targeted, on-the-fly modulation of the invariant distribution without retraining the base model. This geometric formulation aligns naturally with contrastive representation learning, angular-margin classifiers, and embedding-based retrieval, where semantic information is encoded directionally rather than in magnitude. Thus, Spherical LITL provides a principled and sample-efficient mechanism for human control of learned representations, enhancing adaptability and robustness, while preserving the global structure of pretrained representations.

Alanine-Dipeptide molecule: The second experiment is a controlled molecular transfer benchmark, demonstrating how LITL transfers data samples from biased (non-physical) simulations of Alanine-Dipeptide to recover physical transition rates. The system has 21 dimensional state made of dihedral angles, and due to the curse of dimensionality spectral decompositions cannot be numerically computed. The key message is that this simple molecule displays two main slow modes, of which the slower one is identified by the dihedral angle ϕ and cannot be observed without the addition of a bias potential during a simulation, which then requires the transfer learning method. ## 6 Conclusions 

We introduced Langevin-Informed Transfer Learning (LITL), a framework for recovering and trans-ferring target stochastic dynamics from biased samples using only black-box feedback. By learning the target generator’s slow spectral structure and projected drift with finite-sample guarantees, LITL enables principled coarse-graining, kinetic reconstruction without target trajectories, and post-hoc steering of ML models without retraining. Conceptually, LITL reframes transfer learning as the recovery and manipulation of stochastic dynamics rather than parameter adaptation. In other words: 

Instead of transferring weights or features, LITL transfers generator structure, enabling geometry-aware steering through learned slow manifolds. 

Our results suggest broader relevance beyond molecular systems. Since Langevin-type dynamics also underlie stochastic gradient flows and diffusion-based generative models, spectral operator learning may provide a new route for analyzing and steering latent stochastic dynamics in modern AI systems through gradient-free feedback. 

Limitations. LITL currently focuses on reversible Langevin dynamics and assumes informative black-box evaluations together with identifiable low-dimensional slow structure. Transfer learning, domain adaptation, and meta-learning. Classical transfer learning focuses on reusing parameters or representations learned on a source task to accelerate optimization or improve generalization on a target task, typically through fine-tuning or feature reuse [see 44, 60, and references therein]. Domain adaptation and covariate shift methods reweight losses or samples to correct distribution mismatch, assuming access to target data and gradients [52, 3]. Meta-learning provides a different setting in which shared representations are learned from multiple related tasks [2, 19, 29, 42]. While effective, these methods fundamentally rely on retraining and explicit optimization against a target objective. LITL departs from this paradigm by transferring dynamical structure rather than parameters or losses , enabling adaptation to unseen objectives without optimization in the target domain. 

Bayesian sampling and Langevin dynamics. Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD) and related MCMC methods provide approximate Bayesian inference by injecting noise into gradient-based optimization [58, 40]. These approaches assume direct access to the target energy or its stochastic gradients and are not designed for controlled transfer across objectives. LITL is intrinsically linked to these methods, but addresses a distinct inverse problem: learning and transferring the generator itself, rather than sampling from a known energy. This enables principled post-hoc adaptation without modifying step sizes, noise schedules, or loss functions. 

Diffusion and score-based generative models. Score-based diffusion models and denoising diffusion probabilistic models learn gradients of log-densities (the potential) in order to generate samples by reversing a predefined noising process [25]. Latent diffusion models further improve scalability by learning generative dynamics in compressed latent spaces [46]. LITL is related to score-based diffusion models in that learn gradients of log-densities which can be used to generate samples. However, the similarity is superficial. Diffusion models learn pointwise, time-dependent score fields using samples from the target distribution or its noisy marginals [53]. In contrast, LITL never observes samples from the target Gibbs/Boltzman distribution and does not learn a score field directly. Instead, it learns spectral representations of the Langevin generator from biased trajectories and forward-only side information, and reconstructs the target drift only after projection onto the dominant slow eigenspaces. This yields a spectrally filtered score that governs long-time dynamics rather than pointwise density matching. As a result, LITL enables the creation of unseen kinetic models with finite-sample guarantees, capabilities that are fundamentally outside the scope of existing diffusion and score-based approaches. ## B Background: Langevin dynamics and Dirichlet forms

D.6 Extension to general compact operators 

The formulation (42) requires Rμ to be Hilbert–Schmidt in Wμπ for the norm ∥Rμ∥2HS( Wμπ ) to be finite. However, the equivalent form (52) expressed via covariance matrices W θ and C θ is well-defined whenever Rμ is compact (guaranteed by confinement) and the neural network features (zθj )mj=0 and their gradients are in Wμπ (guaranteed by Assumption (BE) ). This broader applicability is crucial since 

compactness (rather than Hilbert–Schmidt) is the natural condition ensured by standard confinement assumptions on the potential (Assumption (CP) ). Indeed the covariance matrices W θ and C θ involve only Wμπ -inner products between finitely many neural network features (always finite if features are in Wμπ ) and expectations under π′ of bounded functions (bounded by Assumption (BE) ). Crucially, no infinite sums or traces over the full spectrum of Rμ appear in (52), unlike in (42). Therefore, the covariance formulation extends naturally to all compact operators. 

D.7 Resolvent estimation in a given representation. 

Finally, we remark that our approach, defined by equations (12) and (13), differs fundamentally from regression-based methods that minimize the Hilbert-Schmidt approximation error of the resolvent (see in particular equation (16) in [16]). In spirit our approach is closer to CCA approaches [ ? 35, 59] based on L2

E.2 Assumptions (BE), (CP) and (RA): discussion 

Before presenting the proofs for resolvent (App. E.3), generator (App. E.4), and gradient estimation (App. E.5), we briefly discuss the assumptions used throughout the analysis, namely (BE) , (CP) and 

(RA) .

Bounded embeddings: (BE) There exist τ ≥ 1 such that ess sup x′∼π′ |∂kzθj (x′)| ≤ τ < ∞,

∀k = 0 , . . . , d and ∀j = 0 , . . . , m , with the convention ∂0zθ = zθ .Assumption (BE) requires then uniform boundedness of the embedding functions (zθj )mj=0 and their first-order derivatives under the biased sampling distribution π′. This condition ensures finite variance of empirical spectral and gradient estimators and allows uniform concentration over basis indices and coordinates. It is satisfied by many standard choices of embeddings, including truncated trigonometric bases, bounded neural feature maps, or other smooth finite-dimensional representations. When the dynamics evolve on a compact manifold , such as the sphere Sd−1, this assumption can be substantially relaxed. Indeed, compactness implies that any continuous embedding is automatically bounded, and any C1 embedding has bounded (intrinsic) first derivatives. Consequently, for spherical Langevin dynamics, a (BE) -type condition holds automatically for any fixed truncation level m,provided the embeddings are C1 on the manifold. 

Confining potential: (CP) The potential U satisfies U (x) ≥ 2α∥x∥21{∥ x∥≥ R} − c, ∀x, for some 

α > 0, R > 0, c ∈ R, and the bias obeys ∥V ∥∞ < ∞.Assumption (CP) ensures stability and the existence of an invariant distribution for Langevin dynamics in Euclidean space. The quadratic lower bound on U outside a compact set guarantees exponential tail decay of the invariant measure, implying finite moments of all orders. This integrability is essential when working with biased samples , as it ensures that reweighting by a bounded bias does not lead to ill-conditioned estimators. Together with boundedness of the bias V , this assumption yields equivalence between the biased and unbiased measures, ergodicity of the dynamics, and consistency of empirical averages. Furthermore, crucially, (CP) implies that x ∼ π and x′ ∼ π′ are 

K-sub-Gaussian with K2 ≲ (βα )−1, allowing one to derive concentration bounds for the gradient estimation. In contrast, spherical Langevin dynamics evolve on a compact state space. Compactness prevents escape to infinity and ensures the existence of an invariant distribution under much weaker conditions. In this setting, no confining growth of U is required; it suffices that the target density is well-defined with respect to the surface measure and that the drift is sufficiently regular. 

Gradient regularity: (RA) ∃p ≥ 1 such that ∀k ∈ [d], ∥∂kU ∥Wμ,p π < ∞, that is ∥∇ U ∥Wμ,p π < ∞.

30 Assumption (RA) requires the gradient of the potential to belong to a Sobolev space Wμ,p π for some 

p ≥ 1. This ensures that ∇U is well-defined in a weak sense and has finite p-th moments under the invariant distribution. The condition is used to control the bias–variance trade-off in gradient estimation and to establish stability and convergence of the resulting gradient-based dynamics. Importantly, it does not require global Lipschitz continuity or pointwise smoothness, and is therefore compatible with nonconvex or moderately rough potentials.

For our method LILT in this experiment, we used standard feed forward neural networks, with tanh activation function. We used μ = 100 , α = 5 and trained 20 features. One of the strengths of LITL is the estimation of the drift. In this section, we compare our estimation with respect to the ground truth. In Figure 5, left panel we show how the drift can be projected on the basis of the eigenfunctions of L obtained by numerically solving the eigenvalue problem.
