Abstract Studies suggest that protoplanetary disks around stars with high carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) ratios can produce terrestrial planets with carbon-dominated compositions instead of silicon-dominated ones. While previous work studied the evolution of these disks and the planets that form within them, none analyze in detail how the differences in the surface density profiles of these disks could affect the architectures and iron and siderophile contents of exoplanetary systems. Here, we present a new study that uses the N-body integrator REBOUND and data from a sequential dust condensation model to simulate the formation of planets in systems with varying C/O ratios. We find that bulk density could be used to distinguish between silicon-rich and carbon-rich planets, though it may not be a reliable metric on its own. 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Previous studies indicate that rocky planets are likely common throughout the galaxy (Bryson et al. 2021) and can be enriched or depleted in certain chemical species compared to the inner four planets of the Solar Sys-tem (Bond et al. 2010b). Researchers often link these compositional discrepancies to a host star’s metallicity since this parameter likely reflects the conditions of the protoplanetary disk in which these exoplanets formed (Adibekyan et al. 2021). Of particular interest has been the C/O ratios of these stars and systems, which theo-ries suggest can determine whether or not a planetary system has a primarily silicate composition — like the inner Solar System — or a composition dominated by carbon and its compounds. While the vast majority of nearby stars appear to have C/O ratios below unity (Brewer & Fischer 2016; Stonkut˙ e et al. 2020; Delgado Mena et al. 2021; Sharma et al. 2024), stars with ratios above unity do exist, 1 mak-ing the discovery of carbon planets rare but possible. So far, only one candidate around a main-sequence star, 55 Cancri e, has been found (Madhusudhan et al. 2012). However, separate studies of its host star’s C/O ratio have returned several different values (Delgado Mena et al. 2010; Teske et al. 2013; Brewer & Fischer 2016), some of which are substantially less than unity, leading to uncertainty about its carbon-rich nature. Considering the systematic errors present in trying to measure C/O ratios in stellar atmospheres (Teske et al. 2013; Sharma et al. 2024), we require other methods to determine if a planet is carbon-rich. For example, if the carbon content of a planet is related to its core mass fraction (CMF) or bulk density, then we could differen-

Previous work has developed multiple models and pieces of software to resolve the chemical compositions and structure of early protoplanetary disks (Cassen 1996; Ciesla 2008; Bond et al. 2010b,a; Pignatale et al. 2016; Li et al. 2020). In addition, numerous studies have used N -body simulations to simulate the formation of planetary systems, especially during the late-stage of planet formation where interactions are dominated by gravity and impacts between protoplanets and planetes-imals (Kokubo & Genda 2010; Chambers 2013; Bonsor et al. 2015; Childs et al. 2019; Scora et al. 2020; Childs & Steffen 2022; Scora et al. 2022; Kaufmann & Alibert 2023; Childs et al. 2023; Woo et al. 2024). These simula-tions are often used to perform statistical studies on the architecture and multiplicity of planetary systems and the physical properties of the resulting planets, including their sizes, masses, and compositions. Researchers have added additional features to these simulations in recent years, including imperfect collisions between particles, which can affect the final masses and compositions of the resulting planets. Some studies have connected these chemical and N -body simulations (Bond et al. 2010b,a,c; Elser et al. 2012; Moriarty et al. 2014) to investigate the compositional diversity of the planets that form. 2. MODELS AND METHODS 

2.1. Sequential Condensation Model 

We use results produced by the sequential dust conden-sation model found in Li et al. (2020). This model com-bines the disk evolution model found in Cassen (1996), the dust advection model in Jacquet et al. (2012), and the 

GRAINS condensation code Petaev (2009) to allow chem-ical species to sequentially condense out of a disk rather than all at once. Their algorithm uses three different phases for its over 500 condensates: a gaseous phase, an advected dust phase that allows condensates to flow with gases in the disk, and a decoupled dust phase that allows condensates to settle and remain unperturbed at a sin-gle orbital distance. This algorithm allows for condensing species and the evolving disk to react with each other in a feedback loop to produce the desired sequential dust production. See Li et al. (2020) for more details. The data for this paper come from Shakespeare et al. (2025), who used this model to study how C/O ratios affect the compositions of protoplanetary disks around Sun-like stars. They used two methods to change the C/O ratio in their simulations: varying the amount of carbon and oxygen in a simulation while keeping their sum constant and varying the amount of carbon while keeping the amount of oxygen constant. The data we use were produced using the first method, which they defined as the “constant sum” method in their publication. Note that the amounts of other elements are held constant at solar values, so this method will change the ratios of other elements with carbon and oxygen such as the nitrogen to oxygen ratio. 2.2. Initial Disks 

When determining the placement of objects for our simulations, we assume that the protoplanetary disks from which they form are all circular and perfectly sym-metrical about the system’s host star and extend from 0.3 AU to 4.0 AU. Additionally, in order to form a variety of Super-Earths and nothing larger, we assume that ev-ery disk contains 20 M⊕ of material. The surface density profiles shown in the top panel of Figure 1 indicates that disks with different C/O ratios will not have the same total mass. Therefore, we normalize each of these pro-files so that they contain the chosen amount of material. The center panel of Figure 1 shows the surface density profiles for each C/O ratio after normalization. Following the procedure in Quintana et al. (2016), Childs & Steffen (2022), and Ferich et al. (2025), we cre-ate a late-stage protoplanetary disk with 260 planetes-imals and 26 embryos that are ten times more massive than the planetesimals. For this study, our planetesimal mass is 0.0385 M⊕ and embryo mass is 0.385 M⊕. With this arrangement, the total mass is split evenly between planetesimals and embryos. To determine where objects are placed in the disk, we start at the inner edge of the disk and move outwards until a particular region has enough mass to form a planetesimal or embryo, which we determine by numerically integrating the curves shown in the bottom panel of Figure 1. We place the embryo at the midpoint between the inner edge and outer edge of the region and begin this process again at the outer edge. For example, to create the first embryo in a particu-lar disk, we start at 0.3 AU and move outwards until we encompass a region that contains 0.385 M⊕. If we as-sume this region occurs between 0.3 AU to 0.7 AU, then we place the embryo at 0.5 AU and repeat this method, now starting at 0.7 AU and continuing outwards until the entire disk contains 26 planetary embryos. We then perform this process over again for planetesimals until the disk contains 260 of them. Once the semi-major axes of all objects have been de-termined, we draw random values from uniform distri-butions for other orbital parameters. > 2.3.

N-body Simulations and Fragmentation 

We use REBOUND (Rein & Liu 2012) to simulate the for-mation of planetary systems around a 1 M⊙ star from 160 initial disks. Each simulation is run for 10 Myr of simu-lation time or until they reach 1152 hours of CPU time. In certain cases, simulations did not reach the allotted 10 Myr of simulation time. However, in all cases, we were able to obtain full planetary systems with distinct plan-ets and some leftover material. We use the MERCURIUS 

integrator, which can accurately treat close encounters between objects, for every simulation. For regular disks, we use a time step of 0.01 years, which corresponds to around 1/25 of the period of the innermost orbit, and for compact disks, we use a time step of 0.001 years, which corresponds to around 1/22 of the period of the innermost orbit. We also use an expansion factor of 3 on all particles in all simulations. This factor artificially inflates the initial radius of particles at the start of the integration. We note that this parameter can affect the collisional history of planets (Childs & Steffen 2022) and how much radial mixing occurs in the system during for-mation (Grimm & Stadel 2026); we assume our value for it is small enough to keep these effects to a minimum. We utilize the fragmentation module for REBOUND cre-ated by Childs & Steffen (2022) to implement imperfect collisions between the objects. This module uses mod-els from Leinhardt & Stewart (2012), Asphaug (2010), and Genda et al. (2012) to semi-analytically determine the outcomes of impacts between a more massive “tar-get” object and a less massive “projectile” object. The outcome of a particular collision depends on the impact angle, impact velocity, and masses and radii of the col-liding objects. The largest object remaining after the collision is named the largest remnant and any less massive objects the collision produces are named fragments. During the course of a simulation, the module produces a collision report that records all collisions that occur during the runtime. Users can then input this file into the Differ-entiated Body Composition Tracker to study how the CMFs of objects change over the course of a simulation as a result of impacts (see Section 2.4 for more details). The fragmentation module contains eight possible out-comes for collisions, which we combine into six categories in this paper for brevity: bounces, mergers, erosions, accretions, and hit-and-runs. Bounces occur when the colliding object remains completely intact after impact. Mergers occur when the two colliding objects combine into a single object. Erosions lower the mass of the tar-get and turn the projectile and the removed mass into equal-sized fragments. Accretions raise the mass of the target and turn the remaining mass in the projectile into equal-sized fragments. The minimum possible mass of these fragments is a variable that can be changed by the user. For our study, we choose this minimum mass to be half of the planetesimal mass (0.0193 M⊕). Finally, hit-and-runs are similar to accretions but allow the pro-jectile to remain intact, albeit with less mass. For more information on the fragmentation module, see Childs & Steffen (2022).  

> 2.4.

Composition Tracking and Interior Modeling 

To track the changes in CMF produced by collisions between objects, Ferich et al. (2025) created a mod-ule called the Differentiated Body Composition Tracker (DBCT). This post-processing software takes a collision report from the fragmentation module for REBOUND and a composition input file that details the initial CMFs of all objects in a simulation and produces a composition output file that returns the CMFs of all objects remain-ing at the end of a simulation. The DBCT assumes that all objects are perfectly spherical and have two distinct layers of core and mantle material and determines the CMF changes from an individual impact by using the collision type, impact angle, and core and outer radii of the two colliding objects. Users can fine tune how much core material is transferred during a collision by setting the minimum and maximum fraction of material that can be included in the ejecta of an erosive or accretive collision; these parameters are known as fmin and fmax 

respectively. For this paper, we set fmin = 0 .0 and fmax = 1 .0, which is the maximum possible range between the two. In addition, users can change the densities of the core and mantle material, which will determine the core and outer radii of the colliding objects. For this study, we set the density of core material to 7874.0 kg m −3, the density of iron, and the density of mantle material to 3000.0 kg m−3. For more details on the DBCT, see Ferich et al. (2025). The results we obtained from Shakespeare et al. (2025) show that the C/O ratio of a protoplanetary disk can determine how much of a particular element condenses at certain regions within the disk. A portion of these condensed elements are siderophiles: elements that will settle into the core of a larger object. By adding the surface densities of these siderophiles together 2 and di-viding it by the disk’s total surface density, we can create effective CMF distributions across the disk for each C/O ratio, which are shown in Figure 3. The plot indicates that the C/O ratio can significantly change the effective CMF at a particular location in the disk. This effect is mainly due to how the combined surface densities of carbon and oxygen, which are not considered siderophiles, vary between different C/O ra-tios. At a ratio 0.5, which is near the solar value, the majority of the disk will have a CMF of just under 0.3, a value close to the CMF of Earth ( ≈0.33 (Stacey 2005)). As the C/O ratio increases, the CMF profile shifts up-wards, with significant spikes below 1.0 AU for ratios of 0.8 and 0.9. This could lead to rocky planets with CMFs higher than that of Earth. To do so, we first find the average planetary CMF and mass for each C/O ratio. We then input these values into MAGRATHEA (Huang et al. 2022) — a planetary interior modeling software that now has the ability to model carbon planets — to find the av-erage outer planetary radius for each C/O ratio. Once we have these results, we use the average planetary mass and average planetary radius to calculate the average bulk planetary density for each C/O ratios and compare them to see if there are significant differences between carbon and silicon planets. We note that we do not con-sider any detailed effects of the oxidation state of the planet-forming material, which can cause variation in the core mass fraction of a forming planet (Dale et al. 2026).  

> 3. RESULTS

We present the results from our analysis of all 160 sim-ulations in the following sections. In the first, we ana-lyze the statistics from the final planetary systems to try any find any observable differences between the architec-tures of systems formed from disks with different C/O ratios. In the second section, we look at the percentage of siderophiles that compose each planet, which we con-sider to be a planet’s CMF, and try to discern any cor-relation between the CMFs and bulk densities of planets and whether they are majority carbon or silicon. For all results, we assume that a planet is an object with a mass equal to or larger than the embryo mass, a fragment is an object with a mass equal to or below the planetesimal mass, and a planetesimal is any object with mass between the initial planetesimal and embryo masses. In addition, we consider C/O ratios with values below unity as low ratios and systems produced in those disks as silicate-dominant, and we will consider C/O ratios with values of unity or above as high ratios and systems produced in those disks as carbon-dominant (see Section 2.1 for reasoning).

4. DISCUSSION 

4.1. Distinguishing Carbon-rich Planets 

Our results indicate that the bulk density of a planet could be used to determine if a planet is silicon-rich or carbon-rich. However, our interior models assumed that planets were made of only rocky material and contained no significant hydrospheres or atmospheres, even though planets above 5 M⊕ are likely to retain an H/He enve-lope after formation (Rogers 2015). Many of the planets we formed, especially the most massive ones ( ∼10 M⊕), could contain substantial layers of volatiles, which will affect their observed radii and bulk densities. We would require observations of any potential atmospheres sur-rounding these planets to have confidence in these mod-els. Obtaining interior models that do include these ad-ditional layers would also provide a similar benefit. Additionally, the measured values of exoplanetary masses and radii often have uncertainties that could make it difficult to confirm whether or not a planet is majority silicate or carbon with bulk density alone. To quantify this error, we derive how uncertainties in radius and mass measurement will propagate to uncertainty in a bulk density calculation. We find that a mass mea-surement with a precision of ±0.01 M⊕ and a radius measurement with a precision of ±0.01 R⊕ for an exo-planet with the same mass and radius as Earth would lead to an uncertainty of approximately ±0.174 g/cm 3

for the calculated density. This error is around an order of magnitude lower than the highest difference in den-sity we found between silicon and carbon planets (2.34 

g/cm 3). Finally, an exoplanet’s CMF could lead to degeneracies in bulk density that could make distinguishing between silicon and carbon exoplanets difficult. A carbon planet with a particularly high CMF could be misidentified as a silicon planet, while a silicon planet with a particularly low CMF could be misidentified as a carbon planet. For example, results from Royer et al. (2026) show that a carbon planet with a mass of 1 M⊕ and CMF of approx-imately 0.5 would be similar in mass and radius to Earth, and therefore, have the same bulk density to an outside observer. While giant impacts during planet formation could lead to the abnormally high or low CMFs for this to occur, our results show that the majority of planets will have CMFs similar to the system average. In addi-tion, our results indicate that carbon planets often have lower CMFs on average than silicon planets, so a high CMF carbon planet and a low CMF silicon planet would require a rare formation pathway. Therefore, we argue that CMF will not have a significant impact on how well bulk density can be used as a tracer of carbon planets. Overall, we argue these limitations and caveats do not prevent bulk density from being a possible method to dif-ferentiate between carbon and silicon planets. > 4.2.

Future Work 

One important assumption we made when building our disks in Section 2.2 is that all of them contained a total mass of 20 M⊕, even though the top panel of Figure 1 shows discrepancies between the surface density profiles for different C/O ratios. In order to show how these differences affect the total mass available in each disk, we numerically integrate our surface density profiles from 0.3 AU to 4.0 AU and place the resulting masses in Table 9. The table shows that disks with a C/O ratio of 0.9 have the lowest amount of available mass. The table also shows that the total mass increases more rapidly from this minimum moving towards higher C/O ratios than lower ones, indicating that systems highly enriched in carbon could form higher-mass planets than silicate-dominated ones, which was mentioned in Shakespeare et al. (2025). However, our results in Section 3.2 show that disks with a smaller C/O ratio of 0.5 formed some of the largest planets on average, which could mitigate this effect to some degree. Still, future work should remove this assumption when performing simulations. In addition, we applied the surface densities from one of the earliest stages of planet formation (dust conden-sation) to the last stage of planet formation (giant im-pacts). In between those stages, processes such as radial mixing and pebble accretion could significantly affect the total surface density of the disk as well as the individ-ual surface densities of different elements and chemical species. Future studies should find ways to include the influence of other stages of planet formation when ini-tializing late-stage integrations.  

> 5. CONCLUSIONS

For this study, we performed N-body simulations of late-stage planet formation for systems with different C/O ratios with the goal of finding observable differences between silicon-rich and carbon-rich planets. C/O ratios of 0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, 1.5, and 2.0 were chosen; the lower four we assumed formed all silicate-dominated planets and the upper four we assumed formed all carbon-dominated planets. The simulations included a Sun-like star and 276 objects: 26 planetary embryos and 260 planetesimals which are ten times less massive than embryos. We used two types of disks with each C/O ratio: one type that extended from 0.3 AU to 4.0 AU, which we called regular disks, and one type that extended from 0.09 AU to 0.8 AU, which we called compact disks. We determined the initial layouts of each disk for these simu-lations using surface density profiles of different C/O ra-tios obtained from a sequential dust condensation model (Li et al. 2020; Shakespeare et al. 2025). In addition, we determined the initial CMFs of objects in each disk using the surface density profiles of siderophile elements obtained from the same model. Ten simulations were performed for each C/O ratio and each type of disk, giving us a total of 160 plane-tary systems to analyze. These simulations included im-perfect collisions between objects. With our results, we first studied the architectures of these planetary systems to determine if there were any significant differences be-tween systems with carbon-rich planets and silicon-rich planets. We then determined the bulk densities of plan-ets formed in these simulations using the interior model-ing code MAGRATHEA . We then compared these densities for different C/O ratios in order to find if this property could be used to discern between carbon-rich and silicon-rich planets in observations. Our data showed that, across all C/O ratios, larger planets formed towards the center of a disk, a trend that was found in a previous study with a similar setup (Ferich et al. 2025). However, after analyzing the orbital proper-ties of these systems, we found no significant differences between carbon-rich and silicon-rich planetary systems. Our results also showed that planetary CMFs tend to remain close to the initial average CMF for a particular C/O ratio, especially when it comes to larger planets. This result was similarly found in Ferich et al. (2025). After finding the average planetary bulk densities with these CMFs using MAGRATHEA , we determined that they are substantially different for carbon and silicon planets. Our results indicate that bulk densities could be used to differentiate between carbon and silicon planets. How-ever, it may not be a robust metric on its own and should be combined with other tracers — such as the stellar C/O ratio — to determine if a planet could be carbon-dominated. We note the setup for our simulations of late-stage planet formation was directly obtained from models of one of the earliest stages of planet formation. Future studies should determine how the stages in be-tween these two affect the layout of material in a proto-planetary disk.
