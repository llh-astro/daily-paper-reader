Abstract —Optimization in machine learning is usually expressed through iterative rules that update model parameters using information from the loss landscape. In this work, we study an alternative viewpoint in which parameter optimization is treated as the evolution of a dissipative dynamical system. The model parameters are regarded as generalized coordinates, the loss acts as a potential energy, and a positive-definite metric defines the local kinetic geometry of parameter space. Starting from a variational formulation, we derive the corresponding Hamiltonian dynamics, the geometric force generated by aposition-dependent metric, and a metric-compatible Rayleigh dissipation law. The resulting continuous system satisfies amonotonic energy-dissipation relation, while its discrete form allows the influence of curvature on the optimization trajectory to be studied directly. We illustrate the framework using a controlled CIFAR-10 image-reconstruction problem for which the optimum is known analytically. With the image Hessian used as the metric, the curvature dependence of the quadratic modal dynamics is removed. We then reparameterize the same image as a matrix product state, producing a genuinely position-dependent metric and a nonzero geometric force. Poincar´ e return maps provide a complementary phase-space view of the resulting contraction dynamics. These examples establish HAMLET as a geometric, energy-based framework for studying optimization as dissipative motion in parameter space. On a five-seed MNIST MLP benchmark, HAMLET attains the highest mean test accuracy (97.95%) and the lowest mean test negative log-likelihood (0.0696) among the three evaluated optimizers.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Optimization is the process of finding values of a set of variables that make an objective function as small or as large as possible, subject to any constraints of the problem. In machine learning, the variables being optimized are usually the model parameters θ, and the objective is a loss function 

L(θ). The objective information tells us how well the current parameters perform. The parameter-space information describes how changes in the parameters should be interpreted. In the simplest case, parameter space is treated as Euclidean. More sophisticated optimization methods may incorporate curvature, a metric, statistical information, or some other local structure. Finally, an update rule determines how the parameters evolve using this information. Unlike conventional optimization, which specifies an objective and a rule for moving through parameter space, we ask whether this parameter evolution can instead be formulated as the trajectory of a dynamical system whose state obeys physically motivated equations of motion. If the parameters are regarded as dynamical variables, then optimiza-tion can be viewed as motion through parameter space. In this picture, the loss landscape plays a role analogous to a potential-energy landscape, while the parameter trajectory determines how the system moves across it. A purely conservative system would generally oscillate around low-energy configurations rather than settle at them, so dissipation becomes essential if the dynamics are to converge toward a stationary state. This motivates a formulation in which the model parameters θ are treated as generalized coordinates, their rates of change define velocities, and a parameter-space metric g(θ) determines the local kinetic structure of the system. The trajectory followed by such a system depends not only on the loss landscape, but also on the geometry assigned to the parameter space. If the metric is Euclidean, all directions are treated uniformly. In contrast, a position-dependent positive-definite metric g(θ)

allows different directions in parameter space to carry different effective kinetic costs. Directions associated with large metric values behave as if they possess greater inertia, while directions associated with smaller metric values permit faster motion. In this way, the metric modifies how momentum is converted into parameter velocity and therefore reshapes the optimization trajectory without changing the location of the minima of the loss itself. Because the metric may vary with θ, this geometric structure also introduces additional forces into the dynamics. These arise naturally from the position dependence of the kinetic energy rather than being inserted as heuristic correction terms. The resulting motion is therefore governed jointly by the gradient of the loss, the local geometry of the parameter manifold, and dissipative effects that remove energy from the system. This leads to the central question of the proposed framework: can such dissipative geometric dynamics arXiv:2610.04969v1 [cs.LG] 4 Oct 2026 2

provide a useful and mathematically consistent framework for machine-learning optimization? To study this, we derive the Hamiltonian Metric based Learning and Energy based Training (HAMLET) dynamics from a variational formulation, construct the corresponding Hamiltonian system, introduce metric-compatible dissipation, evaluate the resulting optimizer on MNIST classification, and examine its optimization behavior on a controlled image-reconstruction problem. The image prob-lem is first studied directly in pixel space, where the quadratic objective provides a constant curvature metric and allows the spectral action of HAMLET to be examined analytically. We then represent the same image as a matrix product state, transforming the problem into a nonlinear parameter manifold with a position-dependent metric. This second representation activates the geometric-force term and provides a controlled example of the full HAMLET dynamics. To further characterize the resulting system dynamics, we employ Poincar´ e return maps which provide a geometric description of how different curvature modes contract under dynamics and allow the effect of metric normalization to be visualized directly. The remainder of the paper is organized into five parts. The first develops the variational and geometric formulation of HAMLET. The second introduces dissipation and examines the associated energy and symmetry structure. The third derives the discrete dynamics and studies their stability. The fourth reports an MNIST MLP benchmark. The fifth applies the framework to a controlled image-reconstruction problem, first in pixel space and then on an MPS parameter manifold, allowing both constant- and position-dependent metrics to be examined explicitly, and analyzes the resulting phase-space behavior through Poincar´ e return maps. II. R ELATED WORKS 

A substantial body of work has interpreted optimization algorithms through continuous-time dynamical systems. One of the most influential examples is the differential-equation inter-pretation of Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method developed by Su, Boyd, and Cand` es [1]. In this view, acceleration emerges from a second-order ordinary differential equation whose inertial and damping terms help explain the behavior of the underlying discrete algorithm. Such formulations established a useful bridge between iterative optimization and classical dynamical-systems analysis, including questions of stability, asymptotic behavior, and convergence rates. A related line of work derives optimization dynamics from variational principles. The Bregman Lagrangian framework of Wibisono, Wilson, and Jordan [2] shows that broad families of accelerated optimization methods can be understood through ap-propriately chosen Lagrangians and time-dependent variational structures. This viewpoint is particularly important for our work because it demonstrates that optimization updates need not be introduced only as algebraic rules: they may instead arise from an underlying mechanical principle. HAMLET follows this general philosophy, but adopts a metric kinetic energy on the parameter space and carries the corresponding geometric terms explicitly into the equations of motion. Hamiltonian formulations provide another natural phase-space description of optimization. Hamiltonian descent meth-ods [3] introduce momentum variables and study optimization through Hamiltonian dynamics, thereby separating parameter coordinates from their conjugate momenta. This phase-space perspective is closely related to classical mechanics and offers a natural language for inertial optimization. However, conservative Hamiltonian dynamics alone does not generically converge to a minimizer, since trajectories may continue to oscillate around low-energy regions. Dissipation therefore plays a central role whenever Hamiltonian dynamics are used as a model for optimization. Several works have addressed this issue by introducing nonconservative geometric structures. Conformal symplectic optimization methods [4] study dissipative modifications of Hamiltonian dynamics while preserving a controlled deforma-tion of the underlying symplectic structure. Contact-geometric approaches [5] provide another intrinsic description of dissipa-tive systems and have been used to formulate optimization dynamics in an extended geometric setting. These works demonstrate that dissipation can be incorporated consistently into mechanics-inspired optimization rather than being treated merely as an ad hoc numerical damping term. In parallel, geometric optimization methods emphasize that the parameter space itself need not be treated as Euclidean. Natural-gradient methods [6], [7] replace the ordinary Eu-clidean gradient by a geometry-aware direction, typically defined through a positive-definite information matrix. More broadly, Riemannian optimization [8] studies optimization over curved spaces by introducing a metric, geodesics, tangent spaces, and geometry-consistent descent directions. These approaches establish that the local geometry of parameter space can strongly affect the effective direction and scale of parameter updates. Our framework, HAMLET, is positioned at the intersection of these viewpoints. The framework does not introduce continuous-time optimization, Hamiltonian dynamics, dissipation, or Riemannian geometry individually as new concepts. Instead, it combines them within a single mechanical construction in which the loss function plays the role of potential energy and a positive-definite metric field g(θ) determines the kinetic geometry of parameter space. The metric defines the canonical relation 

p = g(θ) ˙θ,

and therefore controls how momentum is converted into parameter velocity. When the metric depends on position, its variation contributes an additional geometric force to the canonical momentum equation. This term is not inserted as an external correction but follows directly from differentiating the metric-dependent kinetic energy. Dissipation is introduced through a Rayleigh dissipation function that is compatible with the same parameter-space metric. As a result, the Hamiltonian, geometric force, and damping law are derived within one consistent variational and mechanical framework. This yields the parameter update equation in a dissipative form with an associated continuous-time energy law. The role of the metric is therefore twofold: it acts locally as a direction-dependent inertial structure and, 3

when position dependent, it also modifies the trajectory through the induced geometric force. III. M ATHEMATICAL FORMULATION

The aim of this work is to study optimization as a dissipative dynamical system evolving on a structured parameter space. Within this formulation, the loss plays the role of potential en-ergy, while the metric determines how canonical momentum is converted into parameter velocity. When the metric depends on the parameters, its variation produces an additional geometric force that changes the trajectory followed by the optimizer. The two image-reconstruction examples illustrate comple-mentary regimes of this construction. In the direct pixel repre-sentation, the quadratic objective admits a constant curvature metric. Choosing the Hessian as the metric removes the spread of curvature eigenvalues from the modal dynamics, so that all directions evolve according to the same normalized equation. This provides a simple setting in which the role of the metric can be seen analytically. The MPS representation changes the problem qualitatively. Although the underlying image is the same, the tensor-network coordinates produce a position-dependent metric and therefore activate the geometric-force term. The resulting dynamics provide a controlled example of HAMLET on a genuinely nonlinear parameter manifold. The Poincar´ e analysis gives a complementary phase-space interpretation. For the curvature-matched pixel problem, all modes possess the same return dynamics. With a Euclidean metric, the return behavior depends on curvature and weak directions may become overdamped. XII. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced HAMLET as a framework in which machine-learning optimization is formulated as dissipative Hamiltonian motion on a parameter manifold. Starting from a metric kinetic energy and the loss as potential energy, we derived the canonical momentum, Hamiltonian equations, geometric force, and metric-compatible dissipation law. The framework was evaluated on an MNIST MLP, where HAMLET reached 97.95% mean test accuracy and 0.0696 mean test NLL over five paired seeds. It was then examined on a quadratic image-reconstruction problem, where an exact curvature metric normalizes the modal dynamics, and then on an MPS parameterization of the same image, where the metric becomes position dependent and the geometric force becomes nonzero. Poincar´ e sections provided an additional phase-space interpretation of how the metric changes the contraction of different modes.
