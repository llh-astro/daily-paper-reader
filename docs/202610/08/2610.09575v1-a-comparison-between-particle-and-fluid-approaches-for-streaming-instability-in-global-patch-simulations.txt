ABSTRACT 

Context. Identifying the process relevant to planet formation is still a major task. Mechanisms have been identified that can trap dust, preventing it from migrating inwards. A particular hydrodynamic instability, known as the streaming instability, is of high interest because it may be responsible for the formation of dust clumps from which the first planetesimals form. 

Aims. We aim to compare the outcome of non-linear streaming instability simulations using both fundamental approaches in a more realistic global disk model. The results will not be used to identify or disentangle the e ff ect of, e.g., numerical di ff usion in fluid simulations or the possibility of having crossing particle streams. Instead, they will enable us to determine whether the Lagrangian particle approach is the most suitable for numerical studies of the streaming instability. 

Methods. We perform 2D, axisymmetric, global, and isothermal hydrodynamic simulations using the two codes FARGO3D (dust fluid) and PLUTO (fluid, particles) to investigate the temporal evolution of dust scale height, midplane dust-to-gas ratio, and maximum dust density as well as to study velocity distributions and vorticities. For PLUTO particle simulations, we also investigate the influence of the gas Riemann solver and spatial reconstruction orders. 

Results. Our results from a resolution study up to 5120 cells per gas scale height indicate convergence between PLUTO fluid and FARGO3D. Both yield a Gaussian profile dominated by small-scale, dust-depleted voids. Particle simulations with PLUTO have not yet converged, as a transient midplane dust component persists, indicating the need for even higher resolution or more particles. Using a more accurate Riemann solver or a higher-order spatial reconstruction scheme helps to bring the dust density profile closer to the converged structure. At the highest resolution, we find that the maximum dust density in fluid simulations is only 36% that in particle simulations, which is not too di ff erent from other code-comparison works. 

Conclusions. Studying streaming instability in a global sense, the choice of Riemann solver, spatial reconstruction order becomes more important than the approach itself (fluid, particle). Including the computational performance of both dust treatments, the fluid approach provides an e ffi cient and suitable tool for streaming instability simulations. 

Key words. Accretion, accretion disks - Instabilities - Methods: numerical - Planets and satellites: formation - Protoplanetary disks 

1. Introduction 

Understanding the properties of dust grains embedded in pro-toplanetary disks is crucial to understanding how planets form. Recent observations on protoplanetary disks with the Atacama Large Millimeter Array, such as Sierra et al. (2025); Han et al. (2023); Doi & Kataoka (2023), highlight that the largest dust grains available are on the order of millimeters or centimeters in size. On the other hand, observations of the disk around PDS 70 show clear indications of already-formed giant plan-ets (Keppler et al. 2018; Trevascus et al. 2025). This raises the question of how it is possible to grow e ffi ciently and rapidly by many orders of magnitude in grain size to form the first larger objects, such as planetesimals. In recent years, streaming insta-bility (Youdin & Goodman 2005) has gained considerable focus, as reported by Johansen & Youdin (2007) and Johansen et al.

(2007), who noted the ability of streaming instability to form overdensities that can then collapse gravitationally to form plan-etesimals, typically on the order of one to hundreds of kilome-ters in diameter (see, e.g., Johansen et al. 2009; Nesvorný et al. 2021). Hughes et al. (2018) has emphasized that observable de-bris disks are an indication that planet formation processes have successfully produced enough planetesimals, which are used as the building blocks for planets. Many works, such as Johansen et al. (2009), Simon et al. (2016), Klahr & Schreiber (2020), or Schäfer et al. (2024), have studied planetesimal for-mation via streaming instability, showing that once dust is su ffi -ciently concentrated, we can skip many orders of magnitude in grain size. Polak & Klahr (2023) have studied the formation of individual planetesimals through the gravitational collapse of a single pebble cloud at high resolution, providing further insights into their formation.

Given certain conditions for the Stokes number and the global dust-to-gas content in the disk, works such as Carrera et al. (2015), Yang et al. (2017), Li & Youdin (2021), or recently Lim et al. (2026) have investigated whether these clumps can grow in density until they reach a critical density threshold and collapse due to their own gravity. These works showed that certain combinations allow dense clumps to form and therefore provide thresholds for simulations that cannot re-solve streaming instability. Typically, shearing box simulations in combination with the particle approach for dust are used, which do not allow for studying the instability on global scales. The work of Schäfer et al. (2020), subsequent studies, as well as the work of Huang & Bai (2025) have begun to investigate the streaming instability in conjunction with vertical shear in-stability (VSI) in global disk models, spanning several tens of astronomical units in size. They demonstrate that both instabili-ties interact, potentially influencing the criteria for the formation of dense clumps. When studying the streaming instability in a global setup with high resolution (as was done in Flock & Mignone (2021) with PLUTO and Lagrangian dust particles and Ostertag & Flock (2025) with FARGO3D), one can see that both the fluid approach and the particle approach for dust allow for studying this instability and its clumping behavior. This raises the question of how the two approaches compare, and what their respective strengths and limitations are in capturing the stream-ing instability and its clumping behavior. A first comprehensive work, aiming to compare stream-ing instability across di ff erent hydrodynamical codes and dust approaches, is provided by the PFITS + 1 collaboration (Baronett et al. 2026) using unstratified shearing box simula-tions. In our work, we compare the particle and fluid approaches for dust in global models with vertical stratification, similar to those in Flock & Mignone (2021). However, we also aim to com-pare the influence of di ff erent gas Riemann solvers and spatial reconstruction schemes, as well as the di ff erence between two di ff erent hydrodynamics codes (PLUTO, FARGO3D). The structure of the paper is as follows: First, in section 2, we describe the disk model, followed by an investigation of the in-fluence of di ff erent reconstruction schemes on simulations with dust particles and a fixed resolution in section 3.1. Next, in sec-tion 3.2, we compare three di ff erent and well-known Riemann solvers, again for dust particle simulations with a fixed resolu-tion. In section 3.4, we compare PLUTO simulations with dust fluid and dust particles, while in section 3.5, we compare simu-lations between PLUTO and FARGO3D, both with a dust fluid. After investigating the convergence of particle and fluid setups with respect to dust scale height, velocities, and vorticity, we compute the performance of the di ff erent hydro codes and nu-merical setups. We conclude this work with a discussion and a conclusion in sections 4 and 5, respectively.

> 2.3. Boundary conditions

In the following, we briefly describe the boundary conditions applied in this work. Particles are simply removed from the sim-ulation once they cross a boundary (both inner and outer) in any of the directions and leave the computational domain. For gas and when dust is treated as a fluid, the following conditions in PLUTO (FARGO3D) are used: in the radial direction, the gas and dust hydro variables have zero gradients (symmetric bound-ary conditions), but neither gas nor dust is allowed to flow into the computational domain. This is achieved by setting the radial velocity at the radial interface between the computational do-main and the boundary to zero if the velocity points into the do-main. In the meridional direction, we extrapolate the exponential gas density profile with a zero gradient (using symmetric bound-ary conditions for the dust density). For the gas (dust) velocities, we again use a zero-gradient (symmetric boundary conditions) approach to prevent gas and dust from flowing into the computa-tional domain. See also section 2.1 in Flock & Mignone (2021) and Appendix A in Ostertag & Flock (2025). 

> 2.4. Damping Zones

In all dust-fluid and particle simulations, we damp the gas den-sity in the entire domain back to the initial condition to pre-vent gas mass loss, following Flock & Mignone (2021) (see their section 2.3 and Eq. 15). As Flock & Mignone (2021) has shown in their Appendix C, the influence of the damping timescale on the gas density is small in this regime. Additionally, we add damping or bu ff er zones at the inner and outer domain edges: For all PLUTO simulations, we apply Eq. 14 from Flock & Mignone (2021) (with Rb = 9.3 AU,

4. Discussion 

4.1. Midplane dust density component 

The purpose of sections 3.1 and 3.2 was to evaluate the im-pact of numerical accuracy on the outcome of density structures. More specifically, we investigated the influence of the spatial re-construction scheme and di ff erent Riemann solvers in PLUTO particle simulations. Both a higher-order reconstruction scheme and a more accurate gas Riemann solver allow the dust to set-tle more strongly to the midplane, showing an additional mid-

Article number, page 13 A&A proofs: manuscript no. main 

plane dust density component for intermediate resolutions. For Riemann solvers, this result can be counterintuitive: A more dif-fusive solver should lead to lower gas velocities, which are not able to mix the dust e ffi ciently in the vertical direction. With Fig. 4, we have shown that at the midplane a more di ff usive solver, like HLL, systematically leads to a broader velocity distribution with larger velocities, compared to a more accurate solver. How-ever, the numerical origin is not yet clear. The di ff erence be-tween HLL and HLLC / ROE is that the latter two do preserve discontinuities of momentum in the transverse direction to the grid cell surface, which are smeared out with HLL. This smear-ing has a direct influence on the gas vorticity distribution (see bottom panel in Fig. A.1), as HLL leads to more frequent values around zero and therefore weaker vortices compared to ROE. We find a similar result between PLM and PPM: a more accu-rate scheme yields higher vorticity values and narrower velocity distributions, such that the dust density shows an additional mid-plane component. Whether and how vorticity explains the di ff er-ence in dust settlement is also unclear, and future research on this aspect is necessary. In the midplane dust-to-gas ratio panels in Fig. 2 and 3, we have noticed a shorter dust settling time for a less accurate spatial reconstruction scheme and Riemann solver before turbulence is able to pu ff the disk up. The influence of this aspect on the presence of an additional midplane dust den-sity component remains unclear and needs to be investigated in future work. We also noted that the midplane dust-to-gas ratio for a higher-order spatial reconstruction scheme (PPM) is very sim-ilar to that for a more accurate Riemann solver (HLLC, ROE), with values just above 1. This raises the question of whether the lower truncation errors of a piecewise parabolic reconstruction scheme are su ffi cient to reduce the smearing of the discontinuity in transverse momentum (or numerical di ff usivity) introduced by the HLL scheme. This momentum discontinuity, however, is conserved with HLLC. One could test whether similar results can be achieved when choosing the HLL solver in combination with a linear reconstruction scheme and an even higher resolu-tion than in this work, compared to the HLLC solver with linear reconstruction and a moderate resolution. Despite HLLC being able to show the additional midplane component of the dust density, it also exhibits large di ff u-sive errors when the gas flow is subsonic (see section 2.4.3 in Leidi et al. 2024). This condition is fulfilled in our simu-lations, with typical Mach numbers below 1%. For this rea-son, the previously mentioned work follows the approach from Minoshima & Miyoshi (2021) and presents a modified version of HLLC, called low-dissipation HLLC or LHLLC, where the ratio of numerical di ff usion to the amplitude of pressure fluctu-ations remains independent of the Mach number. At a low addi-tional cost, this modified solver can improve the accuracy of low-Mach-number simulations. However, its performance in coupled gas-dust simulations needs to be tested in future work. 

> 4.2. Influence of vertical domain size

The vertical domain size of 0.2 Hg, that we have used in sections 3.1 and 3.2, is the same as used in Flock & Mignone (2021). This size is smaller than the 0.4 Hg used in Li & Youdin (2021), who showed that smaller domain sizes produce larger dust scale heights. We have verified this for our disk setup in Fig. 5 for particle and fluid simulations using PLUTO. This means that the true dust layers for the di ff erent spatial reconstructions and Riemann solvers will be smaller, hence we expect to find even higher miplane dust-to-gas ratios and miplane dust densities. However, all setups ( conf01 - conf05 ) are a ff ected in the same way, and any di ff erences in the midplane dust-to-gas ratio or maximum dust density should arise only from di ff erent recon-struction schemes or Riemann solvers. Therefore, one should see these results only as a relative comparison. To further emphasize the importance of a meaningful selec-tion of the vertical domain size, we briefly discuss the results from Li et al. (2018). While clumping remains persistent across nearly all their vertical boundary conditions (VBCs) and box sizes, the particle scale height Hp varies widely across di ff er-ent VBCs and box sizes (see their Fig. 4). But within a specific time window they considered (called stage 3, indicated by two cyan vertical lines in Fig. 4), Hp is similar across VBCs when 

Lz = 0.8Hg. This result shows that increasing Lz reduces arti-facts from vertical boundary conditions. Since they are working with a Stokes number 30 times larger than in the current work, stronger dust settling decreases the fraction of the computational domain influenced by particles, e ff ectively mimicking a larger vertical domain. 

> 4.3. Effect of the outer buffer zone

The 2D density structures for FARGO3D and the PLUTO fluid approach also showed that insu ffi cient dust is delivered from the outer bu ff er zone, leading to a drop in midplane dust density. This was not observed in particle simulations, which show even denser clumps forming at the outer edge of the domain. Even though both approaches use the same dust refilling technique in the outer bu ff er zone, as mentioned in Flock & Mignone (2021), the results di ff er in their e ff ects on the dust disk. In appendix B, we show that for particle simulations, we gain dust mass in time, while for fluid simulations (PLUTO and FARGO3D), we lose dust mass. In order to make a reliable comparison of quan-tities like midplane dust-to-gas ratio or maximum dust density, one has to neglect the radial domain in which boundary e ff ects are visible. We did this by neglecting the simulation results out-side 10.4 AU. In Fig. 9 (first and last row), we saw that for the less accurate PLUTO setup conf06 and resolutions of 512 × 128 1024 × 256, a significant amount of dust was lost at 100 orbits, despite using the refilling in the outer bu ff er zone. This results in a weaker interaction via drag, therefore reducing the strength of resulting instabilities. Hence, the direct comparison between the three setups remains di ffi cult. In future work, a more e ff ective dust refilling mechanism needs to be implemented. One possi-bility is to fix the dust mass flux at the outer boundary, as e.g. in Schoonenberg & Ormel (2017) or Wang et al. (2025) and refer-ences therein, instead of refilling the dust in the damping zone. 

> 4.4. Influence of temporal and spatial accuracy

When we revisit the comparison between the more accurate PLUTO fluid and FARGO3D simulations, the question arises as to why the density structure of both codes is similar. FARGO3D achieves 1 st -order time accuracy (as shown in Fig. 2 of Benítez-Llambay et al. 2018, for a set of six fluids) and 2nd -order spatial accuracy. In contrast, the corresponding con-figurations of PLUTO are 3 rd - and 2 nd -order accurate in time for gas and dust (for dust with explicit dust integrator see Ap-pendix A1 in Ziampras et al. 2025), respectively. Their spatial accuracy is of fourth order for gas due to the spatial reconstruc-tion scheme and of 2 nd order for dust (see Appendix A2.3 in Ziampras et al. 2025). Mixtures of di ff erent orders for tempo-ral and spatial accuracy will presumably average out, but they 

> Article number, page 14 D. Ostertag et al.: Testing particle and fluid approaches for global streaming instability simulations

should be higher than for FARGO3D. This brings up numeri-cal di ff usivity for FARGO3D due to lower-order schemes and a low number of cells per dust scale height at the lowest resolu-tion (128 /(0 .4Hg) and a dust scale height of 0.0127 Hg results in roughly 4 cells per Hd). However, in this case, we should also see numerical di ff usivity dominating the disk structure in the less accurate setup of PLUTO ( conf06 ), where we find a temporal average dust scale height of 0.0044 Hg (see Table 3). An alternative explanation is that FARGO3D better resolves the instability’s turbulent structures. This may not be surprising as FARGO3D is a ZEUS-like code. Despite Seligman & Laughlin (2017) arguing that both PLUTO and ZEUS su ff er from severe vortex dissipation, their Fig. 3 shows that the vortex dissipation is lower for ZEUS than for PLUTO. In Fig. 11 and Fig. 14, we have shown that FARGO3D is indeed able to produce larger vor-ticity values compared to PLUTO, which supports their state-ment of vortex dissipation. At the lowest resolution, the root-mean-square vorticity value for PLUTO particles is comparable to that of FARGO3D, even though the dust disk for particles has a smaller scale height. This shows that vorticity does not explain the di ff erence in scale height between the two codes at the lowest resolution. As we have shown in Fig. 13, the low-resolution dis-tribution of the dust meridional velocity in FARGO3D is much closer to the high-resolution distribution, whereas for PLUTO fluid and particles, it initially deviates significantly. Why the ve-locity distributions for FARGO3D are already close to the final distribution remains an open question and needs to be identified with future work. 

> 4.5. Dust depleted voids

Both PLUTO fluid and FARGO3D simulations indicate a con-vergence of the dust scale height for the broad density com-ponent, as well as a vanishing midplane component that is re-placed by a field of many dust-depleted voids. In Appendix H, we have examined the dust-depleted voids in more detail. Track-ing the trajectories of 8 voids by hand over 1 orbit, we find that some voids tend to migrate in a certain direction, while others stay close to the location where we started tracking. Performing a temporal average of the dust density over di ff erent time win-dows, we find that no void structure survives a time window of 2 orbits. While voids, moving in a fixed direction, do not con-tribute much to the average density field, other voids that tend to circularize around a fixed position will dissolve after around 2 orbits. Considering the duration of our simulations of about 100 orbits, these voids cannot be considered as persistent structures and undergo merging with other voids or dissolve. 

> 4.6. Effect of particle sampling

Because we fixed the number of particles in our simulations de-spite increasing the resolution, the particle sampling per cell de-creases. This should enhance the deviation from results with a dust fluid. In addition, as dust particles settle towards the mid-plane, the particle-resolved region shrinks, reducing the strength of the streaming instability and the induced turbulence in the upper layers. In this way, dust particles cannot reach higher alti-tudes. In order to test the impact of proper particle sampling, we created a new setup of conf05 , using four times the number of particles. This yields an average of 9.9 particles per cell. How-ever, as we have shown in Fig. 6 (see the bottom panel for a res-olution of 4096 × 1024) and Fig. 8, a higher number of particles does not significantly change characteristic properties such as dust scale height, maximum dust concentration, or velocity dis-tribution. Even though we have not adapted the number of parti-cles for the highest-resolution run, which would require evolving 16.6 ×10 7 particles, we do not expect to see a significant change in the dust disk properties. 

> 4.7. Convergence of dust scale height and vorticity distribution

The convergence study of the dust scale height for particle meth-ods in Fig. 12 indicates that the broad density component at the highest resolution should be comparable to those of e.g., FARGO3D. However, from Fig. 9 and 6, we see that the disk is smaller in the particle-method runs. Because we determine the broad dust density component with a Gaussian fit, where we can only use the outer wings of the dust density distribution, a lack of data at the midplane can introduce a certain level of uncer-tainty in the fit parameters, especially for the dust scale height. Correcting the fit parameters could lead to a smaller dust scale height across all setups. The fact that both codes show a power-law trend in the width of the vorticity distribution with resolution (see Fig. 14) indi-cates that this quantity has not yet saturated. We expect to see an even broader vorticity distribution with even larger values when the resolution is further increased. This could also mean that the vortex size increases. 

> 4.8. Comparison to other works

In Section 3.1, we computed dust scale heights for our particle simulations, which are on the order of 0.01 Hg. These values are significantly larger than those reported in Flock & Mignone (2021) (they receive values close to 0.003 Hg) for the same phys-ical setup, but with a numerical issue that led to stronger settling. Other works, for instance Lim et al. (2025), report values of a similar order of magnitude to ours, indicating that we resolved the numerical issue in the current work. Lim et al. (2025) have already done similar Lagrangian par-ticle simulations using the ATHENA code in combination with a shearing box setup. When they increased the resolution, they also found a broadening of the midplane component, which is about to vanish in their highest-resolution run with 5120 /Hg

(comparable to our simulation with 8192 × 2048). See also Fig. G.1 for a comparison of their vertical dust-to-gas ratios. The rea-son we find a midplane component for particles at the highest resolution in the current work, but they do not in the previous work, is that our domain size in the vertical direction is larger (0.4 Hg vs. 0.2 Hg). Both the results in section 3.3 for our work and Fig. G.2 for their work show that a larger vertical domain size leads to a thinner dust disk, which enhances the midplane component. Nevertheless, the results in Lim et al. (2025) support that, if we increase the resolution further in particle simulations, we may see the same converged state for particles as for fluids, but this needs further checking. In addition, Lim et al. (2025) has shown that although in-creases in resolution broaden the midplane component, corre-sponding to a decrease in midplane dust density, it is beneficial in forming dense dust clumps that reach the critical Hill density (see their section 3.4 for details). This could be because turbu-lence and dust-depleted voids become better resolved, as they can e ffi ciently concentrate dust. The e ff ect of this dust collec-tion was already observed in Yang & Zhu (2021), Gerosa et al.  

> Article number, page 15 A&A proofs: manuscript no. main

(2023), and recently studied in Umurhan et al. (2026). The lat-ter work connected the clumping to the turbulent concentra-tion of particles. Based on our dust-fluid simulation results, this turbulence-driven dust concentration may be valid for both dust approaches. It is interesting that Hd of the midplane component increases with resolution, but not that of the broad component. It may be possible that each component traces a di ff erent turbulence-driven mechanism. For example, the midplane Hd could trace stream-ing turbulence, whereas the broad component could trace shear-driven instabilities, such as KHI, which should be most active near the top of the dust layer, if present. If this is true, it suggests that a critical resolution for convergence is higher for streaming turbulence. Because we find strong indications of convergence in the broad component, this raises the question of whether we can resolve the vertically shearing streaming instability Lin (2021). Lim et al. (2025) already argued that this particular resolution may not be high enough. It would be necessary to further in-crease the resolution to determine whether we again observe a significant increase in the dust scale height for the broad density component. Our results of vanishing midplane density component raise another question: How do results from works like Li & Youdin (2021) or Lim et al. (2026), who constrain the parameter space in (total dust-to-gas mass ratio Z, Stokes number τs), where disks are expected to show strong clumping, change with even higher resolutions? This issue was already addressed in Section 4.2 of Lim et al. (2026), which also includes a comparison with 3D simulations. Our results of vanishing midplane dust density component and a density structure covered with dust-depleted voids are con-sistent with the work of Yang et al. (2017) using the PENCIL code and Lagrangian particles. This could either be due to their smaller domain size (0.2 Hg instead of our 0.4 Hg, which can artifically increase the scale height as shown in section 3.3), or it indicates the midplane density component is merely a transient feature that disappears when the numerical schemes are su ffi -ciently accurate, or the (particle) resolution is su ffi ciently large. Assuming it’s a transient feature, we expect this component to vanish in PLUTO particle simulations as well, thereby demon-strating convergence. However, even at our highest resolution, we can still see this midplane component. The work of Baronett et al. (2026) has carried out acomprehensive comparison of di ff erent hydrodynamical codes that treat dust as particles and as a fluid. For unstratified shearing-box simulations of streaming instability (test BA from Johansen & Youdin 2007), they find good agreement between the two approaches, where maximum dust densities for fluids are generally only 30% lower than for particles. We, however, find maximum dust densities to be at least 50% smaller for fluid simulations (comparing the maximum dust density for the high-est resolution for the setups conf05 and conf07 in Table 3). Considering their resolution of 1024 cells per gas and dust scale height (due to an unstratified problem), we expect to find more similar values as we increase our resolution and expect a vanish-ing midplane component for particles. In Fig. F.1, we present the cumulative distribution function of the dust-to-gas ratio, indicat-ing that once the midplane component vanishes, the probabilities are reduced significantly. Our plot can be compared to, e.g., Fig. 12 in Baronett et al. (2026). 

> 4.9. Performance benchmark

In Appendix I, we investigate the performance of fluid and par-ticle simulations performed with FARGO3D (tested separately on CPUs and one GPU) and PLUTO (tested on CPUs). The val-ues presented in Table I.1 serve as a benchmark for the com-munity and future works by providing order-of-magnitude esti-mates. The FARGO3D simulations have a factor of two higher vertical resolution. This should not a ff ect the code’s performance too much as both the full simulation time ∆tSim and the resolu-tion Nr · Nθ in Eq. I.1 should increase by about a factor of 2. However, higher resolution can lead to longer data read times if more disk space is reserved for a simulation and the data is more spread out. This should reduce the performance to some degree, which we did not test here. 

> 4.10. Effect of numerical dissipation and limitations of the model

We briefly comment on our choice of not including an explicit physical dissipation scale into our setups (in PLUTO, we do not use gas viscosity or dust di ff usion at all, and in FARGO3D, the 

α-viscosity and dust di ff usion module is used with α=0). When working with a fluid, it is recommended to account for a small, nonzero, and controllable scale of dissipation. For gas, this could be, e.g., the gas’s kinematic viscosity, whereas for dust, which does not have a viscosity, the analogous quantity is dust di ff u-sivity. If this is not considered, the dissipation scale is entirely determined by the grid cell size and the related truncation er-rors of the numerical solution. Since we are working with a non-linear system of equations, we cannot rule out the possibility that these truncation errors influence the solutions. This complicates our resolution comparison between FARGO3D and PLUTO, as grid-scale numerical artifacts could at some point dominate the density structure and quantities like, e.g., the dust-to-gas ratio. If a dissipation scale is considered, it needs to fulfill two aspects: first, it needs to be small enough not to influence the process of interest, in our case, the streaming instability. Second, the dissi-pation should have an impact at low and high resolutions. Com-bining both e ff ects is typically di ffi cult to achieve. In addition, in our FARGO3D simulations, we enable dust di ff usion despite setting the α-viscosity to zero. Because the dust di ff usivity is calculated using α, we e ff ectively get pure numer-ical di ff usion from using the module. However, the numerical di ff usion is expected to be very small as the dust di ff usivity is identically zero. To assess how much numerical di ff usion a ff ects the results, we have compared dust scale heights in Appendix C, where the dust di ff usion module is not used in the lowest-resolution run. Within the level of variations, the results are the same. In Fig. 6 and 7, we have investigated the di ff erence between PLUTO fluid and particle simulations for an increasing resolu-tion. As we have stated at the beginning of this work, the purpose of our investigations was not to determine whether the di ff er-ences in dust scale height and midplane dust-to-gas ratio arise from additional numerical di ff usivity in the fluid approach or from the fundamental distinction that particle methods allow for crossing trajectories. Therefore, such tests need to be done in future work. 

5. Conclusion 

We performed spherical 2D, isothermal, axisymmetric simula-tions of gas and dust in a global patch setup using the hydrody-

D. Ostertag et al.: Testing particle and fluid approaches for global streaming instability simulations 

namic codes PLUTO and FARGO3D. The goal is to investigate how dust structures formed by the non-linear streaming instabil-ity generally depend on the dust treatment (fluid versus particle), resolution, and on the choice of gas Riemann solver and spatial reconstruction scheme. The purpose was not to identify or quan-tify the pure e ff ect of, e.g., having crossing trajectories in particle simulations. Our results demonstrate that the choice of numeri-cal scheme and resolution significantly a ff ects the evolution of the nonlinear streaming instability in stratified disk simulations. Increasing the resolution has clearly shown that both PLUTO fluid and FARGO3D simulations converge towards a disk state described by a single Gaussian dust density component with a dust scale height between 0.0129 Hg and 0.0141 Hg and a ran-dom field of small dust-depleted voids. Using the highest reso-lution of 8192 × 2048, corresponding to a resolution of 5120 /Hg,we therefore find good agreement between codes (for fluid ap-proach) for a resolution of 66 cells per dust scale height. At intermediate resolutions, an additional midplane component is visible, a transient feature that disappears for su ffi ciently high numbers of cells. As this additional component remains present in our highest-resolution simulation with PLUTO particles but shows an increasing dust scale height, we conclude that either an even higher grid resolution or more than 1 .6 · 10 8 particles are necessary to achieve convergence towards the same density structure as in the fluid case. If the resolution (either due to the grid or to particles) is con-tinuously increased in a simulation using, e.g., an HLL Riemann solver and a low-order reconstruction scheme, we expect to see the midplane component at su ffi ciently high resolution. This fea-ture will then disappear for an even higher number of grid cells to show convergence. Aligning with well-known concepts, using a more accurate Riemann solver and /or a higher-order spatial re-construction scheme, one can e ff ectively reduce the necessary resolution to reach a converged state. However, higher-order nu-merical schemes alone do not guarantee convergence. In inviscid simulations in particular, there is no physical dissipation scale, so each grid refinement allows new, smaller scales to develop at the grid scale. These small-scale vortices can produce steeper gradients in density and vorticity, potentially altering the struc-tures of interest. All scales relevant to a given structure must therefore be resolved first. Only then can higher-order schemes eff ectively reduce numerical errors and yield converged results for those scales. When we compare PLUTO fluid and FARGO3D simula-tions, we find that the FARGO3D dust density structure is com-parable to a more accurate setup of PLUTO, using, e.g., a ROE Riemann solver and a 4 th -order reconstruction scheme. However, FARGO3D consistently reported larger dust scale heights. Com-pared to PLUTO, we showed that the gas velocity distribution at small resolution is much closer to the distribution at the highest resolution In general, the di ff erences we find between the two ap-proaches are consistent with other code-comparison work, e.g., Baronett et al. (2026), and are smaller than the uncertainties in, e.g., the true Stokes number of dust grains. For the average dust-to-gas ratio at the midplane, the PLUTO fluid and particle results are only 1.3 and 1.9 times the FARGO3D result, respectively, at the highest resolution. For the maximum dust density, the corre-sponding factors are 1.2 (PLUTO fluid) and 2.8 (PLUTO parti-cles) relative to FARGO3D. Given that the fluid approach can be at least 3.5 times faster than the particle approach, we can use it as a viable method to study streaming instability in global disk setups.

From the performance results in Table I.1, we observe the following: Compared to the highest-resolution PLUTO fluid simulations, the PLUTO particle simulations at the same resolu-tion show a performance reduction by a factor of 3.5–4. For dust-fluid simulations, the number of cells that are advanced in time is twice the given resolution; e.g., at a resolution of 4096 × 512, we have approximately 4 · 10 6 cells. For a dust particle simulation at the same resolution, we have approximately 2 · 10 6 gas cells and 1 ·10 7 particles to evolve. Therefore, the number of elements to evolve for particle simulation is roughly a factor of 3 higher than for pure fluid simulations. Since the number of particles is held fixed while the grid resolution is varied, the performance gap between particle-based and pure-fluid PLUTO simulations widens at lower resolutions. This is because the particle update per time step, ∆t, dominates the computational cost and occurs at a fixed rate (total particle updates per second), independent of resolution. As each update advances the entire gas grid once, the performance in cells s −1 scales with the total number of grid cells times the particle update rate. This value drops at low res-olutions (e.g., 512 × 64) since few cells are updated per second. In contrast, in pure fluid simulations, cells advance by ∆t, so the single-cell update rate directly determines the maximum number of cells that can be updated in one second.
