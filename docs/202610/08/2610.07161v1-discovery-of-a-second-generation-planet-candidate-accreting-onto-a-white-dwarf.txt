# ABSTRACT 

Many white dwarfs accrete the debris of disrupted planetary bodies, detected in the form of metal-enrichment in their atmospheres, dusty or gaseous circumstellar discs, and photometric transits from debris. The composition of accreted debris has been shown to be diverse yet overall closely resembles Solar System bodies. We report the discovery of a white dwarf accreting material that is unlike any Solar System object. The atmosphere of HS 0209+0832 is strongly enriched in trans-iron elements including zinc, copper, and niobium but depleted in the canonical rock-forming elements silicon and iron. The composition of the accreted object is consistent with a candidate second-generation planet, formed from the stellar material ejected during the giant phase. The key abundance signature which differentiates it from a first-generation planet is the high enrichment of s-process elements. Photospheric helium and the absence of terrestrial rock-forming elements implies the white dwarf is accreting from the escaped atmosphere of a photo-evaporating giant planet candidate. This is further supported by the detection of a sinusoidal photometric period of 4.399 ± 0.026 days at an amplitude of 0.120 ± 0.018 %, which we attribute to thermal emission phase variability from a planetary day-night cycle or a transiting cometary tail of an evaporating gas giant. arXiv:2610.07161v1 [astro-ph.EP] 5 Oct 2026 Main 

HS 0209+0832 is a hot, young white dwarf with an effective temperature of Teff ≈ 35 , 000 K, a cooling age of tcool ≈ 5 Myr, and a hydrogen-dominated atmosphere with a ≃ 1% trace of helium 1. Such a mixed composition is unusual for a white dwarf of this temperature because helium should sink out of the atmosphere within months 2, 3 . A far-ultraviolet spectrum obtained in 1999 with the Space Telescope Imaging Spectrograph (STIS) onboard the Hubble Space Telescope (HST ) revealed the presence of carbon, aluminium, silicon, calcium, titanium, nickel, and zinc in the atmosphere 4, as well as ≃ 100 lines that could not be identified. Whereas the presence of helium and metals indicates ongoing accretion, the nature of that external source remained unexplained 4.In the 25 years since that study was published, the origin of photospheric metals detected in many other white dwarfs has been unambiguously confirmed as planetary debris 5, 6 . This motivated a re-analysis of the observations of HS 0209+0832 obtained with HST /STIS, the Far Ultraviolet Spectroscopic Explorer (FUSE ) and the Ultraviolet and Visual Echelle Spectrograph (UVES) mounted on the Very Large Telescope (VLT), along with the broad-band photometry from PanSTARRS and the Gaia 

parallax (see Methods). In a first step, we determined Teff = 35 , 800 ± 500 K, a surface gravity of log g = 7.90 ± 0.02, and a helium abundance of log (He /H) = −1.90 ± 0.20 (see Extended Data Fig. 1 and Extended Data Tab. 1 ). With these parameters fixed, we computed extensive grids of synthetic white dwarf spectra 7 using detailed lists of metal transitions in the ultraviolet (Extended Data Tab. 2 ). We matched most of the previously unidentified lines to transitions of copper and niobium, with the latter identified using five Nb III lines and 57 Nb IV lines (see Fig. 1). We carefully examined archival FUSE 8 spectra of 33 known metal-enriched white dwarfs with temperatures 20 , 000 K < Teff < 77 , 000 K and confirm non-detections for niobium within that sample. We measured the photospheric abundances (see Methods) for nine metals and calculated upper limits on 15 metals (see Extended Data Tab. 3 ). We excluded any lines originating from the interstellar medium (ISM) in our fit (Extended Data Tab. 4 ). At the temperature of HS 0209+0832, radiative levitation can support certain elements against gravitational diffusion. We investigated this effect for all detected elements, as well as for N, P, and S for which we determined upper limits (see Methods). We selected these volatiles because they are commonly detected in hot white dwarfs 8, 9 , thus it is unusual they are not observed in HS 0209+0832. We find that, for the measured abundances, radiative levitation is negligible for Ca, S, Ti, Ni, Cu, Zn, and Nb. Assuming a steady-state between accretion into, and diffusion out of the white dwarf atmosphere, we computed the diffusion fluxes for each element. We adopt these mass fluxes as being representative of the material accreting onto HS 0209+0832, as they correct the photospheric abundances for the differential sinking time scales of individual metals (Extended Data Tab. 3 ). In contrast, the abundances of C, N, Al, Si, and P are subject to radiative support. These elements must still have an external origin 10, 11 , but our analysis cannot determine their mass fractions within the accreting material. We compare the abundances of the parent body accreting onto HS 0209+0832 to the entire sample of white dwarfs accreting rocky material 12 and to Solar System meteorites 13 , but find no compositional matches (see Fig. 2). We find that the trans-iron elements are strongly enhanced with respect to calcium compared to Solar abundances, in particular niobium which is over three orders of magnitudes more abundant than in the Sun. Most enriched white dwarfs are depleted in volatile elements such as C, N, and S, but a minority are volatile enriched, which can be due to accreting either icy bodies 16 or the atmosphere of a giant planet 17 . The only volatile detected in HS 0209+0832 is carbon (Extended Data Fig. 2 ), with a high photospheric abundance which is compatible with fiducial predictions from radiative levitation. However, there are both theoretical and empirical reasons to believe that our fiducial radiative levitation predictions overestimate the importance of this effect. Most importantly, in HS 0209+0832, the predicted abundances for nitrogen and phosphorus due to radiative levitation are ≃ 2 dex higher than the upper limits we derived. As the physics of radiative levitation does not vary from element to element, this implies that carbon must be significantly enhanced within the parent body with respect to other volatile species. In contrast, volatiles such as N, O, P, and S are significantly depleted relative to solar and comet Halley abundances (see Fig. 2), so HS 0209+0832 cannot be accreting a volatile-rich icy body. The accretion of helium, the depletion in traditional rock-forming elements, and the enhancement in carbon and trans-iron species implies that the parent body formed in an environment unlike that of a typical proto-planetary disc. In particular, the high carbon and niobium abundances can be explained by the accretion of second-generation planetary material formed within a disc established from a small fraction of the wind-driven mass loss that terminated the life of the asymptotic giant branch (AGB) progenitor of HS 0209+0832 23 . Inspecting the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS ) observations of HS 0209+0832 (Extended Data Fig. 4 ), we detected a sinusoidal signal with a period of 4 .399 ± 0.026 days and an amplitude of 0 .120 ± 0.018 % (see Fig. 4, Extended Data Fig. 5 , and Methods). Typical white dwarf spin periods strongly cluster around ≃ 1.25 days, with only one out of 20 systems observed with Kepler having a rotation period longer than four days 35 . The TESS photometry can be explained by a giant planet with an orbital separation of 0.04 AU. Other known close-in white dwarf planets span semimajor axes of 

≃ 0.02 − 0.07 au 17, 36 . The amplitude of the photometric modulation is consistent with the phase curve expected from a young and strongly irradiated phase-locked giant planet (see Methods). The large incident flux of high-energy photons will drive mass loss from this planet 17, 37 which will subsequently accrete onto the white dwarf surface, resulting in the metal-enrichment of HS 0209+0832. For a planetary radius of 1 R Jup , using energy-limited atmospheric escape calculations 37, 38 and the expected EUV and X-ray emission of the white dwarf, we computed an evaporation rate of ˙ Mevap ≈ 0.11 × 10 13 g s −1 for a planetary mass of 1 M Jup and ˙Mevap ≈ 1.43 × 10 13 g s −1 for 13 M Jup . This is larger than our measured accretion rate onto the white dwarf of ˙Macc > 4.45 × 10 8. The difference between the evaporation rate and accretion rate is because the white dwarf accretion rate does not include a contribution from hydrogen, helium, and undetected metals. Thermohaline mixing could increase the accretion rate by an order of magnitude but no existing calculations go up to the temperature of HS 0209+0832 39, 40 . Additionally, a substantial proportion of mass is likely ejected from the system by radiation pressure and the dynamical energy injected into the evaporated material from the planetary orbit 17 .An alternative explanation to the photometric variability is a cometary tail, similar to that seen in evaporating planets around main sequence stars 41 . This is further supported by HS 0209+0832 being spectroscopically variable, with the helium abundance varying by a factor of 2 − 3, but over an unknown timescale 42 . The mass out-flowing from the planet might cross the line-of-sight, explaining the variability in the observed helium abundances, and the unusual structure of the He II 1,640 Å line 4, which is visible in Fig. 1.

# Methods 

Spectroscopic observations 

HS 0209+0832 was observed using HST /STIS in the far-ultraviolet (FUV) on 1999 January 10 4. Two exposures of 2,110 s and 1,140 s were taken using the MAMA detector and the E140M grating, covering the wavelength range 1 , 150 − 1, 710 Å with a resolving power of R ≃ 45 , 800. We used the Hubble Advanced Spectral Products (HASP) spectrum, which combined the individual echelle orders into a single spectrum and co-added the two exposures. The resulting spectrum contains over 200 metal absorption lines, a deep Lyman-alpha line at 1,215 Å, and a He II line at 1,640 Å. There are gaps in the spectrum at 1, 416 − 1, 419 Å and 1 , 438 .5 − 1, 442 Å because this data is flagged as unreliable, which was caused by a large blemish from a shadow on the flat field correction from the repeller wire. The only absorption features present in this region are from metals which have many other lines in the spectrum and hence these gaps do not affect our results. In addition, two short exposures were taken with the G230LB (600 s) and G430L (320 s) gratings, covering the wavelengths 1, 685 − 3, 065 Å and 2 , 900 − 5, 700 Å with resolving powers of R ≃ 700 and R ≃ 500. The low resolution of these gratings prevents the detection of metal lines, but we used these data for measuring the white dwarf parameters. Further FUV spectroscopy of HS 0209+0832 was taken with FUSE on 2002 December 11. The LWRS aperture was used covering a wavelength range of 925 − 1, 180 Å with an exposure time of 4,986 s. The spectra across the four channels were combined, with a resolving power of R ≃ 20 , 000. The FUSE spectrum contains dozen of metal lines, several hydrogen lines, and two helium lines. Finally, HS 0209+0832 was observed with the Ultraviolet and Visual Echelle Spectrograph (UVES) mounted on the Very Large Telescope (VLT) 51 on 2000 July 12 − 15. Three 900 s exposures were obtained covering the wavelength range 3, 300 − 6, 690 Å at an average resolving power of R ≃ 19 , 155. There is no data in the region 4 , 560 − 4, 580 Å due to a gap between the detectors. No metal lines are detected in the UVES spectrum, which is unsurprising given the modest quality of that data, and as the transitions of highly ionised metals are much weaker in the optical compared to the FUV. However, we used the hydrogen and helium lines to constrain the white dwarf parameters. 

White dwarf parameters 

We computed a finely spaced white dwarf model atmosphere grid 7 spanning effective temperatures of 30 , 000 ≤ Teff ≤ 40 , 000 K, surface gravities of 7 .50 ≤ log g ≤ 8.50, and a fixed helium abundance of log (He /H) = −2.00. An initial fit to the Pan-STARRS photometry and Gaia parallax (See Extended Data Table 1 ) resulted in a strong dependence on the assumed reddening, with 

Teff = 36 , 329 ± 970 K and log g = 7.918 ± 0.024 for zero reddening and Teff = 43 , 075 ± 1827 K and log g = 8.049 ± 0.030 for E(B −V ) = 0.01. Whereas the extinction towards HS 0209+0832 is low 52 (E (B −V ) < 0.01 ), this fit is subject to inherent uncertainties as the photometry only samples the Rayleigh-Jeans tail of the white dwarf spectrum. We also note that the uncertainties of the Pan-STARRS are unrealistically small, resulting in statistically poor fits. We therefore adopted a hybrid approach, measuring the effective temperature by fitting the Balmer lines in the VLT/UVES 

> 8/36

and G430L HST /STIS spectra, and measuring the surface gravity from the photometry and parallax. The influence of reddening in this hybrid method is negligible. Taking the weighted mean of the analysis of the three UVES spectra and one G430L STIS spectrum, with zero reddening, results in Teff = 35 , 845 ± 535 K and log g = 7.904 ± 0.015. The fit to the ultraviolet spectra with these parameters is excellent (see Extended Data Fig. 1 ), suggesting that they are well calibrated and that reddening is indeed negligible. We measured a helium abundance of log (He /H) = −1.90 ± 0.20 from the He I (4,026 Å, 4,473 Å, 4,714 Å, 4,923 Å, 5,016 Å, 5,877 Å) and He II (4,686 Å) lines in the UVES spectra and the He II (1,640 Å) line in the E140M STIS spectrum. The uncertainty in log (He /H) determined from individual lines is 0.2 dex which limits an assessment of the variability in the He abundance detected in the UVES and STIS spectra. 

Photospheric abundances 

With Teff , log g, and log (He /H) fixed to the values determined above (Extended Data Table 1 ), we generated grids of synthetic spectra for C, Al, Si, Ca, Ti, Ni, Cu, Zn, and Nb with abundances in the range −10 < log (Z/H) < −4 in steps of 0.25 dex. Unless otherwise stated, the models use atomic data from the Vienna Atomic Line Database 53–56 . Interpolating across these model grids we fitted the spectral regions of the HST /STIS and FUSE spectra which contain absorption lines of a given element using a χ2 minimization technique (see Extended Data Table 2 for the detected absorption features). We detect multiple ISM lines (see Extended Data Table 4 ) in the spectra, but the radial velocity of these lines differ significantly from the photospheric line velocity, so the former are clearly resolved and can be masked during the fitting procedure. After estimating the abundances for each element in this way, we then computed new model grids allowing one element to vary, and fixing all other elements at the value from the initial analysis, and fitted the HST /STIS and FUSE data again. This process was repeated until all abundances converged. We report the abundances determined as the weighted mean of the individual fits to each line (or group of lines), and the standard deviation as the uncertainty. To determine upper limits 57 for N, O, P, S, Cl, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, and Sr, we selected a region that contained a strong line in the model but is undetected in the observed spectrum. We vary the metal abundance by 0.01 dex, determining the χ2 each time. The χ2 values are then converted to a likelihood using Jeffrey’s prior and then the cumulative distribution function is computed. We set the upper limit to be the 99 per cent confidence interval. HS 0209+0832 was the first white dwarf with a detection of photospheric zinc 4. We identified 42 Zn III and 40 Zn IV lines in the STIS spectrum, and used updated atomic data 58 in our analysis. The zinc abundances determined from the different ionisation states agree (log (Zn /H) = −6.24 ± 0.21), but we find the overall abundance to be higher than previously determined 4

by ≈ 0.9 dex. We attribute this difference to improvements in model atmospheres and atomic data. Whereas several Cu IV lines were identified previously 4 discrepant atomic data prevented an unambiguous measurement of the copper abundance. Using the updated atomic data 59 , we find a copper abundance of log (Cu /H) = −6.46 ± 0.14 that provides a good fit to all detected lines, and hence a firm detection of photospheric copper. The niobium lines were identified using the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) atomic line database 60–62 .

All sufficiently strong lines predicted by the synthetic spectra match closely to those in the observed spectrum. We observe no discrepancy between the Nb III and Nb IV lines. The niobium abundance in the HST /STIS and FUSE spectra agree within errors. We note that fitting the C IV resonance doublet (1,548 and 1,550 Å) results in log (C/H) = −4.81 ± 0.06, i.e. ≃ 1.2 dex larger than log (C/H) = −6.04 ± 0.17 determined from the C II and C III lines, (see Extended Data Fig. 2 ). The overlap in wavelength between the HST /STIS and FUSE spectra allows independent measurements using the C III 1175 Å multiplet. The abundances determined from the two spectra taken ≈ 4 years apart agree within the uncertainties. This situation is reminiscent of GD 394, a similarly warm ( Teff = 35 , 700 K) white dwarf, in which high ionisation lines of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus were detected that were too strong compared to the photospheric abundances measured using low ionisation states 63 . Given that the high-ionisation lines in GD 394 have the same velocity as the lower ionisation photospheric transitions, their origin is most likely a layer of hot gas just above the white dwarf photosphere 63 . In HS 0209+0832, the C IV lines also have radial velocities that match the C II and C III lines, so we argue that the C IV lines must originate in gas very close to the photosphere as well. Such a hot “chromosphere” is to be expected as the white dwarf is accreting planetary material with relatively low local mass flow rates 64 , and has been detected in soft X-rays in the debris-enriched white dwarf G 29-38 65 .We find a similar discrepancy in the titanium abundances using the Ti IV (log (Ti /H) = −5.79 ± 0.12, using the Kurucz line lists 66 ) and Ti III (log (Ti /H) = −6.34 ± 0.23, using the VALD atomic data) lines. We detect a single Ti III line in the 

FUSE spectrum, which agrees with the abundance measured using the HST /STIS spectrum. Just as for carbon, the velocities for both ionisation states agree thus we suggest hot gas close to the white dwarf surface as the cause for the higher abundance determined from the higher ionisation lines. 

Radiative levitation 

At the effective temperature of HS 0209+0832, radiative levitation may provide support against gravitational settling for specific elements 2, 67–70 . According to previous work, among the elements we detect in the photosphere of HS 0209+0832, C, Si, and Al are likely susceptible to radiative support 70 , whereas Ca and Ni are not 68, 70 . The radiative support of Ti, Cu, Zn, and Nb in hot white dwarfs has not been assessed so far. We quantitatively estimated the support provided for each of the detected elements (C, Al, Si, Ti, Ca, Cu, Zn, Nb), and a number of elements with upper limits that are of specific interest (N, P, S, Fe). We used our atmosphere code 7 and followed a documented procedure 11 to compute atmosphere structures and synthetic spectra that include both the effects of atomic diffusion and radiative levitation. In this process, we kept Teff and log g fixed to the values in Extended Data Table 1 . In a first step, we computed the equilibrium abundances for each of the above elements, i.e. assuming no external accretion, depositing a small amount of the element within the atmosphere, and iterating the atmosphere structure until the gravity (downward) and radiative levitation (upward) result in a zero velocity of the element under consideration. We found that radiative support is entirely negligible for S, Ca, Ti, Fe, Ni, Cu, Zn, and Nb. The remaining elements are radiatively supported at equilibrium abundances of log (C/H) ≃ − 6.1, log (N/H) ≃ − 6.5, log (Al /H) ≃ − 7.2, log (Si /H) ≃ − 6.5 and log (P/H) ≃ − 6.3. In a final 

> 10/36

step, we computed the downward diffusion fluxes for the detected elements that are not affected by radiative levitation (Ca, Ti, Ni, Cu, Zn, Nb, see Extended Data Table 3 ). Because sinking timescales are so short ( ∼days), we assume that accretion is in the steady state phase, i.e. the downward diffusion flux is equal to the accretion rate. The primary conclusion of this analysis is that the material accreted by HS 0209+0832 is genuinely rich in s-process elements. Another important abundance anomaly to note is the very low Ni /Fe ≳ 2 (by number), compared to ≃ 0.02 in the Sun, CI chondrites and bulk Earth 14, 15 .In the case of low accretion rates it is expected that the photospheric abundances increase with time, saturating at or near the equilibrium value 11, 69, 70 . At higher rates the effect of radiative support becomes increasingly negligible and the photospheric abundances will reflect the steady-state downwards mass flux. We note that for C and Al the photospheric abundances, log (C/H) = −6.04 and log (Al /H) = −7.37, are close to the levels predicted by radiative levitation, −6.1 and 

−7.2, respectively. However, we find a photospheric log (Si /H) = −8.26 which is much lower than the radiative equilibrium support of −6.5. A key assumption in the models of radiative levitation is that the atmosphere is entirely radiative, i.e. devoid of bulk motion and the associated velocity fields. However, accretion inherently introduces a downward flux of material, and its effect on the atmosphere is currently not well understood. Some models predict large-scale convection due to thermohaline mixing 39, 71 , and whereas the physical validity of these models is not fully established 72 , it is entirely possible that a sufficiently large inflow of material interferes–and possibly weakens or fully suppresses–radiative levitation. In the absence of a full understanding of these complex processes, we proceed with the most conservative assumption, i.e. adopting the radiative support resulting from our atmosphere models as outlined above. With the above caveat in mind, we note that radiative support predicts log (N/H) = −6.5, compared to the upper limit on the photospheric abundance of log (N/H) < −8.36. Compared to solar abundances, log (C/N) = 0.6, and given that both elements have similarly strong support by radiative levitation, these numbers may suggest that the abundance of carbon is enhanced in the material accreted by HS 0209+0832. At this effective temperature, helium is not radiatively supported and sinks from the atmosphere on timescales of months 2, 3 .In fact, HS 0209+0832 is in the DB gap, a dearth of white dwarfs with atmospheric helium in the range 30 , 000 < Teff <

45 , 000 K 73 . We therefore attribute the presence of helium to active accretion. Our final conclusion is that HS 0209+0832 accretes material from an external source at a high rate of ˙Macc ,tot = 4.3 ×

10 8 g s −1 with a composition radically different to anything within the Solar System.

Interpretation of photometric period 

The photometric variability detected within the TESS data must have an origin that is either intrinsic to the white dwarf, or external, i.e. related to a companion. Variability in white dwarfs can be caused by pulsations or the combination of rotation and inhomogeneous surface brightness distributions. The latter may be related to the presence of magnetic fields 78 , or variations in atmospheric abundances across their surface 79 , or a combination of both. Given that helium abundance variations have been reported for HS 0209+0832 42 ,associating the photometric variability with inhomogeneities in the surface composition seems a plausible explanation. However, the period of the photometric variability in HS 0209+0832 of ≃ 4.4 days is much longer than those of the other white dwarfs exhibiting surface variations in their helium abundance, ranging from tens of minutes 79, 80 to at most ≃ 10 h 81, 82 . In fact, this period is much longer than the typical spin period of white dwarfs of ≃ 30 hours, which is well-established from Kepler 

asteroseismology 35 . Only one out of 20 stars in the Kepler sample had a rotation period above four days. Moreover, a number of white dwarfs showing surface inhomogeneities of helium are extremely massive ( > 1M⊙) and strongly magnetic ( B ≳ 1 MG), so could be the result of binary mergers, whereas HS 0209+0832 has an average mass (0 .61 M⊙) and no evidence of magnetism (B ≲ 30 kG). We now turn to the possibility that the photometric variability is extrinsic to the white dwarf,noting that the photometric period falls in between those of the two known close-in giant planets orbiting white dwarfs: WD 1856+534 ( P = 1.4 days 36 )and WD J0914+1914 ( P ≃ 10 days 17 ). No transits are detected in the TESS data, which places an upper limit on the orbital inclination of the planet of i ≲ 88 ◦. A period of 4 .40 ± 0.02 days is equivalent to a planet orbiting at d = 0.044 AU, calculated using Kepler’s third law. Therefore, we propose the hypothesis that the photometric variability may be caused by a young giant planet that has a heavy atmospheric haze layer on its dayside and a relatively clear nightside. The maximum of the photometric variability would then correspond to when we are seeing the hot intrinsic emission of the planet on its nightside, while the variability minimum would occur when the dayside is visible and we see a minimal reflection signal from the optically thick dayside haze later. As a consistency check on the haze-covered-dayside, clear-nightside hypothesis, we consider the production and destruction timescales of the haze. The EUV environment of this possible planet is orders of magnitude above that of typical hot Jupiters orbiting FGK stars, where photochemical hazes are already routinely invoked to explain muted transmission features and low geometric albedos 86–88 . Scaling from those works, even modest conversion efficiencies of incident EUV photons into haze particles 89 can build dayside aerosol columns of τ ≳ 1. on timescales much shorter than the day-to-night advective timescale, 

τadv ∼ Rp/vwind ∼ hours-to-days – assuming vwind ∼ 1–10 km s −190, 91 . The dayside can therefore plausibly form and maintain an optically thick haze layer. On the nightside, the planet’s high intrinsic temperature of 1,700 K is close to the thermal decomposition temperatures of most photochemical haze precursors and aerosol monomers 92, 93 , so once the dayside EUV source is removed the hot atmosphere should rapidly destroy the haze layer. The destruction timescale should be at most comparable to τadv , so that particles advected from the dayside are destroyed on roughly the same timescale on which they are delivered. A self-consistent microphysical and circulation calculation is beyond the scope of this work. But the dayside EUV-driven production, together with rapid thermochemical destruction on the hot nightside, gives a plausible way to sustain the asymmetric haze distribution our hypothesis requires. The non-detection of an infrared excess with Spitzer 49 suggests that it was observed during the planet’s hazy dayside phase. We notice that a similar, albeit shorter period (1 .1 days) and higher-amplitude (0 .1%) modulation is seen in the TESS 

lightcurve of GD 394 94 . That white dwarf has a nearly identical temperature to HS 0209+0832 ( Teff = 35 , 700 ± 1, 500 K) 63 , but also displays a large-amplitude (25%) modulation at the same 1.1 days period in the extreme ultraviolet 95 . Magnetic fields and surface inhomogeneities were ruled out by phase-resolved STIS spectroscopy 63 . A close-in planet would suffer mass loss

Abundances incompatible with a first-generation planet 

The most unique abundance pattern of HS 0209+0832 is the enhancements in s-process elements. Strontium and barium, both s-process elements, have been previously detected in high resolution spectra of ultra-hot Jupiter (UHJ) atmospheres such as KELT-9 b 97 , WASP-121 b 98 , and WASP-189 b 99 . Although niobium has not yet been detected in UHJs, it is reasonable to assume it is present given the similar nucleosynthetic origins of s-process elements. However, cross-correlation spectroscopy cannot calculate relative elemental abundances, so we cannot infer abundances of s-process elements in first-generation exoplanets. Using main sequence stars as a proxy for first-generation planet abundances, we identified 100 stars from the Hypatia catalogue with abundances of both calcium and niobium 100 . We found the maximum niobium abundance was log (Nb /Ca ) = −3.3, much lower than log (Nb /Ca ) = −0.94 ± 0.33 for HS 0209+0832. There is no known process to generate such a large niobium enhancement in a first-generation planet compared to its main sequence host star. A first-generation planet could plausibly survive common envelope evolution and its orbit brought close to the white dwarf due to drag forces 101 , but for a planet to be brought ≃ 8 R ⊙ away from the white dwarf, extreme fine tuning is needed 102 or multiple envelope ejection events 103 . Additionally, the timescale where accretion can occur is much longer than the timescale for decay 104 . Therefore, a planet which survived common envelope evolution would not be substantially enhanced in s-process elements, as we see in HS 0209+0832. We can calculate an upper limit on the accretion rate ˙MEdd assuming that the planet accretes material during the common envelope phase at the Eddington limit: ˙MEdd = 4πGM pmp

σTc (2) where c is the speed of light, G is the gravitational constant, Mp is the mass of the planet, mp is the proton mass, and σT is the Thomson cross section for an electron. For an extremely long common envelope phase of 10,000 yrs, a giant planet would accrete a maximum mass of 2 .2 × 10 −5 MJup .

Predicted second-generation planet composition 

The first intact exoplanets discovered, the pulsar planets 105 , are likely of second-generation origin 106, 107 . In contrast to first-generation planets, which form from a standard proto-planetary disc coeval with its main sequence host star, second-generation 

> 15/36

planets form after the host star has finished its life on the main sequence, and thus in a very different environment. Although the material and mechanisms that formed the pulsar planets are quite different in detail, their example has prompted some theoretical investigation of a different type of second-generation planet: one which forms from AGB wind material that settles into a disk in the vicinity of a newborn white dwarf 23 .The details of second-generation planet formation have only received limited investigation. Most existing theoretical works consider second-generation proto-planetary disks formed when a portion of the AGB wind is captured by a binary companion star 23, 27 , although in principle such a disk could form around a single white dwarf if either (i) a progenitor binary system is converted into a single star by a common envelope episode during AGB evolution, leaving behind a white dwarf and a post-common envelope disk 30 , or (ii) a small fraction of the AGB wind returns hydrodynamically, as is known to occur in other contexts. For example, in core collapse supernova explosions, a fraction of the initially unbound ejecta can become bound later on and fall back to the centre of the system. A variety of hydrodynamical mechanisms have been proposed to achieve this 108 ;one that seems relatively plausible for the white dwarf context is ejecta deceleration by reverse shocks 109 . Specifically, the material ejected in a very late thermal pulse has very high velocities 110 and will overtake the remnants of the slow 111 AGB superwind, launching a reverse shock into the final mass ejection that can decelerate it and may bind a fraction of it to the white dwarf. It should be emphasized that these second-generation disks are not purely theoretical: infrared excesses have been detected around a large number of post-AGB stars, pointing to the existence of post-AGB disks 50 where planets could form 28 .Once present, a second-generation proto-planetary disk can in principle create smaller planets by core accretion or larger ones by Toomre instability, as in first-generation disks. The composition of such second-generation planets will be strongly affected by geochemical and (if present) atmospheric chemical processing, but their bulk compositions will in any case differ dramatically from that of their first-generation counterparts. For example, AGB winds often have inverted C/O ratios, which permits non-standard condensation sequences in which rarer oxygen gets locked up in volatiles while carbon can bind into e.g. carbides 112 . In such an environment, planets formed inside the ice line will be heavily depleted in oxygen, rather than carbon (as is typical for first-generation planets). If this process produces Furthermore, because AGB stars are the Universe’s primary locus of s-process nucleosynthesis 24, 113 , objects formed out of AGB wind material will likely be the most s-process enriched planets in the Universe, with corresponding abundance signatures. An alternative formation mechanism is that a first-generation planet accretes a second-generation envelope, a so-called “born-again” planet. For example, a super-Earth or sub-Neptune planet could be scattered towards the second-generation proto-planetary disk and accrete an atmosphere. This would decrease the time needed for planet formation given that the core is already present. However, if the process of enriching a first-generation planet with AGB material works, it would likely be more common to observe accretion of second-generation material onto white dwarfs because most progenitors would eject an s-process-rich envelope, but HS 0209+0832 is the only example of this to date. Regardless, it would be impossible to determine whether HS 0209+0832 b has a first- or second-generation core given the difficulty of measuring interior abundances. Future work on planet formation models within a second-generation proto-planetary disk could deduce whether a first-generation core 

> 16/36

is required for formation of a second-generation giant planet. In this paper, we use the results of detailed stellar evolution and neutron capture nucleosynthesis modelling to explore the parameter space of AGB wind compositions. Our working hypothesis is that second-generation planets will form out of AGB wind material and that their bulk composition will reflect this. We employ a large grid of stellar evolution models covering a broad range of zero-age main sequence (ZAMS) masses MZAMS and metallicities ZZAMS . The models we employ use the Monash stellar evolution code 114 to explore the synthesis of s-process elements in the He-rich intershell region 115 . Specifically, we examine 138 different stellar evolution models, with 1 M⊙ ≤ MZAMS ≤ 7.5M⊙. These models cover a discrete set of ZZAMS 

values: ZZAMS = 0.0001 116 , ZZAMS = 0.0006 117 , ZZAMS = 0.001 118 , ZZAMS = 0.0028 119 , and ZZAMS = 0.007, ZZAMS = 0.014, and ZZAMS = 0.03 115 . We use these models to build a grid of AGB wind compositions in the space of {MZAMS , ZZAMS } and linearly interpolate to fill gaps in this space. Lower-mass (1 M⊙ ≲ MZAMS ≲ 4M⊙) stars synthesize s-process elements during the thermally pulsing AGB in a complex way, relying on α-capture onto 13 C to provide a neutron source in the intershell. The buildup of a 13 C pocket by proton capture is thought to occur during third dredge-up episodes, but is highly sensitive to the details of proton mixing from the envelope, creating a major uncertainty in s-process predictions 25 . In practice, the Monash models put a “partial mixing zone” (PMZ) in by hand with fixed mass 115 . For many {MZAMS , ZZAMS } pairs, we have multiple stellar evolution calculations with different parametrized PMZ masses. When given the choice, we follow the advice of A. Karakas (private communication) and select the closest PMZ mass to 2 × 10 −3M⊙ for MZAMS ≤ 3M⊙, 1 × 10 −3M⊙ for 3 M⊙ < MZAMS ≤ 4M⊙, 1 × 10 −4M⊙ for 4M⊙ < MZAMS ≤ 5M⊙, and no PMZ for higher mass progenitors. At the highest masses, the details of 13 C production become less relevant, as α captures onto 22 Ne provide a stronger neutron source. We show distributions of AGB wind elemental abundances in Fig. 3. In this figure, the y-axis indicates the AGB wind enhancement in a given element relative to the progenitor star’s ZAMS abundance of that element. The width of the distributions comes from mapping our grid of stellar evolution models onto a bivariate probability density function in {MZAMS , ZZAMS },where ZAMS masses are assumed to follow a Kroupa mass function for 1 ≤ MZAMS /M⊙ ≤ 8 and ZAMS metallicites are assumed to follow a log-uniform distribution between 10 −4 ≤ ZZAMS ≤ 0.03. Carbon abundances can be substantially enhanced or depleted, depending on the details of post-main sequence evolution. Trans-iron elements are generally enhanced relative to ZAMS abundances. Nickel is enhanced by a factor ≈ 2; zinc by a factor of ≈ 3 − 5; copper by a broad range of factors, up to an order of magnitude. Actual s-process elements, such as strontium (the center of the first “s-process peak,” where neutron absorption cross-sections are minimized) and niobium, can be enhanced by up to two orders of magnitude relative to ZAMS abundances. In particular, niobium abundances are tightly correlated with strontium abundances in all theoretical calculations of s-process nucleosynthesis. Although we include ≈ 100 strong strontium lines in our synthetic far-ultraviolet spectrum, strontium is not detected in the observed spectrum of HS 0209+0832, and our upper limit on its abundance (log (Sr /H) < −8.86) is more than two orders of magnitude below the detected niobium abundance. However, an analogous discrepancy is observed in several barium stars, which were enriched in AGB material from their binary companion 43 . A sample of barium stars have higher niobium abundances compared to strontium, which may be linked to a late neutron flux moving the first s-process peak to heavier elements such as niobium 43 . The existence of these barium stars points to a nucleosynthetic pathway not captured by present-day s-process models which may also be able to explain our observed Sr/Nb ratio in HS 0209+0832. Other abundance anomalies appear present in HS 0209+0832 as well. One, discussed earlier, is the apparent enhancement in carbon, which is indeed expected for a large subset of AGB stellar winds. Large portions of AGB parameter space produce C/O ratios greater than unity, potentially setting the stage for non-standard condensation sequences that result in strong oxygen depletion and carbon enhancement, the latter of which we have observed. Other anomalies are harder to explain, for example the unusual discovery of a Ni/Fe ratio greater than unity. It is important to note that no theoretical work has been performed on the atmospheres of second-generation planets. Therefore, we do not know the typical cloud-forming species of s-process elements. We only measure upper limits for the s-process elements Y, Zr, Ba, and Pb. However, we note that the atomic data is not complete for these elements due to the lack of transition strengths (log gf) for several lines and ionisation states, and hence the upper limits are not very constraining. Y, Ba, and Pb only have data for their first ionisation state and Zr only for its second ionisation state. The upper limits are not strict enough to constrain their presence in the accreted material, but future observations or atomic data of higher ionisation states may detect more s-process elements. There are several additional unidentified lines which do not match any other known metal feature, and so improvements in atomic data are required to investigate the presence of additional s-process elements.
