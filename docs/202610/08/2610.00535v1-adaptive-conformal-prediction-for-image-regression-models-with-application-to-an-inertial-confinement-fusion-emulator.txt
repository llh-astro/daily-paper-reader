Abstract 

Uncertainty quantification is critical in scientific machine learning, where black-box, image-based models are increasingly deployed in high-stakes settings. In many such applications, model outputs inform costly decisions, yet most methods provide only point estimates without quantifying predictive uncertainty. This challenge is compounded by the limited accessibility and interpretability of model internals, making it difficult to assess reliability across different regions of the input space. As a result, there is a growing need for methods that can provide input-dependent uncertainty estimates to guide both model development and downstream ex-perimentation. To address this need, we propose Adaptive Conformal Prediction using Nearest Neighbors (ACPNN), an input-adaptive conformal framework for image regression. ACPNN leverages information from neighboring samples to produce locally adaptive uncertainty esti-mates while maintaining low computational cost. The neighborhood structure is defined using a scaled distance metric learned via a Gaussian Process with an automatic relevance determi-nation (ARD) kernel. We demonstrate the effectiveness of ACPNN on a diffusion model for emulating inertial confinement fusion (ICF) simulations, showing that it achieves reliable and adaptive uncertainty quantification. 

# 1 Introduction 

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) in machine learning (ML) has become increasingly important as ML models are more widely adopted in scientific research, particularly in high-stakes domains such as inertial confinement fusion (ICF) (Ben Tayeb et al. (2024), Anirudh et al. (2020), Spears et al. (2025)), where both simulations and experiments are costly. ICF seeks to induce fusion by confining matter under extreme pressure and temperature and has the potential to produce clean energy at scale; however, its success is highly sensitive to experimental design under extreme pressure and temperature. High-fidelity multiphysics simulations can model the forward problem, but they are computationally expensive. To address the inverse problem—identifying input designs that achieve desired outputs—ML-based emulators (e.g., Kang et al. (2022)) have been developed. Despite their efficiency, these models often lack rigorous uncertainty quantification, raising critical concerns about their reliability as “black-box” predictors. These challenges are further amplified when working with image-based data, which introduces additional complexity compared to scalar quantities. Incorporating UQ improves confidence in model predictions, which helps guide experimental design across a range of fields—such as protein folding (Jumper et al. (2021), Baek et al. (2021)) and

A wide range of methods have been developed to achieve UQ in the field of computer vision, including Deep Ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017; Kall-Nielsen et al., 2021) and Bayesian Neural Networks (Kendall and Gal, 2017; Blundell et al., 2015), which often works with approxi-mation techniques like Monte Carlo Dropout (Lemay et al., 2022; Abdollahi et al., 2023; Gal and Ghahramani, 2016). Bayesian methods depend on prior distributions that may be misspecified and are usually computationally expensive, while ensemble-based methods provide heuristic uncertainty estimates. Moreover, these approaches requires significant modification to the model architecture and typically lack formal guarantees of reliability. The conformal prediction (CP) framework has attracted substantial attention as a model-agnostic approach to uncertainty quantification. Its key advantage lies in providing coverage guarantee under minimal assumptions, i.e. its prediction sets contain the true outcome with a user-specified significance level, regardless of the underlying model. It requires only one key as-sumption: data exchangeability, i.e. the joint distribution of data is permutation invariant. This condition can be implied by independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), which is a common assumption used in statistics. Beyond its coverage guarantees, the potential input-adaptiveness of CP further enhances its utility in scientific discovery, as it can help identify regions of high uncertainty, thereby guiding model refinement and informing future experimental directions. CP has been incorporated into various computer vision models (Angelopoulos et al., 2021; Roy et al., 2025; de Grancey et al., 2022), including input-adaptive approaches for tasks like image segmentation (Bereska et al., 2025; Luo and Zhou, 2026) and object detection (Timans et al., 2024). Although the related works also involve image-input models, they primarily analyze pixel-level uncertainty, while our method concentrates on the image’s overall structure. In this work, we propose Adaptive Conformal Prediction with Nearest Neighbors (ACPNN), a novel conformal prediction approach designed to produce input-adaptive conformal sets using information from nearest neighbors. Building on the Adaptive Conformal Prediction framework of Papadopoulos et al. (2011), our method extends adaptive uncertainty quantification to high-dimensional input spaces, such as images, where classical approaches may struggle to capture local structure. Our method fills a gap in adaptive UQ by producing statistically reliable prediction sets for image regression models. We apply ACPNN to an ICF dataset and evaluate several CP approaches. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the application moti-vating our method. Section 3 reviews building blocks of our proposed method and introduces our proposed method. Section 4 demonstrates the validity and adaptiveness of our proposed method, benchmarked with other methods.

3.1 Conformal Prediction 

Introduced by Saunders et al. (1999), conformal prediction (CP) is a framework that provides statistically reliable uncertainty quantification for machine learning models by constructing predic-tion sets or intervals with guaranteed coverage performance. Unlike traditional point predictions, conformal methods output sets of possible labels (in classification) or intervals (in regression) that contain the true outcome with a user-specified confidence level 1-α, such as 90% or 95%. The core idea is to measure how “conformal”, or consistent, a new data point is with previously ob-served data using a nonconformity score, and then calibrate thresholds through permutation - or exchangeability-based arguments. Crucially, permuting the random variables does not change their joint distribution, and therefore the rank of the new data point among all data points is uniformly distributed from 1 to the total number of variables. Since CP relies on empirical quantiles, its guarantees are agnostic to model correctness and the form of the error distribution, provided the data are exchangeable. In other words, conformal prediction is distribution-free, model-agnostic, and computationally simple to apply on top of any black-box predictor, offering a practical and theoretically sound way to quantify prediction uncertainty.

In our framework, we adopt the Vecchia approximation because it pre-serves local dependence structures through nearest-neighbor conditioning, which aligns naturally with our overall approach of leveraging neighbor information for uncertainty quantification (UQ). This method is implemented in the GpGp package developed by Guinness (2019). 

3.3 Adaptive Conformal Prediction with Gaussian Process Regression for Im-age Based Models 

While adaptive conformal prediction using nearest neighbors guarantees a desired coverage rate, its application to image-based models is challenging. The primary difficulty lies in defining a meaningful set of “nearest neighbors” for images. Standard image similarity metrics, such as the Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), are inadequate as they only assess pixel-level variations and ignore the relationship between an image and the response variable of interest. To address this, we propose a novel framework that integrates the computational benefits of nearest-neighbor adaptive conformal prediction with the feature-relevance estimation capabilities of GPs. This approach yields prediction sets with guaranteed coverage for image-based regression or classification models.

# 4 Motivating Application: Inertial Confinment Fusion 

4.1 Setups 

Our work focuses on the multi-modal diffusion model introduced by Jones et al. Jones and Kus-towski (2025), which is designed to model the joint and conditional distributions of scalar and image data generated in inertial confinement fusion experiments. Specifically, this joint multi-modal diffusion-based generative model acts as a forward and inverse surrogate for the radiation hydrodynamic code HYDRA (Marinak et al. (2001)), enabling prediction of simulation inputs, our response variable y, from their corresponding radiation hydrodynamic images, x. Sample images are visualized in Figure 2. HYDRA radiation-hydrodynamics simulations played a central role in the “Bigfoot” campaign by providing a computational means to evaluate capsule and drive design parameters (Nora et al. (2017)). In parallel with experiments performed at the National Ignition Facility (NIF) from 2016 to 2017, which sought to increase implosion velocity and reduce low-mode asymmetries by varying laser drive and capsule scale, a large ensemble of 2D HYDRA simulations was generated 8(Casey et al. (2018)). This ensemble was constructed by varying nine capsule inputs and produced approximately 90,000 high-resolution simulations, along with a suite of synthetic scalar outputs and diagnostic images. Producing these data required more than 20 million CPU-hours, making the Bigfoot simulation ensemble one of the largest ICF simulation datasets containing X-ray images. The goal here is to augment the pre-trained point prediction model, f , with rigorous uncertainty quantification. Because f is a diffusion model, a heuristic notion of uncertainty is available by taking the standard deviation of n draws from the model. This is not true for many ML models that are deterministic and we aim to develop a method that can be applied more generally. The diffusion model variability provides a reasonable comparison. We aim to construct adaptive prediction intervals, C1−α(xi), for any given image xi ∈ R48 ×48 , such that the true input yi is contained within the interval with a user-specified probability, formally P yi ∈ C1−α(xi) ≥ 1 − α.

Data and Model Setup: The existing model f was trained on a large dataset of 90,000 image-input pairs simulated by HYDRA (Marinak et al. (2001)), which we denote as Dtrain . For our uncertainty analysis, we utilize an additional 2,630 held-out samples. We randomly partition this unused data evenly into a calibration set Dcal and a test set Dtest , each containing 1,315 data points. This random split ensures that the calibration and test data are drawn from the same distribution, which is a key assumption for conformal prediction. The model generatively predicts capsule simulation inputs R9 (response variables) from simulation output X-ray images R48 ×48 .While X-ray images can be measured in experiment, capsule inputs are challenging to measure and therefore must be inferred from simulation data. These inputs include symmetry parameters and laser energy delivery, which provide crucial information about possible failure modes in ICF experiments. In this work, we focus our analysis on predicting two inputs from this ensemble in particular — the drive mode 1 and mode 2 asymmetries. In indirect drive ICF, lasers do not strike the capsule directly but rather heat the surface of an outer container called a “hohlraum” which produces an X-ray radiation bath that subsequently drives the capsule implosion. Under ideal conditions, this radiation profile is uniform and the capsule remains round as it compresses. More commonly, however, there are small perturbations to the drive which are amplified during the implosion and degrade performance. Mode 1 refers to a net perturbation in any direction, and mode 2 refers to more or less energy being delivered to the pole or equator of the capsule. X-ray images are critical for diagnosing these drive asymmetries, however the mapping is complex and degenerate, as multiple types of perturbations can produce similar images. 

Dimension Reduction: We first reduce the dimensionality of the input images from 48 × 48 to 6 using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). These 6 components capture 90% of the data variance. The visualization of the top 6 principal components (PC) are shown in Figure 3. This projection captures the most significant features of the X-ray images while drastically reducing computational cost for late steps. For example, PC2 can be deemed associated with response variable mode 2 asymmetries. Also, PCA is more computationally efficient compared to other techniques like using Variational Autoencoder(VAE), which can suffer from its sensitivity to the architecture of the neural network and hyperparameter tuning. 

Relevance Estimation: To learn the relative importance of the input features with respect to the response variables of interest, we modeled the relationship between the latent-space inputs and the response variables using a Gaussian process with an ARD Mat’ern kernel (smoothness parameter fixed at 1.5) implemented via the GpGp package. The estimated relevance for PC 5 was lower than that of the other principal components for both response variables, indicating that PC 5 is relatively less informative for the prediction task and contributes less to the construction of the neighborhood-based conditional sets in later stages.

4.2 Results 

We benchmark our method against conformal prediction (CP) and scaled conformal prediction (SCP) by evaluating predictive accuracy, uncertainty bandwidths, and input-conditional coverage rates. Our results show that all three methods achieve the desired coverage levels, successfully cap-turing the true response values. Moreover, both SCP and ACPNN produce more input-adaptive prediction intervals than CP (see Figure 4). We also investigated the prediction performance of ACPNN using different numbers of ensembles, with the corresponding results presented in Ap-pendix A (Figure 6). We further examine the conformal interval widths of ACPNN with different ensemble sizes (3, 5, 10, and 30) and compare them with SCP, which is based on 30 ensembles throughout this paper. A summary of the results is provided in Table 1, with representative examples visualized in Figure 5. Our experiments indicate that when the underlying diffusion model demonstrates varying lev-els of predictive performance (e.g., it predicts mode 1 asymmetry more accurately than mode 2 asymmetry), ACPNN achieves interval widths comparable to those of SCP, particularly in input regions with abundant data (see Figure 5b). In contrast, when predictive performance is weaker, SCP tends to perform better in data-rich regions, whereas ACPNN shows advantages in data-scarce regions (see Figure 5a). This observa-tion can be explained by the fact that interval widths under SCP are more closely tied to model performance, as ensemble outputs are heavily used for both mean and variance estimation. # 5 Conclusion and Discussion 

Our experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method (ACPNN) in its application to ICF data. The method achieves the desired empirical coverage while providing adaptive, image-dependent conformal prediction intervals for code outputs, enabling more nuanced uncertainty quantification. While scaled conformal prediction (SCP) retains some advantage in certain regimes—yielding narrower intervals in regions with abundant data even when model performance is weak—its appli-cability is limited in more constrained settings. In particular, when generating multiple ensembles is computationally prohibitive, as is often the case when emulator evaluations are expensive, ACPNN provides a clear advantage and is better suited for practical deployment. Moreover, in scenarios where ensemble generation is infeasible due to the deterministic nature of the model (e.g., the lane-keeping CNN introduced in Chen and Huang (2017), the Fourier Neural Operator in Li et al. (2021), and vision transformer–style architectures such as ConvNeXt in Liu et al. (2022)), SCP is usually not applicable, whereas ACPNN remains effective and delivers competitive performance even with a single ensemble. These advantages are particularly relevant in scientific settings, where data is often limited and computational constraints favor lightweight models over more expensive approaches such as diffusion models, further reinforcing the practicality of ACPNN. Finally, although our framework employs a simple dimensionality reduction technique (PCA), it still achieves competitive overall performance, indicating robustness and suggesting potential gains from incorporating more advanced representation learning methods. Looking ahead, a compelling direction for future work is to extend this method to scenarios involving distribution shifts, i.e when training data and testing data have different distributions, as if often the case when comparing ICF simulations to experiments. A key challenge in this context will be to develop a meaningful and robust definition of “nearest neighbor”, and defining weighted conformal scores and weighted quintile, especially in the case when distributions of images are more complicated like bimodal distribution. This direction potentially involve exploring density ratio estimation in a learned latent space. Further research could also investigate the theoretical properties of the proposed adaptive sets and explore applications in other high-stakes domains where calibrated uncertainty is critical. 13 Acknowledgments
