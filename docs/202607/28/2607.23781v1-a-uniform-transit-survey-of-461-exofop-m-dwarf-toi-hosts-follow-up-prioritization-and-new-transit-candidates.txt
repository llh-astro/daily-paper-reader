# A uniform transit survey of 461 ExoFOP M-dwarf TOI hosts: Follow-up prioritization and new transit candidates 

Yohann Tschudi 1

Independent Researcher, 69480 Anse, France e-mail: yohann.tschudi@gmail.com 

Submitted to Astronomy & Astrophysics 

ABSTRACT 

Context. M dwarfs are favorable hosts for small transiting planets. A transit-detection and statistical-validation pipeline was previously developed and validated at the photometric noise frontier, on M3–M6 dwarfs observed in few sectors. 

Aims. That pipeline is applied uniformly to the full active ExoFOP M-dwarf TOI population, 461 hosts (M0–M6) each hosting a confirmed planet (CP) or planet candidate (PC), to test its recovery and classification of the cataloged signals and to prioritize the candidates for follow-up. 

Methods. The pipeline chains two complementary blind searches merged per host, a full-baseline transit-least-squares scan and a segmented, semi-coherent search across the gapped multiyear baseline. It adds an event-time-coherence test that rejects signals with incoherent per-transit timing (window-function aliases of stellar variability), all-sector TRICERATOPS false-positive probabilities, and Gaia DR3 background and aperture-localization analysis. 

Results. Of 193 in-range CP, 165 (85.5%) are re-detected, none rejected as a false positive, and 147 (76.2%) reach a Gaia-verified disposition. Of 286 in-range PC, 225 (78.7%) are re-detected, of which 11 are flagged as false positives and 87 ranked as planet candidates for follow-up. Beyond the catalog, 13 new transit candidates of 0 .7–2 .0 R⊕ emerge, including two new members of a compact, dynamically stable near-resonant three-planet candidate system; four cataloged single- or dual-transit TOIs have their orbital periods determined, two of them in the optimistic habitable zone. 

Conclusions. The recoveries and new candidates are released as a uniform, prioritized follow-up target list. 

Key words. planets and satellites: detection – planets and satellites: terrestrial planets – techniques: photometric – stars: low-mass – surveys 

1. Introduction 

The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS; Ricker et al. 2015) has transformed the census of planets around M dwarfs, the most abundant stellar population in the solar neighborhood (Henry et al. 2006). Transit detection on these faint, active tar-gets remains challenging: stellar variability amplitudes often exceed transit depths (Yaptangco et al. 2025), and the sector-based observing strategy produces gapped light curves that limit sensitivity to long-period planets (Sullivan et al. 2015). Several pipelines address these challenges, including the Science Pro-cessing Operations Center (SPOC; Jenkins et al. 2016) pipeline, the Quick-Look Pipeline (QLP; Huang et al. 2020a,b), and the threshold-based LEO-Vetter (Kunimoto et al. 2025), targeting the detection or vetting steps individually. Tschudi (2026) developed an automated transit-detection and statistical-validation pipeline and validated it at the pho-tometric noise frontier, on 121 newly enabled M3–M6 dwarfs, where the limited sector coverage makes the search hardest. The present work applies the complete pipeline, unchanged in its detection core, with the vetting adapted to the heterogeneous ExoFOP population (Sect. 2.2.4), uniformly across the full ac-tive population of M-dwarf TESS objects of interest (TOIs; Guerrero et al. 2021) in the Exoplanet Follow-up Observing Pro-gram (ExoFOP), a sample of signals around stars already known to host a planet or candidate, generally observed over more sec-tors than the noise-frontier sample. Confirmed systems are pro-ductive targets for this purpose, since the host is known to har-bor at least one planet, its stellar parameters are characterized (Muirhead et al. 2018), and radial-velocity or other follow-up data are often already in hand. The first aim is to establish, on the confirmed planets, that the pipeline recovers and correctly classifies known signals, demonstrating a consistent and reli-able solution; the second is to apply it to the unconfirmed candi-dates, removing false positives and prioritizing the survivors for follow-up. A segmented, semi-coherent second search, merged per host with the full-baseline scan, recovers phase-coherent transits that a single full-baseline scan dilutes across the gapped multiyear baseline. A second addition targets a failure mode of the gapped multiyear baseline, where the observing window can stack quasi-periodic stellar variability into spurious transit trains that pass standard statistical validation (Dawson & Fabrycky 2010). An event-time-coherence test rejects such signals by requiring that their individual transit times form a coherent clock across the baseline, extending the event-level vetting heritage of the Ke-pler Robovetter (Mullally et al. 2016; Thompson et al. 2018; Coughlin et al. 2016) and recent TESS vetters (Kunimoto et al. 2025); it is calibrated on a window-function false positive estab-lished within the program. The sample comprises 461 active ExoFOP M-dwarf TOI hosts (M0–M6; Sect. 2.1), of which 193 confirmed planets and 286 planet candidates fall within the 0 .5–100 d search range and set the recovery denominators used throughout.

2. Sample and methods 

The detection, vetting, and verification pipeline of Tschudi (2026), described and validated in full there on 121 newly en-abled M3–M6 dwarfs (only recently searchable through the ac-cumulation of TESS Cycle 6 + coverage), is applied here across the full M0–M6 ExoFOP M-dwarf population. This section recaps the pipeline (Fig. A.1, Appendix A) and sets out the components added for the present survey: a segmented, semi-coherent second search pass and the per-host merge of the two detection engines (Sect. 2.2.2); an event-level coherence screen (Sect. 2.2.3); vetting adaptations for the heterogeneous Exo-FOP population, including the all-sector median false-positive-probability protocol (Sect. 2.2.4); and a photometric aperture-localization test (Sect. 2.2.5). Every operational threshold of these added components was frozen, before any application to the survey, on the ten-system validation set of Sect. 2.3 (Ta-ble 2), chosen to span the survey’s regimes: single- and multi-planet hosts (up to four planets, including a near-commensurable pair), quiet to highly active stars, and 2 to 37 observed sectors. 

2.1. Target selection 

The sample comprises the 461 M dwarfs in the TESS Input Catalog (TIC; Stassun et al. 2019) hosting at least one TOI with an active TESS Follow-up Observing Program Working Group (TFOPWG) disposition, confirmed planet (CP) or planet candidate (PC), in ExoFOP-TESS; the dispositions and all de-rived counts use the pinned 2026 June 29 snapshot. The sample was searched in two campaigns probing complementary spectral ranges (Table 1). This sample does not overlap the 121 newly enabled M3–M6 dwarfs searched in Tschudi (2026). The snap-shot contained 611 M-dwarf TOI hosts; the 150 whose TOIs car-ried only known-planet (KP, 23), false-positive or false-alarm (FP /FA, 102), or ambiguous (APC, 25) dispositions were not searched, leaving the active CP /PC population. The recovery of known transiting planets is characterized in detail on the ten benchmark systems of Sect. 2.3, which are themselves members of this sample. No brightness, sector-count, or stellar-quality fil-ter was applied. The M0–M2 campaign contains 269 targets with 

Teff = 3500–4000 K (76 CP and 195 PC hosts); the M3–M6 cam-paign contains 192 targets with Teff < 3500 K (86 CP and 108 PC hosts).

2.2.2. Segmented search and detection merge 

The SDE contrasts a candidate period against the full ensemble of trial periods. On a gapped, multiyear baseline the observing window raises a forest of alias peaks that inflates that ensemble’s noise floor, so a planet whose folded transit is highly signifi-cant can still vanish into it. On L 98-59 (27 sectors over 2 692 d), planet b reaches SDE = 77 .8 in a windowed search around its pe-riod but only 3 .7 in the blind full-baseline scan. A second, semi-coherent pass therefore complements the full-baseline search: it searches each observing block on its own and then combines them, so a faint but coherent train stands out block by block in-stead of competing against the whole window forest at once. The light curve is cut at gaps longer than ten days into con-tiguous blocks (span ≥ 20 d), each searched blind at full resolu-tion; the surviving peaks (above SDE = 3.5, at most thirty per block) are grouped across blocks by period. A signal found by only one engine is kept unchanged, the two searches being complementary within a host rather than redundant, and every downstream stage treats single-engine and dual-engine candidates identically. 

2.2.3. Harmonic cleanup and event-time screening 

Because the iterative search runs over the residuals, one true pe-riod can reappear as a family of aliases (2 P or 3 P harmonics, a fundamental mistaken for a harmonic, or, on active M dwarfs, BY Draconis dips folded at unrelated periods). Ranking by de-tection statistic is unsafe, so each family is resolved on physi-cal evidence as in Tschudi (2026), analyzing a host’s candidates jointly: a cross-masking test (a dependent alias loses more than half its depth once the stronger signal is masked), an activity-scaled parsimony cap, and a mutual-Hill stability test (3 .5 RH;Gladman 1993); a period-ratio commensurability is reported but never demotes a candidate, and only the concurrence of these tests marks an alias. The cleanup retains every confirmed planet of the validation set (Sect. 2.3). A second screen addresses a failure mode specific to the gapped, multiyear TESS baseline: the observing window can stack quasi-periodic stellar or instrumental structure into a transit-like train at an alias period that survives the standard vet-ting chain. The risk is greatest near the detection floor, where a candidate is a sparse train of a few faint (2–4 σ) events across sector blocks separated by year-long gaps, none inspectable on its own, so a blind detection peak’s period is not yet a measure-ment (Dawson & Fabrycky 2010; Cooke et al. 2021). Using the events’ timing alone, and neither the detection statistic nor the transit depth (both unreliable at this S /N; Mullally et al. 2018), the screen tests, for every surviving candidate, whether the in-dividual events keep time like a single ephemeris or scatter as loosely as unrelated noise. Each candidate is tested at its imposed period and epoch, which are not re-fit. Every predicted event is re-timed locally to the o ff set that maximizes its depth significance, and the re-sulting timing dispersion ω = median |O − C| (observed minus calculated) measures the clock coherence: small for a genuine ephemeris, and as large as the transit window for a train as-sembled by the window function. The verdict is a p-value read against a no-clock null measured on the candidate’s own light curve, a set of N = 200 o ff -signal ephemerides re-timed by the identical procedure,

2.2.5. Gaia verification 

Candidates passing this stage undergo Gaia DR3 background source analysis (Gaia Collaboration 2023): sources bright enough to reproduce the transit depth are searched for within the photometric aperture, and the host renormalized unit weight error (RUWE) is checked against the 1.4 single-star thresh-old (Lindegren et al. 2021). A candidate whose aperture retains a resolved neighbor bright enough to host the transit, which Gaia (resolving only beyond the 0 .4′′ limit of Fabricius et al. 2021) cannot itself assign, is flagged NEEDS_HR_IMAGING : the transit may lie on the primary, and only high-resolution imag-ing can resolve the host. 2.3. End-to-end validation on known systems 

Before application to the survey, the full chain, from acqui-sition to Gaia DR3 verification, was validated end-to-end on the same ten benchmark systems as Tschudi (2026): GJ 357, GJ 486, GJ 3473, Gliese 12, L 98-59, LP 791-18, TOI-406, TOI-700, TOI-782, and TOI-6086, spanning M2–M5 ( Tmag = 8.7– 13 .6) and one to four planets per host, with sixteen TESS-detectable confirmed planets. The merged detection stage recovers all sixteen genuine sig-nals at their archive ephemerides, the two engines being indi-vidually incomplete in complementary ways: the full-baseline search misses L 98-59 d, whose 7 .3814 d peak is an exact 2:1 alias of planet c (ratio 2 .000) lying 0 .93% from the true planet d at 7 .4507 d and is absorbed at the merge; the segmented search recovers it blind, federated in all eleven blocks at a stacked 33 σ; conversely TOI-700 d and Gliese 12 b are found by the full-baseline search alone. Only two of the ten systems (L 98-59, TOI-700) lie in the multiyear-gapped regime the segmented search addresses, so this validation measures the method’s safety rather than its yield, the survey-scale contribution of the sec-ond engine being quantified in Sect. 3. The seven spurious sig-nals the iterative search raises are each removed at the stage de-signed for it, the merge, harmonic cleanup, event-time screen, TRICERATOPS vetting, and Gaia and aperture test in turn. 3. Confirmed planet recovery 

3.1. Transit search recovery 

A signal is considered recovered when TLS detects a period matching the ExoFOP value within < 1% relative deviation, or at an integer multiple or sub-multiple alias ( N ·P or P/N, N ≤ 6) matched to high precision ( < 0.1% in the period ratio). A gen-uine alias of the same transit train reproduces the integer ratio to a few ppm, whereas an unrelated coincidence matches only to 

∼0.5%. Planet and candidate dispositions are taken from the ExoFOP-TESS snapshot of 2026 June 29; under this snapshot the fixed sample of 461 hosts carries 202 confirmed planets: 193 within the 0 .5–100 d search range and nine below the 0 .5 d grid floor (none lies beyond 100 d). Both detection engines run on every target and their candidate lists are merged per host (Sect. 2.2.2). In the 0 .5–100 d range the merged search recovers 165 of 193 in-range confirmed planets (85.5%; 85 /96 in M0–M2, 80 /97 in M3–M6), the full-baseline and segmented engines con-tributing 28 and 23 unique recoveries beyond their 114 in com-mon (Table 3); all but one (TOI-5720.01, at 3 P) are at the archive period, matching the catalog to a median deviation of 0 .0004% (worst case 0 .47%, TOI-2267.01). Eight of the nine confirmed planets below the 0 .5 d floor are recovered through an in-range harmonic and restored to their catalog periods by the sub-floor arbitration of Sect. 2.2.2 (sole loss TOI-6000.01), bringing the overall recovery to 173 of 202 confirmed planets (85.6%). These recovery rates quantify the re-detection of the known TOI cata-log and complement the blind injection–recovery completeness of Tschudi (2026), which maps the pipeline’s detection sensitiv-ity across period and radius. 3.3. False negative taxonomy 

The 28 confirmed planets missed by the merged detection stage (11 M0–M2, 17 M3–M6; Table 3) are not recovered at the cat-alog period; Table 5 tallies the causes, with the per-planet cat-alog in Table D.1. Two regimes dominate and are the expected ones: no signal at any period, or a peak at the catalog period that remains below the detection threshold, for transits too shal-low for the available baseline or masked by stellar activity. One peak is surfaced by the segmented search but fails its federa-tion gates, the measured cost of the frozen detection thresholds (Sect. 2.2.2). Three structural causes account for the rest. Itera-tive masking extracts a stronger signal on the same host before the search reaches the planet (the two 2:1 cases of Sect. 3.1 are its verified instances). Some hosts have photometry the pipeline cannot cleanly search: a diluted triple-star aperture, and an ex-tremely young flare-active star (the regime characterized in the limitation tests of Tschudi 2026). Finally, the 1 .0 d Earth-rotation deadband rejects by construction. Some searched sectors carry only lower-cadence full-frame-image photometry, no 2-minute SPOC product existing for them, and the coarser sampling fur-ther reduces sensitivity to short, shallow transits. 4. Planet candidate recovery 

Of the 311 PC signals in the sample (Table 1), 25 fall outside the search range ( P > 100 d, ultra-short, or no reported period) and are excluded, leaving 286 evaluable signals across 303 host targets. These are unconfirmed transit signals that have not been validated through radial velocity or statistical methods. Apply-ing the validated pipeline provides an independent assessment of which candidates survive the full detection, vetting, and veri-fication chain.

5. New transit candidates after alias-risk screening 

Beyond recovering the cataloged signals, the iterative search identifies detections not listed in ExoFOP. Each was passed through the event-time screen (Sect. 2.2.3), then vetted (Sect. 2.2.4) and verified against the Gaia field (Sect. 2.2.5); where post-detection photometry exists, a held-out-sector predicted-transit check is also reported. After this screen, signals not attributable to a cataloged TOI are retained as new transit candidates, and several signals ini-tially treated as independent detections are reclassified as recov-eries of cataloged TOIs, four of them with their orbital periods determined here (Sect. 5.1). No retained signal reaches the joint statistical-validation thresholds of Giacalone et al. (2021) (FPP 

< 1.5%, NFPP < 0.1%). This reflects the conservative all-sector screen, not the candidates: the recovered confirmed planets miss the same thresholds (median FPP 9 .1%; Sect. 6.3) because the screen counts every resolved Gaia neighbor as a possible host. The FPP therefore ranks candidates and flags contaminated aper-tures rather than validating planets. The strongest candidates are

instead cleared of their main false-positive channel (a blend or a stellar companion) with archival speckle imaging or recon-naissance spectroscopy, and reported as strong candidates; a firm validation still requires a radial-velocity mass and is left to ded-icated follow-up. The candidates are organized along a three-tier follow-up ladder, keyed to the detection and field state rather than the stellar-noise robustness of Tschudi (2026). tier 1: a clean de-tection (folded dip detached and centered) with a clean field, the nearby false-positive probability (NFPP) of the conservative vetting (Sect. 2.2.5) below ∼10% or the resolved-neighbor field cleared by archival high-resolution imaging; the residual on-target eclipsing binary is addressed by radial velocities or transit-shape analysis. tier 2: a clean detection with an in-aperture Gaia neighbor bright enough to host the signal, awaiting imaging to localize the transit ( NEEDS_HR_IMAGING ), or one held short of tier 1 by a marginal statistic or grazing geometry. tier 3: marginal detections, with SDE near the 7 .0 floor or a phase coherence in the stellar-variability regime. 6. Discussion 

6.1. Comparison with other M-dwarf transit surveys 

Several systematic searches have targeted TESS M dwarfs. SHERLOCK (Pozuelos et al. 2020; Dévora-Pajares et al. 2024) runs an iterative TLS search with vetting and statistical val-idation, but one host at a time; the QLP team produced the bulk of M-dwarf TOIs but is detection-first, without integrated Gaia background analysis; and LEO-Vetter (Kunimoto et al. 2025) vets downstream of a TLS or box least squares (BLS; Kovács et al. 2002) search (93% acceptance on pre-detected TOIs) but does not search. The di ff erence here is scale: detec-tion, vetting, and Gaia-based verification are applied uniformly across all 461 hosts rather than one target at a time, with the seg-mented semi-coherent search (Sect. 2.2.2) added for the gapped multisector baselines, complementing the uniform mid-to-late M-dwarf occurrence census of Gillis et al. (2026). The new can-didates have radii of 0 .7–2 .0 R⊕, consistent with the small-planet dominance around M dwarfs (Dressing & Charbonneau 2015; Ment & Charbonneau 2023). The segmented, semi-coherent search introduced here (Sect. 2.2.2) di ff ers in construction from existing multi-sector detection strategies: from the per-event single-event-

statistic to multiple-event-statistic (SES-to-MES) combination of the Kepler and TESS SPOC pipelines (Jenkins et al. 2002; Tenenbaum et al. 2013), whose absolute per-event statistic is structurally insensitive to the window-function forest but re-quires individually significant events; from duotransit period matching (Cooke et al. 2021), which federates individually de-tectable transits rather than sub-threshold periodograms; and from deliberately campaign-independent searches (Zink et al. 2021), which merge only at catalog level. Federating the block periodograms themselves recovers the coherent stacking gain of the full baseline while keeping each detection statistic’s noise floor local to its block. 

> 6.2. Archival guardrails

Three archival guardrails, applicable before any radial-velocity or ground-based follow-up, generalize from this survey’s screen-ing experience. First, when a detection matches an indepen-dently known period, require its predicted transit times to align, not the period value alone (Dawson & Fabrycky 2010): a coinci-dental period match can stack incoherent events into a false train, which only per-event timing separates from a genuine clock. Second, require the signal to reproduce in new data: several can-didates here (TOI-4565.01, TOI-4556.02; Sect. 5) were recov-ered again in TESS sectors observed after the search dataset was frozen, an out-of-sample confirmation stronger than any test on the search data alone. Third, treat a ground-based false-positive verdict as specific to its ephemeris: an eclipsing binary excluded at one period does not clear a neighboring one, each of which must be checked on its own transit times. 

> 6.3. Known limitations

Ten systematic limitations are identified: 1. Validation capped above 8 R⊕: TRICERATOPS cannot dis-tinguish planets from brown dwarfs or low-mass stars for 

Rp > 8 R⊕ (Giacalone et al. 2021), so these are capped at the candidate level (radial-velocity masses required). 2. Validation withheld on high-scatter folds: on a many-sector fold the per-bin error shrinks but an in-transit mismatch (transit-timing variations, residual period error) does not, sat-urating the false-positive probability of an on-target planet (on TOI-700, planet e saturates in 17 of 20 Monte Carlo draws while planet d folds cleanly; Gilbert et al. 2023); this is contained by taking the median of 20 draws, bounding the pre-fold period refinement to a coherence window (Ofir 2014), and flagging a saturated draw distribution as degener-ate. 3. BIC on active M dwarfs: the Bayesian information crite-rion ( ∆BIC) transit test assumes independent data points, but correlated stellar noise reduces the e ff ective sample size (Pont et al. 2006) and can misclassify a real transit as stel-lar variability or a spurious eclipsing-binary secondary. The red-noise floor and binned-error FPP correction of Sect. 2.2.4 recover these signals, so no confirmed planet is rejected (Ta-ble 4); the test nonetheless stays degenerate on the most ac-tive hosts. 4. In-aperture neighbors and conservative NFPP: Gaia DR3 cannot resolve companions closer than 0 .4′′ (Fabricius et al. 2021); a resolved neighbor contributing aperture flux is kept in the TRICERATOPS scenario set rather than cleared by the SPOC centroid alone (Sect. 2.2.4), since no survey clears a non-empty NFPP from a TESS di ff erence image without on-target follow-up (Giacalone et al. 2022; Barkaoui et al. 2024 used ground-based or multicolor photometry). The NFPP is thus a conservative upper bound: even the re-covered confirmed planets, with well-behaved FPP (median 9.1%), mostly miss the joint criterion of Giacalone et al. (2021) on TESS alone (only 11% pass, the NFPP be-ing the binding constraint), and an elevated RUWE flags a possible unresolved companion on nine recoveries and nine candidates ( PC_HEB_CAUTION ). Failing the criterion flags an in-aperture neighbor for high-resolution imaging (NEEDS_HR_IMAGING ), not a false positive. 5. MCMC impact-parameter degeneracy: the b–Rp/R⋆ degen-eracy (Espinoza 2018) lets the MCMC settle at b ≈ 1 for shallow transits, biasing the radius but not causing false re-jections (Sect. 2.2.4). 6. Iterative masking in multiplanet systems: masking a domi-nant signal before searching for the next can remove a shal-lower planet whose transits overlap it, as in the 2:1 config-uration of TOI-406.02; such losses leave no peak and are counted as not-detected (Table D.1). Simultaneous multisig-nal fitting would avoid them but is not implemented. 7. Frozen federation thresholds in the segmented search: the segmented search (Sect. 2.2.2) recovers signals that a full-baseline scan dilutes on heavily gapped multiyear coverage, but it federates block detections against fixed thresholds, so a genuine sub-threshold peak at the catalog period can fall short of federation and be missed (Sect. 3.3). 8. Stellar parameters from TIC only: all stellar radii, tem-peratures, and gravities are from the TESS Input Catalog (Stassun et al. 2019) without spectroscopic verification. For the coolest M dwarfs the TIC R⋆ systematics can exceed the 

∼3% of dedicated calibrations (Mann et al. 2015) and prop-agate into the planet properties: a 10% error on R⋆ gives 

∼10% on Rp and ∼20% on insolation ( S ∝ R2

> ⋆

). Spec-troscopy of the habitable-zone hosts is needed to refine these. 9. Selection bias at the detection threshold: a signal selected by threshold crossing overstates its own detection statistic (the winner’s curse), so a blind detection peak is not a measured period until it passes the event-level timing test calibrated on injected twins; statistical validation does not substitute for that screen (Sect. 6.2). 10. Pixel-level vetting needs a SPOC target pixel file: TRICER-ATOPS and the transit fit operate on the TESS pixel data, so a signal whose host has only Quick-Look or full-frame-image light curves, with no SPOC or TESS-SPOC target pixel file on MAST, cannot be vetted at the pixel level and is reported as inconclusive: 24 signals on 22 hosts (6 .2% of the recov-ered signals). Combining the archival light curve with a full-frame-image cutout would recover them, and is left to future work. 

7. Conclusions 

The principal results of this work are: 1. A uniform end-to-end survey: this work reports the first ho-mogeneous detection, vetting, and verification run, to the au-thor’s knowledge, on the 461 active ExoFOP M-dwarf TOI hosts. The merged search recovers 165 of the 193 in-range confirmed planets (85.5%), of which 147 (76.2%) reach a Gaia-verified candidate-level disposition at a 94.4% vetting acceptance, with no confirmed planet flagged as a false posi-tive; the 28 misses trace to stellar activity, survey-design lim-its, and iterative masking.

2. An event-level timing screen: a per-event no-clock statistic, 

pnoclock , is added to the statistical vetting to separate coherent transit trains from window-stacked noise (Sect. 2.2.3). Cali-brated on the window-alias false positive TOI-521 c (FAIL) against 16 confirmed signals (15 PASS, 1 FLAG, none FAIL), it makes the period of a blind SDE = 7–14 peak a measurement only once it is passed, at archival cost and be-fore any follow-up is committed. 3. Planet-candidate recovery: of the 286 evaluable ExoFOP planet candidates, 225 (78.7%) are recovered by the search. The full chain then yields 87 planet candidates (54 stan-dard, Rp < 8 R⊕, for radial-velocity confirmation, and 33 large-radius, requiring masses), 47 signals flagged for high-resolution imaging, and 9 with an elevated host RUWE; their ephemerides and vetting checks are tabulated for community follow-up (Appendix F). 4. New candidates and cataloged recoveries: the screen retains 13 new transit candidates across three confidence tiers. The two habitable-zone objects both lie in the optimistic zone and are cataloged recoveries whose period is determined here: TOI-2433.01, the coolest signal of the survey ( Teq = 178 K, from a single transit), and TOI-4353.01 (718 d duotransit re-solved to 39 .90 d); TOI-4565.01 (a 35:1 alias of 19 .79 d) and the deep-eclipse TOI-6492.01 (33 .26 d) are likewise recov-ered. The most notable new system is a candidate three-planet chain on the bright TOI-6284: two new small can-didates join the recovered cataloged candidate in a near-commensurable, dynamically stable configuration. The sur-vey also blind-recovers TOI-237 c, validated independently by Timmermans et al. (2026), an external check on the de-tection. 5. Conservative validation and follow-up: none of the recover-ies or new candidates meets the joint validation thresholds (FPP < 1.5%, NFPP < 0.1%) under the all-sector conser-vative screen, which flags a Gaia neighbor bright enough to host the signal for high-resolution imaging rather than reject-ing it as a false positive. The strongest recoveries and can-didates are instead cleared of their dominant false-positive channel by archival follow-up (TRES and CHIRON recon-naissance spectroscopy, speckle imaging) and reported as strong candidates, formal validation deferred to dedicated observations. The new candidates will be registered as com-munity TOIs on ExoFOP with the ephemerides of Table B.1; and as TESS keeps revisiting these hosts, the survey’s reach will extend toward the longer periods where the habitable zone of an M dwarf lies.

– Depth threshold for eclipsing binaries: a signal is classified as LIKELY_EB when its depth exceeds the fixed threshold of 50 000 ppm and the FPP is ≥ 50% (Fig. A.1); a depth above 50 000 ppm implies Rp > 0.22 R⋆, larger than any planet on all but the smallest hosts. ion on a 0 .7 R⊙ star produces only ∼20 000 ppm; the FPP condition supplies the discriminating evidence. The adaptive host-dependent ceiling of the search stage (Sect. 2.2.1) com-plements this cut upstream. For candidates with FPP ≥ 50% (classified as PENDING by TRICERATOPS), Gaia governs the disposition: the absence of a resolvable contaminant yields PLANET_CANDIDATE (the resolv-able BEB scenario excluded independently of the FPP value). For candidates with FPP < 50% ( LIKELY_PLANET ), Gaia can only upgrade, never downgrade (consistent with the statisti-50 

cal validation already performed; Giacalone et al. 2021). Can-didates with a resolved Gaia neighbor inside the photomet-ric aperture, bright enough to host the signal, are flagged as 

NEEDS_HR_IMAGING , requiring high-resolution imaging or on-target photometry to localize the transit. This classification re-flects aperture contamination, not signal quality: 24 confirmed planets in this sample also receive this flag (Sect. 3.2).
