> ABSTRACT
> Context.

Binary–single encounters occur frequently in stellar systems, constitute a non-integrable chaotic three-body problem, and are computationally expensive when treated with N-body codes.  

> Aims.

We aim to construct an accurate and physically interpretable surrogate model for binary–single scattering final states (ioniza-tion, flyby, exchange, or hierarchical), evaluate the reliability of its probabilistic predictions, and trace its predictive failures to the underlying chaotic nature of the scattering problem.  

> Methods.

We trained an XGBoost multiclass classifier on a class-balanced dataset of numerically integrated binary–single scattering experiments, using physically motivated features.  

> Results.

The model achieves a test accuracy of 88 .32% and a top-2 accuracy of 98 .77%. Feature importance indicates binary hardness as the dominant predictor. Misclassifications concentrate near the chaotic boundary separating exchange and hierarchical outcomes. Basin-entropy analysis reveals that such misclassifications are due to intrinsically ambiguous regions of parameter space.  

> Conclusions.

Therefore, a fast machine-learning surrogate can predict binary–single scattering final states with high accuracy, and residual errors arise from chaotic regions rather than model inadequacy.  

> Key words.

stars: kinematics and dynamics – binaries: general – scattering – chaos – methods: numerical – methods: statistical 

1. Introduction 

Binaries are a primary constituent of stellar populations across galactic environments. Observations suggest a binary frac-tion of roughly 50% for solar-type stars in the Galactic field (Raghavan et al. 2010; Moe & Di Stefano 2017), and in the dense cores of star clusters and galactic nuclei, studies report binary fractions reaching up to a few tens of percent (see e.g., Sollima et al. 2010; Milone et al. 2012; Lucatello et al. 2015). In these dense environments, the rate of close gravitational encoun-ters between binaries and single stars (hereafter binary–single) is su ffi ciently high that nearly every binary experiences at least one significant interaction within a Hubble time (Hut & Bahcall 1983; Sigurdsson & Phinney 1993). Therefore, these encounters are not only highly frequent but also fundamental drivers of the dynamical evolution of dense stellar systems. The cumulative e ff ect of binary–single interactions plays a central role in the thermodynamic evolution of stellar clusters. By the Heggie–Hut law, a binary is either hard or soft depend-ing on whether its binding energy exceeds or falls below the mean kinetic energy of nearby field stars (Heggie 1975). Hard binaries tend to become harder, i.e., tighter, with each encounter, transferring energy to the stellar background, heating the cluster core and resisting gravitational collapse, while soft binaries ab-sorb energy and gradually soften until eventually ionized. This constant flow of energy regulates mass segregation, core oscil-lations, and the formation of exotic objects such as blue strag-glers and compact X-ray binaries (Davies 1995; Fregeau et al. 2003; Portegies Zwart et al. 2004). also important in the dynamical formation and evolution of compact-object binaries and gravitational-wave (GW) sources; recent studies show that resonant interactions can produce GW transient signatures (Codazzo et al. 2024; Rando Forastier et al. 2024). Furthermore, repeated binary–single encounters within dense black-hole subsystems increasingly harden binaries and eject single black holes, leading to a gradual depletion of the population (Rostami Shirazi et al. 2024). At the level of an individual interaction, a binary– single encounter represents a realization of the classical chaotic three-body problem. Such encounters generally pro-duce four discrete final-state classes in the point-mass New-tonian regime—ionizations, flybys, exchanges, and hierarchi-cal triples—with mergers excluded as they require relativis-tic e ff ects or finite stellar radii (Heggie & Hut 1993a, 2003; Valtonen et al. 2005). The relative likelihood of these final-state class depends sensitively on the initial conditions. In particu-lar, moderately hard binaries preferentially undergo exchanges that retain the most massive object in the post-encounter binary (Heggie et al. 1996a). Wide or eccentric binaries are more sus-ceptible to disruption, while the precise mass ratio and encounter velocity determine the branching between flybys, exchanges, and ionizations (Heggie & Hut 1993a). Hierarchical formation is favored for extremely hard binaries and specific mass ra-tios, where the third star remains loosely bound in a long-lived resonant orbit (Antognini & Thompson 2014; Leigh et al. 2016; Stone & Leigh 2024). Despite decades of analytical and numerical studies, predict-ing the exact final state of an individual binary–single encounter remains challenging because this kind of scattering is fundamen-tally non-integrable and exhibits strong sensitivity to initial con-

ditions (Hut 1983; Valtonen et al. 2005). Minute perturbations in the initial configuration can evolve into qualitatively di ff erent dynamical outcomes, particularly during resonant phases where the three bodies undergo multiple temporary captures before the system decays into a permanent final state (Portegies Zwart et al. 2023). This intrinsic dependency on initial conditions limits the practical predictability of deterministic approaches and moti-vates the use of data-driven statistical methods capable of learn-ing probabilistic mappings between initial conditions and en-counter final-state class. In this work, we investigate how accurately the complex mapping between initial conditions and final-state class in binary–single encounters can be learned using machine learning techniques. We train a supervised classifier on a dataset of nearly 1, 000 , 000 numerical scattering simulations, using the full set of initial encounter parameters as input features. The goal is to determine whether the final-state class of an encounter can be reliably predicted despite the chaotic nature of the three-body problem. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec-tion 2 we describe the numerical setup and data generation, in-cluding the scattering code, the adopted initial conditions, and the preprocessing applied to the simulation dataset. Section 3 reviews recent ML applications to the three-body problem and presents the machine learning framework implemented in this study, including feature construction, model training, and hyper-parameter tuning. In Section 4 we present the classification re-sults and analyze the learned mapping through performance met-rics, feature importance, confusion matrices, and misclassifica-tions linked to chaotic boundaries. Section 5 interprets these re-sults in the context of chaotic scattering and fractal basin bound-aries, and Section 6 summarizes our main findings and discusses their implications for the dynamical evolution of dense stellar systems. 

2. Method 

> 2.1. Setup and Initial Conditions

The initial conditions are constructed by first treating the in-truder star m3 and the binary center of mass (COM) as an iso-lated two-body system (Fig. 1 (A)). The intruder’s asymptotic velocity v∞ is directed along ( θ, ϕ ), with impact parameter b.Assuming Newtonian point-mass gravity, the relative hyperbolic orbit is determined by conservation of energy and angular mo-mentum, yielding the relative position and velocity vectors at a finite separation rinit = 10–100 a, which ensures that the initial tidal perturbation on the binary components remains insignifi-cant. As shown in Fig. 1 (B), the binary, with component masses 

m1 and m2, is then initialized at pericenter on a Keplerian orbit with semi-major axis a and eccentricity e. The intruder is placed at the analytically computed position rinit with velocity vinit .For each simulation, initial conditions are drawn from the parameter ranges listed in Table 1. Stellar masses are distributed between 0.08 and 150 M ⊙ with m2 ≤ m1 to avoid duplicate configurations, covering the typical mass range found in glob-ular and open clusters, from low-mass M dwarfs to massive O-type stars and black holes (Kroupa 2001; Chabrier 2003; Morscher et al. 2015). The binary semi-major axis a is sampled between 0.01 and 1000 AU, spanning several orders of mag-nitude in binding energy while respecting the non-relativistic limit; even for the most extreme configuration in our parame-ter space ( a = 0.01 AU, e = 0.99, m1 + m2 = 300 M ⊙), the pericenter distance a(1 − e) = 10 −4 AU exceeds the gravita-tional radius Rs = 2G(m1 + m2)/c2 ≈ 6 × 10 −6 AU by more than an order of magnitude, ensuring that relativistic e ff ects re-main negligible (Heggie 1975; Hut & Bahcall 1983; Hut 1983; Rodriguez et al. 2015; Antonini et al. 2015). Eccentricities vary from 0 to 0.99, producing circular to highly eccentric orbits, the latter being of particular interest for gravitational wave appli-cations (Samsing & D’Orazio 2018; Rodriguez et al. 2018). The squared impact parameter b2 is sampled from 0 to 25 a, covering the full transition from head-on to distant encounters. The in-truder asymptotic velocity v∞ is sampled between 0 .05 and 240 km s −1, encompassing the velocity dispersions of various envi-ronments, from the cores of globular clusters ( ∼ 5–25 km s −1)(Harris 1996; Noyola et al. 2008; Leanza et al. 2024) to the nu-clear star clusters of galaxies ( ∼ 154 km s −1) (Krabbe et al. 1995; Schödel et al. 2009). To ensure isotropic coverage of approach directions, cos θ and ϕ are sampled uniformly in [ −1, 1] and [0 , 2π], respectively. Here θ is the angle between the intruder’s approach direction and the binary angular momentum vector (taken as the z-axis), and ϕ is the azimuthal angle measured from the binary pericentre direction (taken as the x-axis); this is im-portant because the final state of binary–single encounters de-pends sensitively on the mutual inclination between the binary orbital plane and the intruder trajectory (Antognini & Thompson 2015; Rodriguez et al. 2018). > 2.2. Data Generation

All binary–single scattering simulations are performed with the REBOUND N-body package (Rein & Liu 2012; Rein et al. 2019), using the adaptive, high-order IAS15 integrator to ensure machine-precision energy conservation during close gravitational encounters. IAS15 is widely used in the litera-ture for three-body simulations, including studies of planetary stability, asteroid dynamics, and exoplanetary systems (e.g., Rein & Spiegel 2015; Ahuja & Ganesh 2025). We adopt the de-fault IAS15 error tolerance of ϵ = 10 −9, which keeps relative en-ergy errors below this threshold throughout the integrations. The simulations are executed in parallel across multiple CPU cores. We adopt a fixed integration time of tmax = 3 tenc for all encounters, where tenc = rinit /vinit . At this time, approximately 85% of simulations have already reached a permanent final state (flyby, exchange, or ionization); the remaining 15% (hierarchical configurations) are still evolving but are classified accordingly. This fixed horizon removes time dependence from the classifica-tion while maintaining a uniform prediction framework. 3. Machine Learning Classification 

Machine learning (ML) methods are increasingly utilized in dy-namical systems where large numbers of numerical integrations are computationally expensive. In the context of the three-body problem, supervised learning algorithms can in principle iden-tify patterns from simulation data and use them to predict final states without performing N-body integrations. Recent studies have demonstrated the e ff ectiveness of tree-based ensemble models in gravitational dynamics. For example, Luiz Neto (2024) trained an XGB oost classifier on 10 5 IAS15 

simulations of the restricted three-body problem, using the ini-tial conditions as input features to predict orbital stability with an accuracy of 98.48%. Similarly, Pinheiro et al. (2025) applied XGB oost to predict stability in planetary systems using orbital parameters such as mass ratios, eccentricities, semi-major axes, and inclinations as features, also achieving high predictive ac-curacy. These results indicate that tree-based ensemble methods

(e.g., Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost) are well suited for problems with strongly non-linear and heterogeneous parameter spaces. Motivated by these successes, we apply the XGB oost algo-rithm to our su ffi ciently large dataset of binary–single scatter-ing simulations generated through the numerical procedure de-scribed in Section 2. The goal is to train a classifier capable of predicting the final state of a binary–single encounter directly from its initial conditions. To our knowledge, ML classification of such scattering across non-restricted parameter space has not been systematically explored. 

3.1. Feature Construction 

Each simulated encounter is represented by a feature vector de-rived from the initial orbital state and a set of dimensionless dy-namical descriptors. The raw initial conditions—including the stellar masses mi, binary orbital parameters and encounter ge-ometry ( a, e, ϕ, θ), the impact parameter b, and the asymptotic velocity v∞—serve as the foundation for our feature set. To capture the complex nonlinear characteristics of binary– single interactions, we compute a physically motivated set of dimensionless quantities that describe the encounter geometry, mass hierarchy, and dynamical state. These include geometric parameters (focusing parameter R, scaled cross section S, en-counter area A, and velocity ratio V), mass hierarchy descrip-tors (intruder mass fraction q3, hardness H, and binary mass asymmetry ∆), and dynamical state indicators (kinetic κ3 and potential Π energy fractions, Safronov number Θ, energy pen-etration η, energy ratio χ, and angular momentum ratio L). The binary phase Φ and separation ζ at encounter are also included. A complete definition of all engineered features is provided in Table 2. 3.2. Model Training and Hyperparameter Tuning 

We employ XGB oost (Chen & Guestrin 2016), a gradient-boosted ensemble of decision trees, selected for its strong per-formance on structured tabular data and its ability to capture highly non-linear decision boundaries. To address class imbal-ance, we employ downsampling of the majority classes to the size of the smallest class, i.e., ionization, yielding a perfectly balanced dataset of 2 .48 × 10 5 cases per class. Weighted sam-pling strategies were also tested but resulted in poor predictive performance for minority classes, likely due to the chaotic na-ture of the three-body problem; assigning higher weights to rare outcomes does not compensate for the lack of representative ex-amples in the training data, whereas downsampling forces the model to learn from equal numbers of each outcome, improv-ing generalization. While downsampling discards a significant portion of the majority class data, the resulting enhancement in predictive performance justifies this choice. A hold-out test set is reserved for final performance evaluation and is not used during model training or hyperparameter optimization. The remaining data are split into training (80%) and validation (20%) subsets. Hyperparameters are tuned using Bayesian optimization with the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler imple-mented in the optuna library (Akiba et al. 2019), optimizing for balanced accuracy on a validation set of 1 × 10 5 simulations. As in Table 3, the final configuration adopts a maximum tree depth of 18, a learning rate of 0 .02, 2000 estimators, subsam-pling of 90% of rows and 80% of columns per tree, a minimum child weight of 8, a splitting threshold γ = 0.4, and L1 and L2 regularization parameters α = 8 × 10 −4 and λ = 0.15, respec-tively. These hyperparameters prevent overfitting while allowing the ensemble to represent the intricate decision boundaries in-herent to binary–single scattering. Training is performed on an NVIDIA RTX 2070 Super GPU using the XGB oost Python library. A fixed random seed en-sures reproducibility of the data split and training procedure. 4. Results 

4.1. Classification Performance 

The model, a GPU-accelerated XGBoost classifier trained on 9.91 × 10 5 class-balanced simulations, achieves a test accuracy of 88 .32% on an unseen holdout set of 1 .98 × 10 5 simulations. The top-2 accuracy is 98 .77%, indicating that even when the top prediction is incorrect, the correct class almost always lies in the second choice. Table 4 presents the per-class classification metrics. The model performs well on identifying ionization and flyby out-comes, with F1-scores of 0 .93 and 0 .90, respectively. Exchange and hierarchical outcomes show slightly lower but still strong performance (F1-scores of 0 .85 each). The balanced perfor-mance across all classes indicates that downsampling e ff ectively mitigated the inherent class imbalance in the raw simulation data. 4.2. Probabilistic Performance and Calibration 

Beyond hard classification, the model produces well-calibrated probability outputs. On the balanced test set, the model achieves a multiclass log loss of 0 .288 and a Brier score of 0 .168, while the top-label expected calibration error is only 1 .81 ×10 −3. Class-wise one-vs-rest calibration errors are similarly small, all below 3.1×10 −3, indicating that the predicted confidence values closely track empirical outcome frequencies across all four classes (see Appendix D for calibration curves). Raw XGBoost outputs fre-quently assign zero probability to one or more outcome classes per prediction, however, this occurs for the true class in only 4 out of 198 ,148 test samples (0 .002%). Hence, within the tested distribution, a zero-probability prediction is a highly reliable in-dicator that the corresponding outcome will not occur, though this should not be interpreted as proof of physical impossibility. 

4.3. Feature Importance 

Feature importance analysis (Fig. 2) reveals that the hardness parameter H is the dominant predictor, accounting for 30% of the model’s decision power, reflecting the fundamental hard– soft binary dichotomy (Heggie 1975). The intruder mass ra-tio q3 (10%) and the focusing parameter R (10%) rank next, highlighting the importance of mass scaling and geometri-cal focusing (Hut & Bahcall 1983; Heggie & Hut 1993b). The Safronov number Θ (9%) and energy penetration ratio η (8%) further quantify the encounter strength. The remaining fea-tures—angular momentum ratio, mass asymmetry, velocity ra-tio, azimuthal angle, and binary potential fraction—each con-tribute less than 4%. 5. Discussion 

The results demonstrate that binary–single encounter final-state classes can be predicted with high accuracy from a small set of initial conditions. This is surprising given the chaotic nature of three-body scattering (Hut 1983; Valtonen et al. 2005), where exponential sensitivity to initial conditions is expected to erase predictability. However, the chaotic dynamics operate within a bounded phase space with attractors corresponding to the four fi-nal states, imposing a large-scale structure that a supervised ML model can capture. The high top-2 accuracy indicates that when the model errs, the correct class is almost always the second choice, reflecting the fractal basin boundaries between neigh-boring outcomes (Zotos 2015). Non-adjacent classes (e.g., ion-ization with hierarchical) are rarely confused. Zero-probability predictions correspond to phase space regions dynamically for-bidden for that final-state class, not numerical artifacts. The dominance of hardness H confirms the Heggie–Hut law: hard binaries resist disruption and grow harder, while soft bina-ries dissolve (Heggie 1975). However, our simulations show that the transition from softening to hardening occurs at H ≈ 2.25, higher than the classical prediction of H ∼ 1 (Appendix C). This shift arises because gravitational focusing enhances the ef-fective encounter cross-section, requiring an e ff ective hardness correction. The intruder mass ratio q3 determines the probability of exchange versus flyby (Heggie et al. 1996b), while the focus-ing parameter R captures how gravitational focusing amplifies the e ff ective encounter cross-section (Hut & Bahcall 1983). The Safronov number Θ and energy penetration η measure the in-truder’s ability to unbind or restructure the binary, controlling the transition from flybys to exchanges to hierarchical triples (Samsing & D’Orazio 2018). Misclassifications concentrate between exchange and hierar-chical outcomes because both require the intruder to lose suf-ficient orbital energy to remain bound, yet not so much that the system stabilizes into a long-lived triple or disrupts. This fine balance lies in a narrow resonant region where three bod-ies undergo multiple temporary captures (Portegies Zwart et al. 2023). Small variations in hardness or mass ratio can push the outcome across the exchange–hierarchical boundary, a hallmark of chaotic scattering (Hut & Bahcall 1983). The elevated basin entropy for this pair compared to the global value quantifies the fractal complexity of this boundary (Daza et al. 2016). The classifier reduces inference cost from minutes per direct N-body integration to milliseconds per prediction—a speedup of roughly 10 5 for a single encounter. This e ffi ciency gain enables applications previously limited by computational expense. Ana-lytic cross-section formulae capture average behavior but cannot predict individual outcomes or account for resonant branching, a gap this surrogate fills. Monte Carlo cluster simulations, for example, typically evolve 10 5 to 10 7 encounters but rely on an-alytic cross-section approximations that omit resonant dynamics (Fregeau et al. 2004; Hypki & Giersz 2013; Giersz et al. 2013). The present surrogate can replace these approximations with machine-precision predictions at negligible overhead, capturing exchange, hierarchical, and resonant outcomes that analytic fits miss. Moreover, rapid cross-section tabulation across continuous parameter grids (Heggie & Hut 1993a) becomes feasible with-out precomputing millions of numerical scattering runs, allow-ing on-the-fly outcome probabilities for arbitrary initial condi-tions. Several limitations should be noted. First, the model assumes point-mass Newtonian dynamics and excludes stellar evolution, tidal dissipation, physical collisions, and post-Newtonian e ff ects, which become important for compact objects (Rodriguez et al. 2018; Samsing & D’Orazio 2018). Second, the classification scheme relies on instantaneous binding energies and escape cri-teria, which can be ambiguous for long-lived hierarchical triples (Antognini & Thompson 2015). Third, the model’s reliability outside the sampled parameter range remains untested. Any de-ployment in applications with di ff erent encounter distributions should first validate the model within that new domain. 

6. Summary and Conclusions 

In this work, we have demonstrated that the outcome of binary– single gravitational scattering can be predicted with high accu-racy using a supervised machine-learning framework. By train-ing an XGBoost classifier on a large set of three-body simula-tions, we have shown that the final-state classification is primar-ily governed by a small set of physically motivated dimension-less parameters, with hardness ( H) being the dominant predictor. Our key findings are as follows: 1. High-Fidelity Classification: The model achieves an overall test accuracy of 88 .32% and a top-2 accuracy of 98 .77%, indicating that the scattering process maintains a high degree of global structure across the parameter space. 2. Reliable Probabilistic Predictions: After post-hoc calibra-tion, the model yields well-calibrated class-probability esti-mates, with low multiclass log loss, low Brier score, and a top-label expected calibration error of only ∼ 1.8 × 10 −3. Ex-act zero probability assigned to the true class is extremely rare on the balanced test set, indicating that zero-probability

Physical Interpretability: Feature-importance analysis shows that the surrogate bases its predictions on physically meaningful parameters, with binary hardness emerging as the dominant predictor, followed by quantities related to mass ratio, encounter geometry, and velocity. This indicates that the model captures genuine dynamical structure rather than merely fitting outcomes empirically. 4. Dynamical Sensitivity: Misclassifications are not stochas-tic but are systematically localized at the chaotic boundaries between exchange and hierarchical outcomes. This confirms that the model is sensitive to the intrinsic unpredictability of resonant few-body interactions. 5. Geometry of Chaos: Through basin entropy analysis, we quantified the complexity of these chaotic boundaries. We demonstrated that the ambiguity between exchange and hi-erarchical classes is significantly higher than in normal re-gions, providing a quantitative link between dynamical the-ory and machine-learning performance. 6. Effi ciency and Utility: The classifier reduces inference cost from minutes to milliseconds per encounter—a speedup of ∼

The surrogate captures resonant dynamics that analytic cross-section approximations miss. Looking forward, this work establishes a foundation for hy-brid dynamical modeling, in which a machine-learning clas-sifier serves as a fast screening layer for binary–single en-counters, while computationally expensive few-body integra-tions are reserved for ambiguous, resonant, or out-of-distribution cases. Because the present model now provides calibrated class-probability estimates, a natural next step is to incorporate these probabilities directly into selective-prediction or fallback strate-gies, in which low-confidence encounters are routed to direct in-tegration while high-confidence encounters are handled by the surrogate. Equally important will be extending the training set beyond point-mass Newtonian dynamics to include additional physical e ff ects such as finite stellar radii, tidal interactions, stellar evolution, and post-Newtonian corrections. Finally, ex-panding the framework to predict richer post-encounter quanti-ties—including final orbital elements, recoil velocities, and en-counter timescales—would further increase its value for cluster simulations and population-level studies of dynamically assem-bled binaries.

Appendix A: Final States; Representative Examples and Dynamical Outcomes 

> A.1. Orbital parameter changes

We found that exchange encounters produce the most dramatic changes to the binary orbit, typically resulting in very high final eccentricities approaching unity. This is consistent with the strong "focusing e ff ect" associated with low incoming ve-locities, which increases the probability of exchange outcomes (Hut 1983; Heggie et al. 1996b). In contrast, flyby encounters lead to only modest changes in both eccentricity and semi-major axis, with the binary often remaining largely undisturbed (Heggie & Rasio 1996). 

> A.2. Escape velocities

In ionization events, all three bodies become unbound (Valtonen & Karttunen 2009). The intruder transfers energy to the binary, causing its disruption, and as a result the intruder it-self loses kinetic energy and escapes with very low speeds, often remaining nearly at rest. The two former binary components gain energy and escape with moderate velocities. In flyby encounters, the intruder interacts only weakly with the binary, exchanging little energy (Heggie & Hut 1993a). It therefore escapes with a velocity comparable to its initial encounter speed. The binary re-mains largely intact with only minor orbital perturbations. In ex-change encounters, the intruder replaces a member of the binary in a close, energetic interaction (Valtonen & Karttunen 2009). The ejected original binary member carries away significant en-ergy, resulting in escape velocities substantially higher than the encounter speed. We caution that these results contain physically unrealis-tic outliers, particularly in semi-major axis distributions, which we attribute to numerical artifacts for near-parabolic orbits. The qualitative trends described above are robust, while quantitative values require careful filtering before interpretation.
