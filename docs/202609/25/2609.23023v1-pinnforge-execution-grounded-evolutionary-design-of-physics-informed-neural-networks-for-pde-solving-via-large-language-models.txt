Abstract 

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) require coordinated choices over network representation, sampling, loss construction, and optimization, while effective configurations often vary substantially across partial differential equa-tions (PDEs). Existing automated PINN design methods can search candidate configurations, but information revealed during actual training is still used mainly for evaluation rather than to improve subsequent design, leading to repeated trial-and-error and inefficient use of training budget. We propose PINNsForge, an LLM-driven evolutionary framework for execution-feedback-based automated PINN design. PINNsForge generates diverse candidate configurations from PDE-related prior knowledge, evaluates them through actual training, and feeds high-performing designs together with accumulated execution evidence back to the LLM. Guided by observed optimization behavior, the LLM then refines, recombines, and explores coupled PINN design components, forming a continual cycle of generation, execution, feedback, and evolution. Unlike one-shot search or evaluation-only feedback, PINNsForge progressively converts training experience into improved design decisions for the target PDE. Across 25 PDE benchmarks, PINNsForge achieves the lowest mean MSE on 24 tasks compared with RoPINN, PINNsFormer, and PINNsAgent. Ablation studies further confirm the importance of the PDE knowledge base, execution feedback, and evolutionary search: removing these components increases the mean MSE to 3.74 ×, 12.10 ×, and 10.10 × that of the full PINNsForge, respectively. 

# 1 Introduction 

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) provide a unified neural numerical paradigm for complex physical systems by directly embedding partial differential equations (PDEs), together with their initial and boundary conditions, into neural network training [Raissi et al., 2019, Toscano et al., 2025, Bai et al., 2025, Michaloglou et al., 2026]. However, 

reliably solving a PDE with a PINN is often substantially more difficult than constructing the PINN itself 

[Krishnapriyan et al., 2021, Rathore et al., 2024]. PINN performance is jointly affected by input representation, network architecture, collocation-point sampling, physical-constraint handling, loss construction and weighting, optimizers, and the training procedure, with strong interactions among these factors [Toscano et al., 2025, Michaloglou et al., 2026, Gao et al., 2025, Zhang and Ding, 2025, Cao et al., 2025, Zhang and Deng, 2025]. Prior studies further show that PINNs can suffer from gradient imbalance [Wang et al., 2021a], inconsistent convergence rates across loss components [Wang et al., 2022], insufficient local sampling [Luo et al., 2025], and long-horizon error propagation [Lin and Chen, 2025]. More importantly, the dominant training bottlenecks differ across PDEs. Multiscale problems may be limited primarily by the ability to represent high-frequency components, complex geometries may depend more strongly on boundary sampling and constraint handling, long-time dynamical systems may be limited by temporal causality and optimization stability, while high-dimensional PDEs and inverse problems may exhibit entirely different training behavior [Toscano et al., 2025, Bai et al., 2025, Thawon et al., 2026, Michaloglou et al., 2026, Wang and Hu, 2025, Francis et al., 2025, 2026, Yang et al., 2026, Liu et al., 2025, Ren et al., 2026]. Consequently, a network architecture or training technique that is effective for one class of PDEs cannot be assumed to transfer directly to another. An unsuitable configuration may not only reduce accuracy, but can also lead to persistent PDE-residual stagnation, inadequate satisfaction of initial or boundary conditions, severe imbalance among loss terms, or even failure of the overall solution process to converge. The central challenge across heterogeneous PDEs is therefore not the absence of a single stronger 1arXiv:2609.23023v1 [cs.AI] 19 Sep 2026 PINN method, but rather how to identify an effective combination of coupled design choices spanning network representation, sampling, physical constraints, loss construction, and optimization strategies. A large body of work has proposed effective techniques for specific training difficulties, including adaptive loss balancing, residual-driven sampling, causal training, and new network representations [Gao et al., 2025, Cao et al., 2025, Luo et al., 2025, Wang and Hu, 2025, Lin and Chen, 2025, Zhang and Ding, 2025, Zhang and Deng, 2025, Yang et al., 2026]. However, these methods typically assume in advance which problem should be addressed. For a new PDE, it is generally unknown whether representation capacity, sampling, physical constraints, or optimization will become the dominant bottleneck, and it is even harder to determine beforehand whether multiple interdependent components must be adjusted jointly. Thus, continually adding new PINN techniques does not resolve a more fundamental question: who determines which mechanisms are needed for the current PDE, and how should these mechanisms be combined? 

This challenge has motivated the development of automated PINN design [Zhou et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2025, Ren et al., 2026]. Auto-PINN uses neural architecture search and hyperparameter optimization to automatically select configurations such as network depth, width, and activation functions [Wang et al., 2023]. PINNsAgent further combines LLMs, Physics-Guided Knowledge Replay, and Memory Tree Reasoning to search for PINN architectures and training configurations across different PDEs [Wuwu et al., 2025]. Work such as Lang-PINN also shows that LLMs can participate in PDE understanding, code generation, and diagnosis of execution results [He et al., 2026]. These methods substantially reduce the reliance of PINN design on manual trial and error and indicate that LLMs are increasingly capable of handling complex PINN design tasks. However, we argue that a key gap remains in current automated PINN design: the rich information produced during real PINN training has not yet been fully exploited to guide subsequent algorithm design and search. Acandidate training run provides far more than a final performance score. Signals such as the PDE residual, initial- and boundary-condition errors, the evolution of different loss components, convergence stagnation, and numerical stability can reveal why a configuration works, where its limitations arise, and what specific design requirements the current PDE imposes. Existing automated methods, however, tend to focus more on candidate generation and performance ranking, with limited use of the specific problems and their causes exposed during training. The problem is therefore not merely how to automatically generate and evaluate more PINN configurations, but how to extract information from real training that can influence the next design round, allowing the search to actively change direction according to the difficulties actually exhibited by the current PDE. 

This challenge is further amplified by the strong coupling among components in the PINN design space. Performance bottlenecks observed during real training often cannot be resolved by modifying a single hyperparameter; they may jointly involve network architecture, activation functions, loss design, sampling strategy, and optimization. Consequently, independent parameter adjustments or predefined local search operations are often insufficient to capture interactions among components or to produce coordinated modifications in response to the observed training behavior. 

An automated design system therefore needs more than the ability to search additional candidates: it must understand the relationships among candidate configurations, training behavior, and failure causes, and use them to decide which mechanisms should be inherited, which components should be adjusted jointly, which existing designs should be combined, and when the search should shift toward new directions. 

This observation naturally connects to recent work on LLM-driven automated algorithm discovery. Studies such as Evolution of Heuristics (EoH), FunSearch, ReEvo, and LLaMEA show that LLMs can be embedded in a “generate— execute—evaluate—evolve” loop, where algorithms are iteratively modified and recombined at a semantic level based on existing candidates and their real execution results [Liu et al., 2024, Romera-Paredes et al., 2024, Ye et al., 2024, van Stein and Bäck, 2025, Wu et al., 2025, Yao et al., 2025, Zhong et al., 2025, Yin et al., 2025, Yu and Liu, 2025, Liu et al., 2026a,b, Yu and Liu, 2026, Qin et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026]. In contrast, the limited prior work applying evolutionary computation to automated PINN design mainly relies on predefined selection, crossover, and mutation operators, using final performance as fitness to search network architectures or hyperparameters [Kaplarevi´ c-Mališi´ cet al., 2023, Zhang and Yang, 2024, Carrillo et al., 2024]. This suggests a largely unexplored direction: can EoH-style LLM-driven evolutionary search be introduced into PINN design so that training behavior exposed during real PDE solving directly participates in designing the next generation of solvers? Building on this idea, we propose PINNsForge , an execution-feedback-driven evolutionary search framework 

over a unified PINN design space. PINNsForge turns each PINN training run from a mere candidate evaluation into part of the subsequent algorithm-design process. The system first generates candidates with different design preferences based on PDE characteristics and relevant PINN knowledge. After real training, it extracts execution information including performance, PDE residuals, physical-constraint satisfaction, and optimization behavior, and feeds this information back to the LLM together with high-performing candidates from the search history. The LLM then performs 2cross-component modification and recombination over representation, network architecture, sampling, constraints, loss design, and optimization through complementary strategies including conservative refinement, physics- and constraint-guided correction, architecture and optimization adjustment, multi-parent synthesis, and novelty exploration. PINNsForge therefore does not aim to propose another fixed PINN architecture or training technique, but instead addresses a higher-level automated algorithm-design question: Can an automated PINN designer learn from the actual performance of previous solvers and continually evolve solvers that are better suited to the target PDE? PINNsForge unifies algorithm design, real PDE solving, training-behavior analysis, and subsequent search within a continuously iterated closed loop. Real training results are used not only to answer “which candidate is better?” but also to determine “how should the next round change?” As a result, the LLM is no longer merely a one-shot configuration generator, but becomes a search module continually driven by real PDE-solution outcomes, extending automated PINN design from static generate-and-evaluate toward a closed-loop design-execute-diagnose-evolve process for algorithm discovery. The main contributions of this work are as follows: 1. We introduce an automated PINN algorithm-design framework driven by real execution feedback. PINNs-Forge extends automated PINN design from one-shot architecture or hyperparameter selection to multi-generation closed-loop search, jointly considering input representation, network architecture, sampling, physical constraints, loss construction, optimization strategy, and training control within a unified structured design space. Unlike methods that generate candidates primarily from static PDE descriptions, PINNsForge grounds subsequent search continually in the candidates’ actual PDE-solving behavior. 2. We introduce EoH-style LLM-driven evolutionary search into automated PINN design. PINNsForge is the first work to apply the Evolution of Heuristics paradigm to complete PINN design. Unlike conventional evolutionary search based on predefined mutation and crossover operations, PINNsForge jointly provides the LLM with PDE priors, high-performing historical candidates, and performance, physical-constraint, and optimization information obtained from real training. This enables conditional inheritance, modification, and recombination of multiple coupled PINN components according to actual candidate behavior, while allowing the search to explore new solution strategies when existing designs are insufficient. 3. We systematically evaluate execution-feedback-driven evolutionary search on a large and heterogeneous PDE suite. We evaluate on 25 PDEs spanning nonlinear, multiscale, high-dimensional, complex-geometry, long-time dynamical, fluid-system, and inverse problems. PINNsForge achieves the lowest mean MSE on 24 tasks in a unified comparison with RoPINN, PINNsFormer, and PINNsAgent. In addition, component ablations on 12 representative PDEs, a budget-matched non-evolutionary search control, multi-generation search trajectories, and low-/high-fidelity ranking analyses provide complementary evidence for the roles of PDE knowledge, real execution feedback, parent-driven joint evolution, and multi-fidelity validation in final performance. 

# 2 Related Work 

## 2.1 PINN Optimization and Failure Modes 

Physics-informed neural networks incorporate the PDE residual together with physical constraints such as initial and boundary conditions into the training objective, enabling neural networks to approximate PDE solutions without requiring large amounts of supervised data. PINN training, however, is not a simple supervised-learning problem. Different physical constraints and loss components can have substantially different scales and optimization dynamics, leading to gradient imbalance, inconsistent convergence rates, and training stiffness. Wang et al. [2021a] analyzed pathological behavior in PINN training from the perspective of gradient flow, showing that gradient imbalance across loss components can substantially affect optimization and motivating an adaptive weighting strategy. Wang et al. [2022] further analyzed the differing convergence rates of PINN loss components through the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK), providing theoretical insight into understanding and balancing multi-objective physics losses. Beyond loss balancing, the distribution of collocation points directly determines the physical supervision received by a PINN in different regions. Wu et al. [2023] systematically studied non-adaptive and residual-driven adaptive sampling and proposed methods such as RAD and RAR-D, which allocate more collocation points to difficult regions 3according to the current PDE residual. For time-dependent PDEs, Wang et al. [2024] further showed that ignoring temporal causality in the physical system can cause errors to propagate and accumulate over long horizons, and used causal training to alleviate optimization difficulties in complex dynamical systems. Beyond gradients and sampling, prior work has characterized PINN training difficulties from broader perspectives. Krishnapriyan et al. [2021] systematically analyzed failure modes in convection, reaction, and diffusion problems and showed that PDE regularization terms can induce severe optimization pathologies. Rathore et al. [2024] further studied ill-conditioning induced by differential operators and the effect of optimizer choice from a loss-landscape perspective. At the level of individual design components, Jagtap et al. [2020] studied adaptive activation functions; Wang et al. [2021b] improved multiscale representation using Fourier features and spectral-bias considerations; McClenny and Braga-Neto [2023] introduced adaptive loss balancing through trainable weights; Sukumar and Srivastava [2022] studied exact imposition of boundary conditions; Yu et al. [2022] incorporated gradient information from the PDE residual into training; and XPINNs and hp-VPINNs extended PINN designs through space–time domain decomposition and variational formulations [Jagtap and Karniadakis, 2020, Kharazmi et al., 2021]. Together, these studies show that PINN performance can be jointly affected by network representation, sampling, physical constraints, loss balancing, and optimization. However, these improvements are typically designed for specific training difficulties, whereas the dominant failure modes differ across PDEs. Multiscale problems may be limited primarily by representation capacity, complex-boundary problems may depend more strongly on constraint handling and sampling, and long-time dynamical problems may be affected by temporal causality and optimization stability. This motivates a natural question: rather than fixing a particular improvement strategy in advance, can a more suitable combination of designs be automatically discovered from the characteristics of the target PDE and its actual training behavior? 

## 2.2 Automated PINN Design 

As the number of tunable PINN design factors has increased, automated network design and hyperparameter search have been used to reduce the cost of manual experimentation. Auto-PINN introduces neural architecture search and automated hyperparameter optimization into PINNs, automatically exploring architectural choices such as network depth, width, and activation functions [Wang et al., 2023], showing that PINN architectures can be determined through systematic search rather than relying entirely on manual experience. Meanwhile, PINNacle provides a large-scale benchmark covering diverse PDE types and typical PINN failure scenarios, establishing a systematic foundation for unified comparison and cross-problem evaluation of PINN methods [Hao et al., 2024]. Beyond Auto-PINN, prior work has explored automated PINN design through neural architecture search and evolutionary computation. NAS-PINN uses differentiable neural architecture search to automatically select the number of hidden layers, neurons per layer, and residual connections [Wang and Zhong, 2024]. Kaplarevi´ c-Mališi´ c et al. [2023] proposed PINN/GA, which jointly searches network architectures and hyperparameters using a genetic algorithm. Zhang and Yang [2024] further used evolutionary computation to jointly explore network topology, shortcut connections, and parameterized activation functions, while MOPINNs applies evolutionary multi-objective optimization to loss weights and network hyperparameters [Carrillo et al., 2024]. These studies demonstrate the feasibility of automated search and evolutionary computation for PINN design, but their optimization targets are concentrated primarily on network topology, activation functions, hyperparameters, or loss trade-offs, and their search operations mainly rely on predefined encodings and evolutionary operators. In contrast, PINNsForge focuses on using performance, physical, and optimization diagnostics produced by real training as context, allowing an LLM to semantically inherit, modify, and recombine multiple design components over multiple search generations. Recent studies have also begun to use large language models (LLMs) for automated PINN design. PINNsAgent combines Physics-Guided Knowledge Replay with Memory Tree Reasoning to retrieve prior PDE-solving experience and search candidate configurations, enabling automated exploration of PINN architectures and hyperparameters across different PDEs [Wuwu et al., 2025]. Compared with conventional numerical hyperparameter optimization, such approaches allow LLMs to use PDE descriptions and prior knowledge in design decisions. Lang-PINN further adopts a multi-agent framework that assigns PDE understanding, PINN architecture design, code generation, and execution feedback to different agents, while a Feedback Agent analyzes execution results and performs iterative corrections [He et al., 2026]. These studies show that LLMs can not only understand PDE descriptions and propose PINN configurations, but can also use external execution results to further adjust generated outputs. However, real PINN training provides substantially richer information than the final performance score alone. In 4addition to solution error, PDE residuals, boundary- and initial-condition errors, changes in different loss components, and optimization stability reveal the detailed behavior of a candidate configuration. PINNsForge therefore focuses not merely on expanding the range of configurations that can be automated, but on how to continually incorporate such real training evidence into multi-generation search so that the LLM can adapt subsequent designs according to successes and failures already observed on the current PDE. 

## 2.3 LLM-Guided Evolutionary Algorithm Discovery 

The role of LLMs in automated algorithm design has recently expanded from one-shot program generation to iterative search. FunSearch combines a pretrained LLM with an automated evaluator and evolutionary program search. By repeatedly executing and selecting candidate programs, high-quality programs are reused as context to guide subsequent generation [Romera-Paredes et al., 2024]. This work demonstrates that real program execution results can provide an important signal for continual LLM-driven design improvement. Evolution of Heuristics (EoH) further makes this idea explicit as an automated algorithm-discovery framework that combines LLMs with evolutionary computation [Liu et al., 2024, Yao et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2026b]. Its core idea is to embed the LLM in a “generate—execute—evaluate—evolve” loop, such that the model not only generates candidates but also continually proposes new algorithm designs based on high-performing individuals and actual evaluation results. ReEvo uses performance differences among candidates and LLM reflection to produce language-based search feedback that guides subsequent heuristic improvement [Ye et al., 2024], while LLaMEA uses runtime evaluation, selection, and iterative modification to continually generate and optimize candidate metaheuristics [van Stein and Bäck, 2025, van Stein et al., 2025]. Together, these methods suggest that, compared with random mutation or fixed numerical perturbations, an LLM can perform a more semantic form of evolutionary operation: it can understand the structure of existing candidates and their execution behavior, and use this information to decide which mechanisms should be inherited, modified, combined, or re-explored. This capability is particularly well suited to automated PINN design because executing a PINN candidate produces not only a scalar performance value but also training evidence with explicit physical meaning. PINNsForge further applies this “execute—feedback—evolve” principle to PINN configuration search. Rather than determining the next search direction solely from final error, PINNsForge jointly uses PDE-related priors, execution experience accumulated during the current search, and performance-, physics-, and optimization-level diagnostics to provide the LLM with richer design evidence. During evolution, different search strategies perform conservative modifications, physics- and constraint-guided corrections, network and optimization adjustments, multi-parent synthesis, and novelty exploration, allowing configuration changes to coordinate around different problems exposed by actual training rather than being restricted to random perturbations of independent parameters. 

# 3 Method 

PINNsForge applies the “generate—execute—evaluate—evolve” principle of Evolution of Heuristics (EoH) to au-tomated PINN design. Rather than having an LLM produce a network architecture or training configuration only once, PINNsForge executes candidate configurations on the PDE and continually feeds performance, physical, and optimization evidence from real training back into subsequent search, so that new designs are grounded in observed solution behavior. Given a PDE, PINNsForge first combines equation characteristics with relevant PINN priors to generate an initial set of candidates with different design preferences. After real PINN training, the system accumulates solution errors, PDE residuals, constraint satisfaction, and optimization behavior, and maintains the best-performing Global Top-3 over the entire search history. The LLM then jointly modifies and recombines multiple coupled PINN design components based on high-performing parents, execution feedback, and PDE knowledge through five complementary search strategies: Refine, Physics-Guided, Architecture-Guided, Synthesis, and Novelty. The overall search process can be summarized as PDE Prior → Candidate Design → Real PINN Training → Execution Evidence → Joint Evolution This closed loop continues until the search terminates. ## 3.1 Problem Definition 

Let z denote the input coordinates of a PDE, and let u(z) ∈ Rdu denote the physical field to be solved over domain D,where du = 1 corresponds to a scalar field and du > 1 to a vector field containing multiple state variables. The target PDE can be written uniformly as 

N [u]( z) = 0 , z ∈ D ,

where N denotes the differential operator defined by the specific PDE. In addition to the governing equation, the problem typically includes initial conditions, boundary conditions, or other physical constraints, which can be written uniformly as 

Ck[u]( z) = gk(z), z ∈ Γk,

where Ck denotes the k-th constraint operator and Γk denotes the corresponding region or boundary on which the constraint is imposed. ## 3.2 From PDE Priors to Real Execution Experience 

PINNsForge jointly uses PDE-related prior knowledge and candidate execution experience during search. The former describes general physical principles and PINN design guidelines, providing directional guidance for candidate design; the latter comes from the actual training results of candidate configurations on the current PDE and is used to evaluate the effectiveness of different designs and analyze their performance bottlenecks. As the search progresses, candidate generation gradually shifts from initial knowledge-guided candidate generation toward execution-grounded adaptation supported by real execution evidence. 

3.2.1 PDE-Related Prior Knowledge 

For a target PDE P, PINNsForge first analyzes its basic problem characteristics, including time dependence, periodicity, multiscale structure, geometric complexity, locally steep regions, and types of physical constraints, and retrieves relevant PINN design knowledge accordingly. This knowledge does not directly prescribe a specific PINN configuration. Instead, it provides the relevant physical and numerical rationale to help the LLM understand the relationship between PDE characteristics and PINN design and formulate corresponding design hypotheses for the current problem. Prior knowledge therefore does not restrict the search to a predefined set of configurations; rather, it is used throughout the search together with the accumulating execution evidence to guide candidate generation and adjustment in every round. 

3.2.2 Building Execution Experience from Real Training 

Prior knowledge captures general design principles, while the real training results of candidates provide empirical evidence specific to the current PDE. After each candidate finishes training, PINNsForge extracts execution evidence from three perspectives: performance evidence, including the candidate’s final solution accuracy and its performance relative to other candidates; physical evidence, including satisfaction of the PDE residual, initial conditions, boundary conditions, and other physical constraints; and optimization evidence, including loss reduction, training stagnation, gradient or loss imbalance, and numerical stability. Together, these signals characterize the candidate’s actual training behavior and are used to analyze the main factors limiting its performance. PINNsForge organizes the execution evidence accumulated throughout the search into a run-scoped posterior memory. Unlike generally applicable PDE priors, this memory summarizes only the designs and execution outcomes actually observed for the current PDE within the current independent search run, providing problem-specific empirical evidence for subsequent candidate generation and adjustment. Accordingly, as the search progresses, the information available to the LLM gradually expands to [ PDE Prior + Observed Execution Experience,] where PDE priors continue to provide general physical and design rationale, while execution experience continually adds evidence actually observed on the current PDE. Subsequent candidate design can therefore follow general design principles while increasingly adapting to the actual training results obtained for the current PDE. 

## 3.3 LLM-Driven Configuration Generation and Evolutionary Search 

PINNsForge uses an LLM to generate complete PINN configurations rather than selecting candidates from a finite, pre-enumerated configuration set. For each generation, the LLM jointly receives relevant PDE prior knowledge, real training feedback from candidates, posterior experience accumulated during the run, and the current search role, and generates a new candidate configuration accordingly. Candidates are represented as structured AlgorithmSpecs rather than arbitrary executable code; generated outputs are mapped to existing PINN implementations only after validity, compatibility, and budget checks. We define an AlgorithmSpec as a structured representation of a complete PINN 7design that uniformly describes its representation, network architecture, sampling strategy, constraint handling, loss construction, optimization strategy, and training control. 3.3.1 Knowledge-Guided Initial Candidate Generation 

At Generation 0, no candidate execution history exists for the current PDE, so the initial population is generated primarily from the PDE description and its relevant prior knowledge. To increase the design coverage of the initial population, PINNsForge defines eight initialization roles focusing on: 1. Robust reference design; 2. Physical constraints and temporal causality; 3. Local structure and adaptive sampling; 4. Network representation; 5. PDE residual and loss construction; 6. Optimization and numerical stability; 7. Model capacity and network topology; 8. Novel cross-component combinations. These roles specify only the design focus during candidate generation and do not correspond to fixed algorithm templates. Each role receives the same PDE description and prior knowledge and independently generates a complete configuration. The resulting Generation 0 forms an initial population with diverse design preferences and serves as the starting point for subsequent evolutionary search. 

3.3.2 Candidate Execution and Feedback 

Each generated candidate is evaluated through actual PINN training. For candidate configuration ai and target PDE P,we denote the execution process as 

Execute( ai; P) → (yi, E i),

where yi denotes the primary performance metric used for candidate comparison, and Ei denotes the execution evidence obtained during training, including MSE, PDE residual, initial- and boundary-condition errors, loss evolution, optimization stagnation, and numerical stability. Candidate evaluation therefore produces not only a scalar performance metric for ranking, but also diagnostic information associated with training behavior. The former is used for candidate selection, while the latter is used during subsequent candidate generation to identify which design components in the current configuration may need adjustment. 

3.3.3 Global Historical Elite Selection 

Let Hg denote all candidates evaluated up to generation g. PINNsForge selects the three best distinct candidates from the entire search history rather than only from the previous generation: 

Pg+1 = GlobalDistinctTop3 ( Hg ) .

where Pg+1 serves as the parent set for generating candidates in the next generation. With global historical selection, high-quality configurations found early in the search can continue to participate in subsequent evolution as long as they remain competitive, preventing them from being discarded solely through generational replacement. 8The Global Top-3 simultaneously retains multiple validated high-quality designs, providing a parent pool for subsequent local refinement and cross-candidate synthesis. 

3.3.4 Execution-Feedback-Driven Joint Evolution 

Starting from Generation 1, next-generation candidates are generated from the Global Top-3, their associated execution evidence, PDE priors, and different evolutionary strategies. Conventional parameter-level mutation typically changes only a small number of independent variables, whereas representation, network architecture, sampling, constraints, loss design, and optimization are strongly coupled in PINNs. For example, insufficient representation capacity may be jointly related to network capacity, loss balancing, and optimization, so adjusting a single hyperparameter may not improve training. PINNsForge therefore allows the LLM to modify multiple related components simultaneously according to execution evidence. To cover different search directions, each generation uses five evolutionary strategies: Search Strategy Primary Evolution Direction 

Refine Use the current best candidate as the primary parent, preserve effective existing designs, and make limited adjustments targeting its main shortcomings 

Physics-Guided Adjust sampling-, constraint-, and loss-related designs based primarily on the PDE residual and initial/boundary-condition errors 

Architecture-Guided Adjust network representation, architecture, and related training configurations based on representation capacity, model capacity, and optimization behavior 

Synthesis Combine well-performing design mechanisms from multiple candidates into a new unified configuration 

Novelty Explore design combinations not yet covered by the current high-performing candidates or existing search trajectory The strategies above define different search preferences. Which parent designs are preserved, which components are modified, and the magnitude of each modification are determined by the LLM based on PDE characteristics, parent configurations, and execution evidence. Refine focuses on local improvement around the current best configuration; Physics-Guided and Architecture-Guided target issues related to physical constraints and model representation/optimization, respectively; Synthesis exploits complementary designs across multiple high-performing candidates; and Novelty expands the coverage of the current search trajectory. Through these mechanisms, PINNsForge extends candidate search from independent parameter adjustment to cross-component configuration updates grounded in execution evidence. New evidence produced by real training in each generation is added back to the posterior memory and, together with the global historical elites, forms the context for the next round of candidate generation, yielding a continual “generate—execute—evaluate—evolve” loop. 

## 3.4 Multi-Fidelity Search 

Execution-feedback-driven evolutionary search requires evaluating many candidates, while fully training every candidate would incur substantial computational cost. Inspired by prior multi-fidelity neural architecture search work, MF-NAS [Phan and Luong, 2024], we adopt a two-stage strategy that combines low-fidelity search with high-fidelity evaluation. Multi-fidelity optimization typically allocates a small initial budget to many candidates and reserves larger computational budgets for a small number of promising candidates, balancing search efficiency and evaluation reliability [Won et al., 2025]. 9At the same time, candidate rankings obtained under limited budgets do not necessarily reflect final performance after full training. Prior NAS studies have shown that proxy evaluations used during search can produce rankings that are inconsistent with the true performance of independently trained candidates [Yu et al., 2020]. PINNsForge therefore does not directly take the best low-fidelity candidate as the final result, but instead divides the overall procedure into two stages: Uniform 1K-step Evolutionary Search 

| {z }

> Low-fidelity Search

→ Independent 10K-step Evaluation of Global Top-3 

| {z }

> High-fidelity Evaluation

The low-fidelity stage rapidly advances evolutionary search under a uniform (1{,}000)-step budget while continually updating the Global Top-3. In the high-fidelity stage, each candidate in the final Global Top-3 is reinitialized and independently trained for (10{,}000) optimizer steps. 

3.4.1 Low-Fidelity Search 

In the low-fidelity search stage, every valid candidate is trained using a uniform budget of (1{,}000) optimizer steps. For each candidate, the system records the PDE residual, boundary/initial-condition errors, and convergence and stability information from optimization, and feeds these execution results into subsequent candidate generation. The goal of the low-fidelity stage is not to accurately predict a candidate’s final performance after full training, but to rapidly compare different PINN designs under a fixed computational budget, continually eliminate weaker designs, and retain more promising configurations for subsequent evolution. Generation 0 contains 8 initial candidates, followed by at most 9 generations with 5 new candidates per generation. Throughout the search, the system continually maintains the Global Top-3 over the entire search history and uses them as an important reference for subsequent candidate generation. As evolution proceeds, new candidates continually compete with the best historical configurations, enabling the search to explore and improve PINN designs under a limited budget. When little improvement is observed for several consecutive generations, the system increases the intensity of novelty exploration; if further search still fails to produce effective improvement, low-fidelity search terminates early and the final Global Top-3 is passed to high-fidelity evaluation. 

3.4.2 High-Fidelity Evaluation 

Low-fidelity search continually explores and improves PINN designs over multiple generations while retaining the best-performing Global Top-3 from the entire search history. The high-fidelity stage fully trains these high-quality candidates selected through evolution and determines the final best configuration for the current PDE. For each selected candidate, the system keeps the PINN design identified during low-fidelity search unchanged and reruns only the training process: 1. Keep the candidate configuration unchanged and reconstruct the corresponding PINN; 2. Use a new random initialization; 3. Independently train from scratch for (10{,}000) optimizer steps; 4. Select the candidate with the lowest MSE under high-fidelity evaluation as the final output. 

# 4 Experiments 

In this section, we evaluate PINNsForge on a large and heterogeneous set of PDEs and focus on four questions: 1. Overall performance: Can PINNsForge automatically discover PINN configurations with higher solution accuracy across different types of PDEs? 2. Search cost: Can these performance gains be achieved under controlled search and training budgets? 3. Mechanistic contributions: What roles are played by PDE-related knowledge, real execution feedback, and parent-driven evolution? 4. Search process: Does the final performance genuinely arise from continual multi-generation improvement driven by execution feedback, rather than from an accidentally strong configuration generated in Generation 0? 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

LLM backbone. Across all PINNsForge experiments, we use Qwen3.6-27B as the fixed LLM backbone for candidate PINN configuration generation, feedback analysis, and subsequent evolutionary search, without any additional PDE-10 specific fine-tuning. Candidate generation uses a fixed role-conditioned temperature schedule, with different sampling temperatures for different search roles; complete settings are provided in Appendix C.2. 

Benchmark. The experiments include 25 PDE problems, 21 of which are drawn from the PINNacle benchmark [Hao et al., 2024], spanning nonlinear transport, reaction–diffusion, multiscale and long-time dynamics, complex geometries, high-dimensional PDEs, inverse problems, and fluid systems. Beyond the PINNacle problems, we additionally include four classical PDEs—Allen–Cahn, variable-coefficient Darcy flow, the shallow-water system, and Kovasznay flow [Allen and Cahn, 1979, Darcy, 1856, de Saint-Venant, 1871, Kovasznay, 1948]—to broaden the range of equation structures. Definitions of all 25 PDEs are provided in Appendix A. 

Baselines. We select RoPINN, PINNsFormer, and PINNsAgent [Wu et al., 2024, Zhao et al., 2024, Wuwu et al., 2025] as the unified comparison baselines, representing PINN optimization, architectural design, and LLM-driven automated configuration, respectively. We additionally report PINNacle Best as an oracle-style reference. Because it combines the best method across different tasks, it is excluded from average-rank calculations and statistical significance tests. 

Evaluation Metrics. For each PDE, before the experiments begin, we fix a partition of the evaluation points associated with the reference solution into two non-overlapping sets: 30% as the search-validation reference set and 70% as the final-test reference set. The search-validation reference set is used only for candidate comparison, Global Top-3 updates, and stagnation and escape decisions during PINNsForge search. The final-test reference set remains unseen throughout search and model selection and is used only for performance evaluation after the final candidate has been determined. We use solution MSE on the final-test set as the primary reported metric and report the mean ± standard deviation over 10 independent runs. 

Training Protocol. RoPINN, PINNsFormer, and PINNsAgent are trained according to their respective unified experimental settings, and final performance is evaluated on the same final-test reference set. PINNsForge uses multi-fidelity search. During the low-fidelity stage, each candidate is trained for 1K optimizer steps, and the Global Top-3 over the full search history are continually maintained according to search-validation MSE. After search, the Top-3 candidate configurations are kept unchanged and each is trained for 10K optimizer steps from a new random initialization to obtain the final result. The complete PINNsForge search protocol is provided in Appendix C.1. 

Implementation Details. All experiments are run on a Linux server with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.6.0+cu124, and CUDA 12.4. Each PDE search uses a single GPU and FP32 training. Random seeds control network initialization and training sampling; complete evaluation and randomness settings are provided in Appendix C.3.

## 4.5 Training Budget 

To compare the training cost required by each method to obtain its final result, we count the optimizer-step budget used for candidate selection on a single PDE. For RoPINN, PINNsFormer, and PINNsAgent, each independent experiment trains five candidates with different random seeds, using 20K optimizer steps per candidate, and selects the lowest MSE as the final result. The total training budget per independent experiment is therefore 

5 × 20 K = 100 K

optimizer steps. PINNsForge uses multi-fidelity budget allocation. Generation 0 contains 8 candidates, followed by at most 9 generations with 5 candidates generated per generation, resulting in at most 

8 + 9 × 5 = 53 

low-fidelity candidates. Each candidate is trained for 1K optimizer steps, giving a maximum low-fidelity budget of 53K. After search, the Top-3 candidates are selected from the full search history, their configurations are kept unchanged, and each is independently trained from a new random initialization for 10K optimizer steps for high-fidelity evaluation, adding a further 30K steps. The maximum total training budget of PINNsForge is therefore 

53 K + 30 K = 83 K

optimizer steps, which is lower than the 100K budget used by the three unified comparison baselines.

# 5 Conclusion 

We propose PINNsForge, an execution-feedback-driven automated search framework over the complete PINN design space. The method jointly incorporates PDE prior knowledge, performance and physical diagnostics produced by real training, run-scoped posterior experience, and high-performing candidates from the full search history into a multi-generation search process. It uses an LLM to jointly modify and recombine coupled design components spanning network representation, architecture, sampling, constraints, loss design, and optimization, forming a closed-loop “design—execute—feedback—evolve” search mechanism. The experimental results show that automated PINN design can be extended from one-shot configuration generation to cross-component evolutionary search continually driven by real solution evidence, providing a unified and scalable implementation paradigm for automated PINN design across 17 heterogeneous PDEs. Across 25 heterogeneous PDEs, PINNsForge achieves the lowest mean MSE among the four unified comparison methods on 24 tasks, with an average rank of 1.04; ablations on 12 representative problems further indicate that execution feedback and parent-driven evolutionary search play key roles in overall performance. 

# 6 Broader Impact 

PINNsForge is intended to reduce the reliance of PINN design in scientific machine learning on manual expertise and repeated trial and error. By preserving structured candidate configurations together with their corresponding execution evidence, it also improves the traceability and auditability of the automated solver-design process. The framework may support automated model design and method exploration across different types of PDEs. It is important to note that a low reference error obtained through automated search does not by itself guarantee physical correctness or generalization. Candidate solvers may still degrade in unsampled regions, out-of-distribution regimes, or under more complex parameter settings, and may inadequately satisfy local physical constraints or conservation relations. PINNsForge is therefore better viewed as a tool for assisting scientific computing and algorithm design, and its outputs should still be assessed through independent validation, physical-consistency checks, uncertainty analysis, and domain-expert review. In addition, LLM-driven multi-round search introduces extra inference and computational overhead. Evaluations of such automated design methods should therefore consider not only final solution accuracy, but also training cost, LLM-call overhead, and overall resource consumption to support fairer and more comprehensive comparisons.

# B Implementation Details and Mechanistic Evidence for LLM Candidate Generation 

## B.1 LLM Inputs, Outputs, and Information Boundaries 

The LLM in PINNsForge does not directly generate arbitrary executable code. Instead, each call proposes only one complete structured AlgorithmSpec . A controller is responsible for subsequent parsing, validation, instantiation, and real PINN training, so the LLM cannot bypass the predefined search space or directly modify the experimental protocol. ## B.3 Deterministic Validation, Normalization, and Candidate Repair 

The LLM is responsible only for proposing candidates; whether a candidate is admitted to training is determined entirely by a deterministic controller. Every returned AlgorithmSpec passes through Parsing → Normalization → Validation → Instantiation → Training .

The validation stage checks at least the following conditions: • whether the structure of the AlgorithmSpec is complete; • whether all components belong to the allowed search space Ω;• whether different algorithm components are mutually compatible; • whether the number of network parameters satisfies the budget; • whether the sampling budget satisfies the constraints; • whether the training iteration count strictly matches the current low- or high-fidelity training budget. Invalid candidates do not enter training directly. Instead, they undergo structured repair within a fixed repair budget. Repair may address only missing fields, illegal values, or component incompatibilities; it cannot modify the target PDE 

P, enlarge the search space Ω, or relax the experimental budget. If the required number of valid candidates still cannot be obtained within the allowed generation and repair process, low-fidelity search terminates according to the stopping rule in Appendix C.4. Candidates that pass validation are reconstructed and trained according to the experimental protocol, and their actual execution results form (yg,k , E g,k ) for subsequent search. Natural-language or structured suggestions produced by the LLM are therefore not themselves considered valid candidates; only AlgorithmSpec s that pass deterministic validation and complete real PINN training are admitted to the history and the Global Top-3.
