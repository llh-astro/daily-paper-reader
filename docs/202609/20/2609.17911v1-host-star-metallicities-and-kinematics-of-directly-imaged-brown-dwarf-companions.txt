ABSTRACT 

Context. Brown dwarfs are common as free-floating objects but rare as close companions to Sun-like stars, a disparity known as the “brown-dwarf desert”. Host-star metallicity can constrain whether these companions form mainly through metal-sensitive core accretion or through less metal-dependent disc or cloud fragmentation. 

Aims. We extend our homogeneous spectroscopic analysis of directly imaged planet hosts into the brown-dwarf regime and compare their metallicities with those of planet hosts and close-in brown-dwarf hosts. 

Methods. We compiled 54 unique directly imaged brown-dwarf systems selected over an inclusive 13–80 MJup interval and projected separations from about 5 au to several thousand au. Objects near the model-dependent 70–75 MJup hydrogen-burning boundary may instead be very-low-mass stars. For 31 hosts with archival high-resolution spectra, we derived atmospheric parameters and metallicities using Bayesian spectral synthesis. Literature companion masses and projected separations are heterogeneous and often model-dependent, and are used only for demographic context. Galactic velocities were calculated for 46 hosts solely to characterise the youth-biased imaging sample. 

Results. The host stars have a broadly solar metallicity distribution, with a median [Fe /H] of +0.06 dex and a median absolute deviation of 0 .11 dex, and show no strong metal-rich bias. No statistically significant metallicity di ff erence is detected between the lower- and higher-mass directly imaged subsamples at the current sample size. The hosts are kinematically cold, as expected from the youth-biased selection of direct-imaging surveys. 

Conclusions. The absence of a strong metal-rich bias suggests that classical core accretion does not dominate the wide-orbit brown-dwarf population. Disc instability and cloud fragmentation remain plausible, but the current sample and heterogeneous companion properties do not permit object-by-object discrimination between these channels. Comparisons involving the smallest samples remain exploratory. 

Key words. brown dwarfs – planetary systems – stars: abundances – stars: kinematics and dynamics – stars: fundamental parameters – methods: statistical 

1. Introduction 

Brown dwarfs occupy a unique position in the substellar mass spectrum, with masses of approximately 13–75 MJup , brack-eted by the deuterium-burning and hydrogen-burning limits, and they are well established as a numerous free-floating population in star-forming regions and the field (Rebolo et al. 1995). As companions to Sun-like stars, however, they are markedly un-derrepresented at close orbital separations relative to both giant planets and stellar binaries, a deficit referred to as the “brown-dwarf desert” (e.g. Marcy & Butler 2000; Halbwachs et al. 2000; Grether & Lineweaver 2006). The desert points to a transition in formation and evolutionary pathways near the planet–brown-

dwarf boundary, and identifying its physical origin remains a central question in substellar demographics. Unlike stars, brown dwarfs do not sustain core hydrogen fusion, although they can fuse deuterium and, in some cases ( ≳ 65 MJup ), lithium (Bur-rows et al. 1997; Basri 2000; Chabrier & Bara ff e 2000; Burrows et al. 2001; Spiegel et al. 2011; Chabrier et al. 2023). Their in-trinsic faintness at optical wavelengths, combined with their pre-dominantly infrared emission, makes brown dwarfs challenging to detect as stellar companions (Goldman et al. 1999; Bowler 2016; Kirkpatrick et al. 2021). Indeed, the first unambiguous brown-dwarf companion, GJ 229 B, was confirmed only in 1995 through direct imaging (Nakajima et al. 1995; Oppenheimer et al. 1995). Two decades of monitoring established its dynami-cal mass (Brandt et al. 2020), and most recently it has been re-

solved into a close binary of two brown dwarfs with individual masses of 38 .1 ± 1.0 and 34 .4 ± 1.5 MJup (Xuan et al. 2024); even this well-studied benchmark was not previously recognised as a binary. Quantitatively, the deficit is sharpest at orbital periods 

P ≲ 100 days and companion masses ∼ 35–55 MJup (Grether & Lineweaver 2006; Ma & Ge 2014), where radial-velocity (RV) surveys are most sensitive. Complementary constraints at inter-mediate separations of ∼ 5–50 au, derived from Hipparcos –Gaia 

astrometric accelerations (Brandt 2021), probe a longer-period regime inaccessible to orbital-averaged RV signals and reveal a similar paucity of intermediate-mass brown-dwarf companions, indicating that the scarcity is not primarily an observational arte-fact of the RV technique. Brown-dwarf companions on wide orbits (tens to hun-dreds of au) are primarily detected through high-contrast direct-imaging surveys (e.g. Bowler 2016; Nielsen et al. 2019; Swastik et al. 2026b). These systems probe a complementary region of parameter space, overlapping with massive planets and provid-ing leverage on formation mechanisms that close-in samples alone cannot access. Three formation pathways are commonly discussed: (i) molecular-cloud fragmentation, analogous to stel-lar binary formation (Bate et al. 2002; Chabrier et al. 2014); (ii) gravitational instability in massive protoplanetary disks (Rice et al. 2005; Stamatellos & Whitworth 2009; Kratter & Lodato 2016); and (iii) core accretion followed by runaway gas accre-tion (Pollack et al. 1996; Mordasini et al. 2012). These formation mechanisms predict di ff erent host-star metallicity signatures. Core accretion is expected to favour metal-rich environments, whereas disc instability and molecular-cloud fragmentation are comparatively insensitive to metallicity and instead reflect the properties of the parent disc or molecular cloud. We use the homogeneous host-star metallicities as the primary population-level formation diagnostic in this work. Galactic kinematics provide information on stellar popu-lation membership and are sensitive to age. However, in di-rectly imaged samples they are strongly shaped by the youth bias of imaging target selection (Sect. 3.5). We consequently used the kinematics only to characterise the selected population and to confirm its youth, not as an independent diagnostic of the companion-formation channel. Observationally, a transition near ∼ 40–45 MJup has been suggested in several diagnostics— the companion mass function (Sahlmann et al. 2011), the or-bital eccentricity distribution (Ma & Ge 2014), and the host-star metallicity distribution (Mata Sánchez et al. 2014; Maldonado et al. 2017)—with lower-mass brown dwarfs showing planet-like characteristics and higher-mass objects resembling stellar com-panions. A related structural transition near ∼ 60 MJup was iden-tified by Hatzes & Rauer (2015) from the mass–density relation, below which giant planets and brown dwarfs form a single con-tinuous sequence. More recently, the combination of precision astrometry from Hipparcos and Gaia with high-contrast imag-ing has enabled the discovery and dynamical mass measurement of a growing number of benchmark brown-dwarf companions at separations of ∼ 5–50 au (e.g. Brandt 2021; Li et al. 2023; Franson et al. 2023), substantially expanding the accessible pa-rameter space. Host-star metallicity is a key empirical diagnostic of com-panion formation. Giant planets preferentially orbit metal-rich stars, consistent with core-accretion models (Fischer & Valenti 2005; Mordasini et al. 2012), whereas stars hosting only low-mass planets show little or no metallicity enhancement (Sousa et al. 2011; Buchhave et al. 2012; Narang et al. 2018; Swastik et al. 2022). For brown-dwarf hosts, previous studies have re-ported a broad distribution centred near solar metallicity with only weak or absent trends (Ma & Ge 2014; Maldonado et al. 2017). Whether any mass-dependent metallicity structure exists within the brown-dwarf regime, and whether such trends extend from close-in radial-velocity samples to the wide-orbit, directly imaged regime, remain open questions that the present sample is well placed to address. Swastik et al. (2021) presented a homogeneous spectro-scopic analysis of host stars with directly imaged planetary com-panions. More recently, Swastik et al. (2026a) characterised the protoplanet host WISPIT 2 through optical spectroscopy, deriving atmospheric parameters and a low-resolution, model-dependent global metallicity. The brown-dwarf regime, by con-trast, has remained less uniformly characterised: a significant fraction of directly imaged brown-dwarf host stars rely on het-erogeneous or indirect metallicity estimates, which limits our ability to place them in a consistent demographic context and to compare them directly with planet hosts and with the close-in brown-dwarf population catalogued by Stevenson et al. (2023). In this work, we extend the homogeneous spectroscopic framework of Swastik et al. (2021) into the brown-dwarf regime. We compiled an updated sample of 54 directly imaged brown-dwarf companions (13–80 MJup ), spanning projected separations from ∼ 5 au to several thousand au, and derived stellar at-mospheric parameters, including e ff ective temperatures, surface gravities, metallicities, and projected rotational velocities, for the 31 host stars with archival high-resolution spectra from HARPS, UVES, FEROS, and HIRES. Combined with Gaia DR3 astrom-etry and radial velocities, we examined the host-star metallicity distribution, the mass–separation demographics, and the Galac-tic kinematics, and compared them with directly imaged planet hosts and with close-in brown-dwarf companions from RV and transit surveys. The homogeneous host-star metallicities are the primary new measurement of this work; the companion masses and projected separations are adopted from the literature and are used only to place the systems in the mass–separation plane. This approach allows us to place directly imaged brown dwarfs in a broader demographic context and to assess whether their host-star properties support distinct or overlapping formation channels across the planet–brown-dwarf boundary. The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 describes the sample selection and outlines the spectroscopic and kinematic analysis. Section 3 presents the results. Section 4 discusses their implications, and Section 5 summarises our conclusions. 

2. Sample and analysis 

> 2.1. Sample selection and properties

Our sample contains directly imaged companions selected within the nominal brown-dwarf interval 13 ≤ Mcomp ≤ 80 MJup .This range spans the interval between the deuterium-burning minimum mass ( ∼ 13 MJup ; Spiegel et al. 2011; Caballero 2018) and the hydrogen-burning minimum mass, which for solar com-position lies near ∼ 73 MJup and ranges over ∼ 70–75 MJup 

depending on model and metallicity (Chabrier & Bara ff e 2000; Burrows et al. 2001; Caballero 2018). We compiled the sam-ple by querying the Encyclopaedia of Exoplanetary Systems 

(Schneider et al. 2011) 1 and cross-referencing with recent com-pilations in the literature (e.g. Bowler 2016; Nielsen et al. 2019; Stevenson et al. 2023). We retained only systems in which the companion has been confirmed as gravitationally bound by com-mon proper motion, orbital monitoring, or joint RV and astro-metric constraints.

Near the boundaries of the adopted mass range, we followed the classifications prevalent in the literature. We included com-panions whose masses are consistent with the 13 MJup limit within their uncertainties and that are commonly treated as brown dwarfs; objects falling significantly below this thresh-old were classified as planetary-mass companions and excluded from the primary sample, although several were retained for comparison (Sect. 2.3). At the upper end, we included a small number of companions straddling the hydrogen-burning limit when their nominal masses remain within 80 MJup . We re-tained the inclusive 80 MJup upper selection bound so as not to reject boundary objects whose mass uncertainties overlap the hydrogen-burning limit; we caution, however, that compan-ions with nominal masses approaching or exceeding ∼ 73 MJup 

(most notably HR 7672 B at 75 .4 MJup , with HD 19467 B and HD 4747 B also close to the limit) may be very-low-mass stars rather than brown dwarfs. We treated these systems as boundary objects and avoided using their individual classifications as de-cisive evidence for a formation channel (Sect. 4.5). For the ma-jority of directly imaged companions, masses are derived from evolutionary models in conjunction with host-star age estimates and therefore carry uncertainties dominated by the age determi-nation. Where model-independent dynamical masses are avail-able from joint fits of radial velocities, direct-imaging astrome-try, and Hipparcos –Gaia proper-motion anomalies (e.g. Brandt 2021; Franson et al. 2022), we adopted these in preference to model-dependent values. This procedure yielded a final sample of 54 unique directly imaged brown-dwarf (DIBD) systems. The sample encompasses both “classical” companions discovered through blind high-contrast imaging surveys (e.g. Chauvin et al. 2005; Marois et al. 2008) and a growing population of benchmark brown dwarfs first identified through long-baseline radial-velocity trends or Hip-parcos –Gaia astrometric accelerations and subsequently con-firmed via high-contrast imaging (e.g. Li et al. 2023; Franson et al. 2023). > 2.2. Spectroscopic and kinematic data

Of the 54 unique host stars in our DIBD sample, 31 have archival high-resolution optical spectra from the ESO Science Archive Facility 2 or the Keck Observatory Archive 3 from which reli-able atmospheric parameters could be derived: 15 observed with HARPS ( R ≈ 115 000), 1 with UVES ( R ≈ 40 000–110 000), and 1 with FEROS ( R ≈ 48 000) at ESO, and 14 with HIRES (R ≈ 67 000) at Keck. For these 31 systems we derived ho-mogeneous stellar atmospheric parameters, namely the e ff ective temperature ( Teff ), surface gravity (log g), metallicity ([Fe /H]), and projected rotational velocity ( v sin i), through the Bayesian spectral-synthesis procedure described in Sect. 2.4. The remain-ing 23 hosts could not be characterised in this way: either no suit-able archival spectrum was available, or the available spectra are of predominantly hot and /or rapidly rotating stars whose spectra contain too few unblended metal absorption lines owing to their high e ff ective temperatures and /or rotationally broadened line profiles to constrain the atmospheric parameters reliably. The derived parameters for the 31 systems with atmospheric charac-terisation are listed in Table 1. We cross-matched the full sample against the Gaia DR3 catalogue (Katz et al. 2023) by first re-trieving the catalogued Gaia DR3 source identifier for each host star from SIMBAD (Wenger et al. 2000), and then querying the 

Gaia archive on these identifiers to extract the astrometric and radial-velocity parameters. This procedure yielded a Gaia DR3 counterpart for all 54 host stars in the sample. Reliable parallaxes were recovered for 53 of the 54 systems. One target (Gliese 337) lacks a usable Gaia DR3 parallax and was therefore excluded from the subsequent kinematic analy-sis. Gaia DR3 radial velocities are available for 47 of the 54 host stars; the remaining seven systems (HIP 64892, HIP 79098, HIP 81208, GQ Lup, κ And, HD 151985, and CT Cha) lack a 

Gaia radial velocity; they are retained for the astrometric (par-allax and proper-motion) analysis but cannot enter the space-velocity computation. Combining these two cuts, full three-dimensional Galactic space-velocity components ( U, V, W) are available for 46 of the 54 host stars, following the formalism de-scribed in Sect. 2.5. The adopted parallaxes, proper motions, and radial velocities are reported in Table B.1 (Appendix B). 

> 2.3. Comparison samples

To place the DIBD hosts in context, we compare them with three complementary populations. For planet hosts we adopted the SWEET-Cat catalogue (Sousa et al. 2021), which provides homogeneous stellar parameters from consistent spectroscopic analyses; we divided this sample into giant-planet hosts ( Mp ≥

0.3 MJup ) and small-planet hosts ( Mp < 0.3 MJup ), enabling sepa-rate comparisons with the metal-rich population associated with gas-giant formation and the low-mass regime where core ac-cretion operates under di ff erent conditions. For close-in brown-dwarf companions we used the compilation of Stevenson et al.

> 2.4. Spectroscopic analysis

For the subset of 31 host stars with archival high-resolution op-tical spectra (Sect. 2.2), we derived the stellar atmospheric pa-rameters, namely the e ff ective temperature ( Teff ), surface grav-ity (log g), metallicity ([Fe /H]), and projected rotational velocity (v sin i), through spectral synthesis. The instruments used and the parameters derived for each system are listed in Table 1. The analysis was carried out with the iSpec framework (Blanco-Cuaresma et al. 2014a,b), following the methodology of Swastik et al. (2021). Synthetic spectra were computed from MARCS model atmospheres (Gustafsson et al. 2008) with the radiative-transfer code SPECTRUM (Gray & Corbally 1994) and the atomic line list distributed with iSpec . We used the same di-agnostic wavelength intervals as Swastik et al. (2021): the Mg i

triplet region at 515–520 nm, which is sensitive to log g; the 600–620 nm region, which contains numerous relatively isolated lines of Fe i, Fe ii , Ti i, Ni i, and Ca i and constrains [Fe /H] and 

v sin i; and the H α region at 654–659 nm, whose wings constrain 

Teff . For spectra a ff ected by H α emission or veiling, we used the emission-free 590–596 .5 nm interval and, where appropriate, the 610–620 nm interval. The observed spectra were fitted itera-tively with these synthetic spectra, simultaneously varying Teff ,log g, [Fe /H], and the projected rotational velocity v sin i. The parameter space is sampled with the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) ensemble sampler emcee (Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013), which yields the full posterior probability distributions of the parameters together with their covariances; we adopted the posterior medians as the best-fit values. This procedure provides robust parameters for the F-, G-, and K-type stars that make up the majority of the spectroscopic sub-sample. The few early-type hosts are more demanding. For the A-type star HIP 74865 ( v sin i ≳ 75 km s −1), for example, rapid rotation blends most lines and leaves few features suitable for synthesis. For such stars the same procedure was applied, with [Fe /H] constrained from the subset of comparatively unblended metal lines in the spectral regions least a ff ected by rotational broadening. For the FGK-type stars, the typical internal uncertainties from the MCMC posteriors are σ(Teff ) ≈ 50 K, σ(log g) ≈

0.05 dex, and σ([Fe /H]) ≈ 0.03 dex, consistent with the pre-cision achieved in our previous analysis of directly imaged planet hosts (Swastik et al. 2021). We caution that these inter-nal MCMC uncertainties are statistical lower limits; the realis-tic error budget is dominated by systematic e ff ects (the adopted model atmospheres, atomic data, and analysis method). 3.3. Dependence on companion mass 

We next investigate whether the host-star metallicity distribution varies with companion mass within the brown-dwarf regime. Following the mass scale adopted in earlier studies of the brown-dwarf desert (Ma & Ge 2014; Maldonado et al. 2017), we par-tition the BD-other and DIBD samples at Mcomp = 40 MJup , a value that approximately divides the bottom of the brown-dwarf mass function from the high-mass tail dominated by deuterium-burning, more massive substellar companions. The correspond-ing two-bin comparison is shown in panels (b) and (c) of Fig. 3. Panel (b) shows the result for the combined BD-other +DIBD sample (i.e. close-in and directly imaged brown-dwarf hosts taken together). Of the 162 hosts in this combined sample, all have a tabulated companion mass and enter the mass split. The two subgroups have nearly identical metallicity distribu-tions: companions below 40 MJup (N = 78) have a median host metallicity of [Fe /H] = + 0.06, while those at or above 40 MJup 

(N = 84) have a median of +0.01, with strongly overlapping interquartile ranges. The same exercise restricted to the DIBD sample alone (panel c) yields medians of +0.04 for the 17 hosts of lower-mass companions and +0.08 for the 14 hosts of higher-mass companions. In both panels the medians di ff er by no more than 0 .05 dex, and the boxes overlap almost entirely. We do not detect a metallicity dependence on companion mass within the brown-dwarf regime in either the larger RV /astrometric sample or in the directly imaged subsample. A complementary view is provided by Fig. 5, which shows host metallicity as a function of companion mass, from terrestrial-mass planets to the stellar boundary. The solid black curve traces a robust running median across the combined SP +GP +BD distribution, computed with Cleveland’s robust lo-cal regression (LOWESS; Cleveland 1979) in log-mass space (and in log-separation space for the lower panel). The curve uses the nearest 25% of points at each location and iteratively down-weights outliers, so that it follows the bulk of the distribution and is insensitive to a handful of extreme [Fe /H] values. The running median rises gradually from ∼ 0.00 dex at Mcomp ≲ 10 −2 MJup to a maximum of +0.12 dex near Mcomp ≈ 1 MJup , where the giant-planet metallicity excess dominates, and then declines through the intermediate brown-dwarf regime back toward solar values, reaching [Fe /H] ≈ +0.03 by Mcomp ≈ 80 MJup , the upper edge of the brown-dwarf range. The DIBD points (dark-blue diamonds) and DIP points (red stars) are broadly distributed in [Fe /H] at their respective masses, and their sample medians (large filled symbols) sit within ∼ 0.05 dex of the running median curve. The directly imaged populations therefore follow rather than de-part from the overall mass–metallicity trend defined by the much larger close-in samples.

3.5. Galactic kinematics of DIBD host stars 

We computed Galactic velocity components for 46 of the 54 unique DIBD host stars. Figure 6 presents the Toomre diagram for the DIBD hosts together with the comparison samples. The DIBD host stars are concentrated in the thin-disk region, cluster-ing at low peculiar velocity, as expected given the large fraction of young, nearby systems in direct-imaging surveys. To quantify the di ff erences between the samples, we com-puted median peculiar velocities and three-dimensional velocity dispersions as listed in Table 2. The giant-planet hosts have a me-dian peculiar velocity of ∼ 33 km s −1 and a velocity dispersion of ∼ 42 km s −1. The DIBD host sample is kinematically colder, with a median peculiar velocity of ∼ 13 km s −1 and a dispersion of ∼ 23 km s −1. The RV and transit brown dwarf hosts, by con-trast, are kinematically warmer (median ∼ 41 km s −1, dispersion 

∼ 63 km s −1), as expected for an older field population. We also divided the DIBD hosts at Mcomp = 40 MJup , match-ing the split used in the metallicity analysis (Sect. 3.3). Overall, the kinematic analysis shows that DIBD host stars are predominantly young, thin-disk objects, kinematically colder than both the giant-planet hosts and the close-in (RV and transit) brown-dwarf hosts. This is consistent with the strong youth bias of direct-imaging selection. The velocity contrast with the close-in brown-dwarf sample is therefore driven by selection (young imaged hosts versus older field hosts), not by origin, and cannot be used as evidence for a formation channel.

4. Discussion 

4.1. Formation channels and observational diagnostics 

Three formation channels can in principle produce brown-dwarf companions: core accretion in a protoplanetary disc, gravita-tional instability of the disc itself, and star-like collapse of the parent molecular cloud (Pollack et al. 1996; Bate et al. 2002; Stamatellos & Whitworth 2009; Chabrier et al. 2014). Their ex-pected parameter-space regimes, in mass ratio q = Mcomp /M⋆,separation a, and host metallicity, are summarised in Table 3. Core accretion is the only channel with a strong, established metallicity dependence (Fischer & Valenti 2005; Mordasini et al. 2012), reflecting its sensitivity to the solid reservoir of the pro-toplanetary disc: a more metal-rich disc provides more mate-rial for e ffi cient core growth. Disc instability and cloud frag-mentation depend mainly on local disc and cloud thermodynam-ics and are essentially metallicity-independent at the population level (Bate et al. 2002; Stamatellos & Whitworth 2009; Chabrier et al. 2014), although a weak residual opacity-driven depen-dence is expected through the cooling e ffi ciency of the parent gas. The metallicity distributions of di ff erent companion popu-lations should carry fingerprints of which channel dominates in each mass and separation regime. The representative ranges in Table 3 are drawn from theoret-ical models and population studies (core accretion: Pollack et al. 1996; Ida & Lin 2004; Mordasini et al. 2012; Benz et al. 2014; disc instability: Toomre 1964; Rice et al. 2005; Rafikov 2005; Stamatellos & Whitworth 2009; cloud fragmentation: Bate et al. 2002; Chabrier et al. 2014). These regimes overlap substantially, and post-formation migration or scattering can blur primordial signatures (Chabrier et al. 2014). As emphasised in Sect. 2.3 and Appendix A, we treat individual classifications as indicative rather than definitive; the meaningful unit is the population-level distribution. We use this framework in the following subsections to interpret the host metallicity distribution (Sect. 4.2), its de-pendence on companion mass within the brown-dwarf regime (Sect. 4.3), and the close-orbit deficit (Sect. 4.4).

> 4.2. Host metallicity and the mass–metallicity arc

The cumulative metallicity distributions in Fig. 7 show the giant-planet hosts systematically shifted toward higher [Fe /H], reach-ing the 50% level only at [Fe /H] ≈ +0.12, while the small-planet, BD-other, DIBD, and DIP curves all reach the median between [Fe /H] ≈ 0 and +0.06. The DIBD and BD-other curves track each other closely from below −0.3 dex to above +0.3 dex. Pair-wise Mann–Whitney tests with Bonferroni correction (Fig. 4) confirm this picture: the only comparisons that pass at p < 0.001 are SP–GP (the classical planet–metallicity correlation; Gonza-lez 1997; Fischer & Valenti 2005) and GP–BD-other. No sta-tistically significant di ff erence is detected for the other pairs at the current sample sizes; only the giant-planet sample is o ff set from the others. The DIBD–GP comparison is informative but does not survive correction at our sample size: the DIBD median (+0.06) is o ff set from the GP median ( +0.12) by ∼ 0.06 dex, smaller than the ∼ 0.11 dex o ff set that separates BD-other from GP but qualitatively in the same direction; only the latter, sam-pled with N = 131 versus N = 31, crosses the threshold. The consistency between DIBD and BD-other, and the joint off set of both from giant-planet hosts, indicates that the host-metallicity signature found for close-in RV brown dwarfs (Ma & Ge 2014; Maldonado et al. 2017) extends to the wide-orbit, di-rectly imaged regime. The mass–metallicity arc in Fig. 5 shows this directly, with the DIBD and DIP medians sitting on the running-median curve at their respective masses. We note, how-ever, that the near-solar metallicity of brown-dwarf hosts is also consistent with the distribution observed for hosts of long-period Jupiter analogues (e.g. Buchhave et al. 2018; Banerjee et al. 2024), which are themselves weighted toward solar values and only modestly metal-rich. Metallicity alone does not exclude a contribution from core accretion in the lower-mass brown-dwarf regime. Taken together, the available evidence is consistent with disc instability or cloud fragmentation, but does not distinguish between these two formation channels. A natural extension of this comparison is towards higher companion masses. Wide FGK +M binaries provide a homoge-neously analysed sample of primaries spanning the low-mass stellar regime (Montes et al. 2018; Duque-Arribas et al. 2024), and merging such samples with the substellar companions stud-ied here would allow the mass–metallicity comparison to be extended continuously from the deuterium-burning limit to ∼

0.6 M⊙.

> 4.3. No mass dependence within the brown-dwarf regime

A second question is whether the brown-dwarf population is in-ternally homogeneous. The ∼ 40–45 MJup boundary (Ma & Ge 2014; Maldonado et al. 2017) is motivated empirically rather than by a single physical threshold: it coincides with the driest part of the brown-dwarf desert and with a transition in the com-panion eccentricity distribution near ∼ 42 .5 MJup , from the low eccentricities typical of disc-formed, planet-like objects below the boundary to the higher, more stellar-like eccentricities above it (Ma & Ge 2014), and with a weakening of the host-metallicity correlation toward higher masses (Maldonado et al. 2017). It was thus proposed as the mass above which star-like fragmen-tation dominates and below which a tail of core-accretion prod-ucts could remain present; both disc instability and cloud frag-mentation are essentially metallicity-independent, so a metallic-ity step across this boundary would only be expected if a core-accretion component persists at the lower-mass end. Adopting a 40 MJup split, the lower- and higher-mass DIBD subgroups have closely similar median metallicities ( +0.04 vs. +0.08; MWU test, p = 0.65), as do the combined BD-other +DIBD subgroups (+0.06 vs. +0.01, p = 0.70). We do not recover the ∼ 0.2 dex off set reported by Maldonado et al. (2017), which we attribute to (i) recently characterised low-mass DIBDs at near-solar metal-licity, and (ii) revised dynamical masses that have shifted several systems across the boundary. The absence of a metallicity step is consistent with a negligible core-accretion contribution across the brown-dwarf mass range, and does not by itself distinguish disc instability from cloud fragmentation. A null result is not the same as ruling out two formation channels. With N = 31, our sensitivity to a ∼ 0.1 dex di ff er-ence between sub-populations is limited by the intrinsic 0 .19 dex scatter of host metallicities. A complication also remains: the lower-mass DIBDs include a substantial fraction of young ( <

100 Myr) companions identified by blind imaging of nearby moving groups, drawn from metallicity-narrow parent popula-tions, while the higher-mass DIBDs are mostly older field FGK stars selected through long-baseline RV trends and Hipparcos –

Gaia astrometric accelerations, drawn from a more metallicity-diverse pool. Any mass-dependent formation signature could be partially masked by this contrast between host populations. 

> 4.4. The brown-dwarf desert at close orbits

The classical brown-dwarf desert at close orbits is well estab-lished in radial-velocity and transit surveys at P ≲ 100 days and ∼ 35–55 MJup (Marcy & Butler 2000; Grether & Lineweaver 2006; Ma & Ge 2014), and is recovered in our sample at the same location in the mass–separation plane (Fig. 1, s ≲ 1 au). The di-rectly imaged population shows the complementary feature: the same ∼ 25–50 MJup mass range that is depleted at close orbits is well populated at projected separations of tens to hundreds of au. To localise where each sample is sparsest in mass, we ran a sliding-window depletion scan over 13 ≤ Mcomp ≤ 80 MJup , us-ing 80 logarithmic windows of width 0 .15 dex centred at points uniformly spaced in log 10 Mcomp . At each window centre we count the observed number of companions and compare it to a null distribution generated by a smoothed bootstrap of the full BD mass sample: at each of Nsim = 5000 iterations we resample with replacement from the observed log-masses and add Gaus-sian jitter equal to the Scott bandwidth of a Gaussian kernel-

> Article number, page 10 of 15 Swastik et al.: Host-star metallicities and kinematics of directly imaged brown-dwarf companions

density estimate of the sample. The local p-value at each window is ( Nsim , counts ≤obs + 1) /(Nsim + 1), and we take the smallest value across the scan as the local significance of the strongest deple-tion. Because the minimum p-value of a multi-window scan is bi-ased low, we also compute a global p-value via a look-elsewhere correction (e.g. Gross & Vitells 2010): at each outer iteration we draw a fresh null dataset by the same smoothed bootstrap, run the full scan against an independent ensemble of 512 null comparison datasets, and define the global p-value as the frac-tion of outer iterations whose minimum local p is at least as ex-treme as that observed in the data. We applied the scan to both the close-in Stevenson et al. (2023) compilation and the DIBD subsample, restricted to the 13–80 MJup regime (Fig. 8). The lo-cal p-value minima fall at ∼ 22–31 MJup in the close-in sample (local p ≃ 0.04) and at ∼ 35–50 MJup in the DIBD subsample (local p ≃ 0.013). After the look-elsewhere correction, how-ever, the corresponding global p-values rise to ≃ 0.6 and ≃ 0.2, respectively. Two points follow. First, the desert itself is not in doubt: it is firmly established at close orbits by the much larger RV and transit demographics (Grether & Lineweaver 2006; Ma & Ge 2014) and appears at the expected location in our mass– separation plane (Fig. 1). Second, the scan is simply underpow-ered at the present sample sizes to confirm that deficit on its own; the non-significant global p-values reflect the small number of systems, not the absence of a desert. What the scan does show is that the lowest-density windows in both samples coincide with the ∼ 20–50 MJup range where the close-orbit desert is inde-pendently known to lie, so it is consistent with the established picture rather than evidence against it. The interpretation below rests on the combination of the established close-orbit deficit and the well-populated wide-orbit regime in the same mass range, not on the scan in isolation. The contrast between the close-orbit deficit and the popu-lated wide-orbit regime in the same mass range can be under-stood as a combination of where each formation channel oper-ates and where post-formation evolution acts. In the 25–50 MJup 

range, core accretion struggles to deliver this much gas because gap opening limits the supply (Lubow et al. 1999; Mordasini et al. 2012), and cloud fragmentation preferentially forms ob-jects above the Jeans mass, which in typical molecular-cloud conditions is ≳ 30–50 MJup (Bate et al. 2002). Disc gravitational instability can populate the intermediate range, but fragments form preferentially at a ∼ 30–100 au where the disc is massive and cool enough to be gravitationally unstable (Rice et al. 2005; Stamatellos & Whitworth 2009; Kratter & Lodato 2016), not at sub-au separations. The net result is that intermediate-mass com-panions are produced where disc instability operates, precisely the wide-orbit regime where the DIBD sample lives, and are not produced in situ at close orbits. Post-formation dynamical evolu-tion further sculpts the close-orbit distribution: tidal interactions and migration preferentially remove close-in brown dwarfs from short-period orbits (Damiani & Díaz 2016), deepening the close-orbit desert relative to the underlying formation rate. 

4.5. Caveats and outlook 

Several limitations should be borne in mind. The DIBD spec-troscopic sample is modest ( N = 31) and drawn from hetero-geneous discovery programmes, which limits our power to re-solve sub-population di ff erences below ∼ 0.1 dex. Most com-panion masses are derived from substellar evolutionary mod-els tied to host-star ages and carry systematic uncertainties of 20–30%; where dynamical masses are available they can dif-fer from model values by several sigma (e.g. Brandt 2021).

Direct-imaging surveys preferentially target young, nearby stars, complicating direct comparisons with the close-in RV and tran-sit populations, and for most companions only projected sep-arations are known, limiting period- and eccentricity-based di-agnostics. In addition, the inclusive 80 MJup selection bound lies above the hydrogen-burning limit ( ∼ 73 MJup ), so a few of the most massive companions may be low-mass stars rather than brown dwarfs. These limitations will ease over the coming decade as Gaia DR4 and the next generation of high-contrast imagers (VLT /ERIS, JWST /NIRCam, ELT /METIS) expand the dynamically constrained sample and reach lower companion masses around mature field stars. 

5. Conclusions 

We compiled 54 directly imaged companions selected over 13– 80 MJup and homogeneously derived atmospheric parameters and metallicities for the 31 hosts with suitable archival HARPS, UVES, FEROS, or HIRES spectra. Companion masses and pro-jected separations are adopted from the literature. Galactic space velocities are available for 46 hosts and are used only to char-acterise the selection function of direct imaging. The principal results are as follows. 1. The DIBD hosts span −0.56 ≤ [Fe /H] ≤ +0.33 dex, with median +0.06 dex and MAD 0 .11 dex. We detect no sta-tistically significant di ff erence between the DIBD metallic-ity distribution and those of the close-in brown-dwarf or di-rectly imaged planet hosts at the current sample sizes. Only the SP–GP and GP–BD-other comparisons remain signifi-cant at p < 0.001 after Bonferroni correction; the GP–DIBD comparison is not significant.

2. Splitting the DIBD sample at 40 MJup yields median metal-licities of +0.04 and +0.08 dex. The present data show no statistically significant evidence for a sharp metallicity tran-sition within the brown-dwarf regime, although the modest sample size limits sensitivity to small o ff sets. 3. The DIBD hosts are kinematically cold (median vpec ∼

13 km s −1 and σv ∼ 23 km s −1) and concentrated in the thin disc. The directly imaged planet hosts show similarly cold kinematics. This is expected from the preference of imaging surveys for young, nearby targets and is not an independent constraint on formation. 4. The ∼ 35–55 MJup deficit seen at close orbits is not apparent in the wide-orbit imaging sample. This contrast is consistent with the brown-dwarf desert being primarily a close-orbit de-mographic feature rather than a global absence of compan-ions in this mass interval. The absence of a strong giant-planet-like metal-rich bias suggests that classical core accretion is unlikely to dominate the wide-orbit brown-dwarf population. Metallicity-insensitive channels such as disc instability and cloud fragmentation re-main plausible, but the present metallicity data and the heuris-tic mass–separation assignments cannot distinguish these chan-nels for individual systems. These conclusions are population-level and exploratory: the spectroscopic sample contains only 31 hosts, the reference samples are highly unbalanced, the BD-other metallicities are heterogeneous, and objects near the 70–75 MJup 

hydrogen-burning transition may be very-low-mass stars. Larger homogeneous samples and improved dynamical masses from future Gaia releases and high-contrast follow-up will provide stronger tests.
