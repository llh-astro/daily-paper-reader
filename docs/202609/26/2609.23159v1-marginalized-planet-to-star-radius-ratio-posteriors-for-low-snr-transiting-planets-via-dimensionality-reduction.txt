Marginalized planet-to-star radius ratio posteriors for low-SNR transiting planets via dimensionality reduction 

Oryna Ivashtenko 1 and Barak Zackay 1

> 1Department of Particle Physics and Astrophysics, Weizmann Institute of Science, Rehovot 7610001, Israel

ABSTRACT Occurrence rate estimates of small long-period planets rely on the radii of low signal-to-noise ratio transit candidates whose posteriors should be propagated into the population inference. These pos-teriors should include the error budget from all latent parameters and account for the effects of the correlated noise. Full Markov chain Monte Carlo fits with Gaussian processes take days of runtime per target, and provide information on latent parameters that will be marginalized over anyway. We present a method that computes the marginalized posterior of the planet-to-star radius ratio while sampling only a lower-dimensional effective parameter space. The 11-dimensional problem reduces to a 4-dimensional one, while the posterior retains the uncertainty budget of all the nuisance parameters, including eccentric orbits, stellar parameters, and limb darkening. The method operates on whitened light curves, mitigating biases due to correlated noise. We show that the posteriors are calibrated on simulated systems: the true radii have uniformly distributed p-values. Applied to 419 faint long-period Kepler Objects of Interest, the method yields radius ratios broadly consistent with the KOI table, although their differences exceed those expected from the reported uncertainties. 

Keywords: Transit photometry (1709) — Bayesian statistics (1900) — Exoplanet catalogs (488) — Astronomy data analysis (1858) 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Context of the problem: occurrence rates —Estimating the exoplanet occurrence rates using the Kepler mission (Borucki et al. 2003) data remains a topic of ongoing re-search (Kunimoto & Matthews 2020; Dattilo et al. 2023; Robnik & Seljak 2025). Kepler is still a unique mission for statistical studies of small long-period planets due to its unprecedented photometric precision, large uniform stellar sample, long continuous observation time and well-characterized detection sensitivity. In addition, de-velopment of statistical methods with Kepler data serves as a preparation for the analysis of the future missions such as PLATO (Magrin et al. 2018) and Earth 2.0 (GE et al. 2024), aiming to uncover a new population of rocky planets. An accurate and precise occurrence rate measurement requires several components: - A homogeneous search and vetting over the target cat-alog; - Reliability and completeness products for search and vetting; - Precise stellar parameters; - Precise planetary parameters; - Good selection of occurrence rate estimation method and model; - Target selection characterization. Each component of this chain is being continuously im-proved, with new detection and vetting pipelines devel-oped (Robnik et al. 2026; Ivashtenko & Zackay 2025), new statistical approaches to occurrence rates (Hsu et al. 2018; Bryson et al. 2020a), investigations of the reliabil-ity effects (Bryson et al. 2020b), precise measurements of stellar properties (Berger et al. 2020). For small long-period planets, the occurrence rate es-timation is especially challenging due to the low num-ber of detections, low signal-to-noise (SNR) complicat-ing the vetting and the parameter estimation, and low reliability and completeness in this regime (Bryson et al. 2020b,a; Thompson et al. 2018). In Ivashtenko & Za-ckay (2025), we developed a search pipeline with con-trolled reliability to reduce the false alarm contamina-tion of the small-planet catalog. This pipeline also uses injection-recovery and an empirical background distri-bution to estimate, for each candidate, the probability of being a genuine planet rather than a noise-induced detection. We intend to use the resulting candidate cat-alog and these probabilities to infer planet occurrence arXiv:2609.23159v1 [astro-ph.EP] 19 Sep 2026 2rates, allowing even low-SNR candidates that cannot be individually confirmed to contribute probabilistically to the measurement. Such an occurrence-rate analysis re-quires posterior distributions for the radii of the candi-date planets. In this paper, we present an efficient and robust method for obtaining these radius posteriors. 1.1. The role of radius posteriors in occurrence rate inference 

The primary occurrence rate product for transiting planets is the occurrence rate as a function of orbital pe-riods and planetary radii (e.g. Bryson et al. 2021; Dattilo et al. 2023). This choice is natural, since the period and radius are the parameters most directly constrained by the transit periodicity and depth (e.g. Seager & Mallen-Ornelas 2003). The orbital periods are usually measured precisely, apart from possible errors when a harmonic of the true period is found (e.g. Petigura et al. 2013). The error in the period measurement (e.g. the error reported in the NASA Exoplanet Archive Akeson et al. 2013a) is typically much smaller than the characteristic period bins used for occurrence rate measurements (e.g. Hsu et al. 2018; Kunimoto & Matthews 2020; Dattilo et al. 2023). The error in the planetary radius can exceed 10% (e.g. Bryson et al. 2020a) and may lead to misattribution of planets to incorrect bins, if binning is used (Kunimoto & Matthews 2020). In Bryson et al. (2020a) and Foreman-Mackey et al. (2014), it was shown that neglecting the radius uncertainty can bias the occurrence rate and un-derestimate its dispersion. Historically, in the planetary population inference, planetary radii were treated as point estimates (max-imum a posteriori or median values) (e.g. Zhu & Dong 2021; Petigura et al. 2013). Modern studies propa-gate the planetary radius uncertainty, for example the Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) framework (Hsu et al. 2018; Kunimoto & Matthews 2020). In it, the forward model simulates true underlying planets, as-signs them noisy observed radii sampled from analytic uncertainty prescriptions, and bins them according to these radii. Using the full posteriors, as prescribed by Foreman-Mackey et al. (2014), is still not common in planetary population inference, but is gaining acceptance (e.g. Van Zandt et al. 2026). In other fields, this approach is standard, for example, in gravitational wave astron-omy (Mandel et al. 2019; The LIGO Scientific Collabo-ration et al. 2026). In this prescription, the hierarchical Bayesian inference framework models the population as a Poisson process and derives the rates by marginalizing over the posterior distribution of each detection. Ef-fectively, it makes every candidate contribute to several parameter space bins according to the weight of the pos-terior, and avoids the artifacts of assigning one point to one bin described in Hsu et al. (2018). We intend to use this approach in our future work to incorporate the catalog information into the population inference more efficiently and avoid biases and artifacts. Thus, we need uniformly measured planetary radius posteriors for all candidates in our catalog. The poste-rior width should incorporate the entire error budget that affects the planetary radius measurement. This is particularly important in regions of parameter space with low completeness, where only a few detections shape the overall occurrence rate, and posterior tails can contribute substantial probability mass. 1.2. Motivation for a reduced-dimensional inference  

> The inference problem —

Multiple degenerate parameters influence the measured transit properties: the stellar mass, radius, effective temperature, surface gravity, and metallicity; the orbital period, transit epoch, inclina-tion, eccentricity, and periastron argument; the plane-tary radius; and other parameters in more complex mod-els. Most stellar parameters influence the model only by means of the effective stellar density and the limb darkening coefficients (Li et al. 2019; Rowe & Thomp-son 2015a; Lissauer et al. 2024). The limb darkening coefficients are degenerate with the impact parameter in how they influence the transit shape, and the im-pact parameter is degenerate with the planet radius in how they influence the transit depth. Transits offer very limited information about orbital elements, such as the eccentricity and periastron argument (Dawson & John-son 2012). In the low-SNR regime, the transit shape is poorly resolved, the data constrain almost no physical parameters, and the influence of priors is significant. In addition, Kepler light curves contain correlated (or red) noise (Jenkins 2002; Jenkins et al. 2010) that im-pacts both the detection and the parameter estimation (Li et al. 2019; Matesic et al. 2024). Approaches to treat it include Gaussian processes (Matesic et al. 2024; Alme-nara et al. 2026), pre-whitening (Li et al. 2019), wavelet analysis (Carter & Winn 2009; Csizmadia 2020), empir-ical noise variance correction (Wong et al. 2020), and detrending followed by a white-noise likelihood (Rowe et al. 2014; Alexoudi 2022; Niraula et al. 2017; Van Eylen & Albrecht 2015; Eylen et al. 2019). Detrending with a sliding average or polynomials does not properly ac-count for the frequency structure of the noise and leaves residual low-frequency noise. Gaussian processes re-quire a proper model choice to avoid overfitting and are preferably fit simultaneously with the other parameters 3(Aigrain & Foreman-Mackey 2023; Barros et al. 2020), which increases the fit complexity.  

> Current practice —

For individual systems, full poste-rior evaluations are routinely performed (e.g. Almenara et al. 2026) with MCMC or nested sampling codes (e.g. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013; Gazak et al. 2012; Eastman et al. 2013, 2019; Espinoza et al. 2019; Foreman-Mackey et al. 2021). The reported run times depend on the im-plementation and the hardware, but their dependence on the problem is instructive. A multi-parameter fit with a white-noise likelihood takes on the order of minutes on a typical computer (Eastman et al. 2013, 2019; Es-pinoza et al. 2019). Problems with ∼ 20 parameters and several Gaussian processes take up to a day (Espinoza et al. 2019). Global fits that add a spectral energy dis-tribution and stellar evolutionary tracks take from tens of minutes to hours for a single planet, depending on the initialization, weeks for complex multi-planet sys-tems, and the automated fits of all TESS Objects of Interest are capped at 2.5 days per target, a limit that most of them reach before converging (Eastman et al. 2019). For low-SNR long-period Kepler candidates, a fit of seven transit parameters on a circular orbit with a three-parameter Gaussian process noise model, sampled with nested sampling to obtain the Bayesian evidence for the planet hypothesis, takes of order a week per tar-get on a 32-thread node (Matesic et al. 2024). The cost thus grows with the number of parameters and with the complexity of the noise model, while the convergence of the samplers degrades with the correlations and the cur-vature of the posterior (Ford 2005; Eastman et al. 2013; MacDougall et al. 2023).  

> Survey catalogs —

Survey catalogs use reduced models, for example, by assuming a circular orbit, fixing stel-lar parameters, or using directly measurable values such as transit duration, ingress/egress times, or impact pa-rameter. The uniform Kepler analysis assumed circu-lar orbits and fixed the limb darkening coefficients to tabulated values, sampling six parameters with ∼ 10 6

chain elements per candidate (Rowe et al. 2014; Rowe & Thompson 2015b; Coughlin et al. 2016; Thompson et al. 2018). The Kepler Data Validation pipeline re-ports best-fit values from a damped least-squares fit with uncertainties from analytically computed Jacobians (Li et al. 2019), and the TESS Objects of Interest catalog reports the pipeline fit values (Guerrero et al. 2021). For the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope (Akeson et al. 2019), maximum-likelihood fits are planned for all can-didates, with full posteriors as a later product (Rowe 2026). These simplified methods are adequate and very help-ful in getting approximate best-fit parameters and er-rors. However, fixing the omitted parameters rather than marginalizing over them underestimates the pos-terior width and biases the best-fit values (e.g. Rowe et al. 2014). A white-noise analysis of a light curve with red noise may significantly misestimate the parameters (Carter & Winn 2009) and shrink the error bars (Carter & Winn 2009; Gazak et al. 2012). Fixing the limb dark-ening coefficients to theoretical values biases the radius ratio (Csizmadia et al. 2013; Espinoza & Jord´ an 2015). If one fixes the eccentricity to zero, one omits from the posterior the option that the same transit depth could have been caused by a higher eccentricity in periastron with a lower impact parameter and a smaller planetary radius; the Kepler validation analysis had to introduce a nuisance parameter scaling the errors of the stellar den-sity measured under this assumption (Rowe et al. 2014). The Data Validation radii are not always in agreement with the KOI table values (Akeson et al. 2013b) obtained with MCMC (Kepler 2017).  

> Requirements of this work —

Our goal is to obtain plane-tary radius posteriors for an unbiased occurrence rate estimate of small planets. We need the posterior of the radius only, marginalized over the other parame-ters, which are not measurable anyway at low SNR, but whose full error budget has to be propagated. We should avoid both biasing the median radii and underestimat-ing the spread of the posteriors, so the red noise has to be included, and the calibration has to be verified. The inference needs to be uniform over the candidates and cheap enough to be rerun: stellar parameters may be updated, and the population inference depends on the priors that may change (Hogg et al. 2010; Foreman-Mackey et al. 2014). Neither of the existing options satisfies these require-ments. Reduced fits do not preserve the error budget of the fixed parameters, fast MCMC fitters treat the red noise by detrending, and sometimes the convergence cannot be reached at low SNR. Full fits with a Gaus-sian process and nested sampling deliver full parameter posteriors, evidence, and noise hyperparameter posteri-ors that we do not use, in a regime where the sampled shape parameters are not resolved, at a cost of a week per target. A full MCMC would explore broad, degen-erate, multi-dimensional posteriors that are ultimately marginalized anyway to only get a planetary radius pos-terior that we need for population inference. Therefore, we simplify the problem rather than the sampler: we develop a method for low-SNR signals that exploits the parameter degeneracies and reduces the dimensionality of the parameter space, while preserving the error bud-4get from all the latent parameters through pre-computed priors. The red noise is taken into account by whitening the light curves before folding them, using a whiten-ing filter estimated from the data (Ivashtenko & Zackay 2025); when fitting, we also apply a high-pass filter to both the folded data and the model to remove the power from the non-informative low frequencies that the finite data length does not allow to whiten properly.  

> This work in a broader context —

The same problem ex-ists in other fields of astrophysics, where the parame-ter space is often multi-modal and more complex than for transiting planets. In gravitational-wave astron-omy, re-parameterizing the 15-dimensional model space and semi-analytical marginalization resulted in orders-of-magnitude speed-up of the inference (Islam et al. 2022; Roulet et al. 2022; Mushkin et al. 2025). In gravi-tational microlensing, the thousands of binary events ex-pected from Roman motivated replacing per-event sam-pling by amortized inference (Zhang et al. 2021), and even with fast fitters (e.g. Sandhaus et al. 2025; Abril-Pla et al. 2023) the inference would benefit from an ef-ficient parametrization (Eastman 2026). The need will grow: PLATO (Magrin et al. 2018) is expected to yield 

∼ 5000 exoplanets and Roman ∼ 10 5 transiting plan-ets (Wilson et al. 2023), and the number of candidates requiring parameter estimation will be even larger. Finally, the validation of transit fits routinely consists of convergence diagnostics such as the Gelman-Rubin statistic (Gazak et al. 2012; Eastman et al. 2019), which test whether the sampler has reproduced its target dis-tribution, not whether that distribution is calibrated with respect to the true parameters. Coverage tests on simulated transits have been done for specific questions (Carter & Winn 2009) but are not routine, whereas in gravitational-wave parameter estimation, the percentile-percentile test on injections is standard (Veitch et al. 2015; Romero-Shaw et al. 2020), as is simulation-based calibration in statistics and in simulation-based infer-ence (Cook et al. 2006; Talts et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2021; Vasist et al. 2023). We adopt this practice and calibrate our posteriors on simulations (Section 3.1). 1.3. Structure of this paper 

In this work, we describe the method developed to de-rive the planetary radii posteriors, summarized in Sec-tion 2.1. In Section 2.2, we present the mathematical formulation of the method. Implementation details of each stage of the algorithm are provided in Section 2.3. In Section 3.1, we characterize the performance of the method using simulated data. Then, in Section 3.2, we compare the radii measured for faint Kepler planetary candidates with the values reported by Kepler. Finally, Section 4 discusses the limitations of this approach and the directions for future work.

METHODS 2.1. Summary of the method 

The method exploits symmetries of the transit model and properties of the likelihood to compute the poste-rior distribution of the planet-to-star radius ratio while avoiding sampling the full multi-dimensional parameter space. The full parameter space consists of 11 parameters with prior distributions: the orbital period P , epoch t0,eccentricity e, periastron argument ω, and inclination 

i; planetary radius Rp; and the stellar mass M⋆, radius 

R⋆, effective temperature T⋆, surface gravity log g⋆, and metallicity Z⋆. Our goal is to compute the posterior distribution p(λ|d) of λ = ( Rp/R ⋆)2 given the folded binned whitened flux d. For efficiency, we want to call the transit model generator and evaluate the likelihood as few times as possible. We treat each parameter according to how it enters the likelihood: - P is assumed known because it is measured very pre-cisely from the periodicity search. - T⋆, log g⋆, Z⋆ and their priors are only used to produce the limb darkening coefficients u = ( u1, u 2) and their prior π(u). - M⋆ and R⋆ with their priors are only used to produce the prior distribution of semi-major axis a, π(a). - λ is treated semi-analytically using the approximate linearity of the transit model. - t0 is treated using the convolution theorem. - e, ω, i, a and their priors are used to construct a tem-plate bank parametrized by transit duration τ and impact parameter b. This parametrization is natu-ral because, for the observer, e, ω, i, a determine how close to the limb ( b) and how fast ( τ ) the transit is. Our template bank covers the same transit shapes as the original parameter space and preserves the original probability mass using the prior π(τ, b ). As a result, the transit model is computed only for the templates, and the enumeration runs over the 4-dimensional space ( τ, b, u) instead of the 11 original pa-rameters, while the template priors preserve the proba-bility mass and hence the marginalized λ posterior. This section elaborates on how the formalism of Sec-tion 2.2 was implemented in our algorithm. This work is designed for the cases of low-SNR (matched-filtering SNR of the folded whitened signal 

≲ 20), periods between about 30 and 500 days, and with no TTVs, corresponding to our search pipeline (Ivasht-enko & Zackay 2025), although it can be used beyond these limits. We condition on the signal being a small planet, i.e. b < 1, and no eclipsing-binary contamina-tion. A detailed list of method assumptions is provided in Appendix F. 2.3.1. Stellar parameters 

We use stellar radius, effective temperature, surface gravity, and metallicity from the Gaia DR3 GSP-Phot, and stellar mass from Gaia DR3 FLAME (Vallenari et al. 2022). For targets for which Gaia data are not available, we use the stellar properties from Berger et al. (2020). We need distributions of the stellar parameters, whereas the catalogs report only a value with asymmet-ric uncertainties. The catalog uncertainties are more-over known to be underestimated, as they do not include systematic errors (Andrae et al. 2023). To quantify the missing uncertainty, we compare determinations of the same parameters for ∼ 8 × 10 4 overlapping FGK Kepler targets: the GSP-Phot stellar radii against the FLAME and Berger et al. (2020) values, and the Gaia masses, effective temperatures, metallicities, and surface gravi-ties against those of Berger et al. (2020). The differ-ences are broader than the reported uncertainties allow, with a median absolute difference of ∼1.1–1.5 times the combined reported error, where 0.6745 is expected for consistent Gaussian errors. For each parameter, we therefore derive a system-atic error floor κ: the additional uncertainty added in quadrature to the reported errors so that the median of 

|∆|/pσ2∆ + κ2 equals 0.6745, where ∆ is the difference between the two catalog values and σ∆ their combined reported error. For mass, radius, and effective temperature we use log-arithmic differences of the catalog values v1, v2 in two catalogs, ∆ = ln( v2/v 1), and σ2∆ = ( σ1/v 1)2 + ( σ2/v 2)2.Metallicity and log g⋆ are already logarithmic quanti-ties, so we use absolute differences and obtain κ in dex. As a result, we add a 6% systematic error for the stellar radius, 4% for the mass, and 4% for the effective temper-ature, and absolute floors of 0.27 dex for the metallicity and 0.06 dex for log g⋆. Stellar metallicity, surface gravity, and effective tem-perature enter the transit model only through the limb darkening coefficients (LDC). We use the quadratic limb darkening law with coefficients u = ( u1, u 2) tabulated for the Kepler bandpass by Sing (2010). We sample the stellar parameters from their priors (Section 2.3.1) and get LDC for each sample from the interpolated table, propagating the stellar parameter uncertainty. This propagation alone is known to underestimate the LDC uncertainty (Csizmadia et al. 2013; Howarth 2011; Patel & Espinoza 2022; Maxted 2018), therefore we add scatter around every u for each stellar-parameter sam-ple. The measured u1 and u2 are known to be strongly anti-correlated (Howarth 2011), with a correlation coef-ficient between −0.75 and −0.99 depending mostly on the impact parameter (P´ al 2008). We describe the cor-related scatter in the basis aligned with the principal axes of the correlation ellipse, 

γ1 = u1 cos ϕ − u2 sin ϕ, γ2 = u1 sin ϕ + u2 cos ϕ, (14) with ϕ = 40 ◦. This places the poorly constrained di-rection γ1 at −40 ◦ from the u1 axis, within ∼ 10 ◦

of the decorrelation direction of P´ al (2008), who rec-ommends ϕ ∼ 40 ◦ in a convention rotated oppositely to ours. In this basis, we draw uncorrelated Gaus-sian scatter with the larger standard deviation along γ1,

σγ1 = 0 .4 max( γ1, γ 2), which evaluates to ≈ 0.2 for typi-cal FGK targets, matching the observed offsets between tabulated and empirically measured coefficients (Patel & Espinoza 2022; Csizmadia et al. 2013; Maxted 2018). > Parametrization efficiency —

The template bank covers the space of transit shapes only approximately, both be-cause it is discretized and because of the parametriza-tion itself. The simplifying assumptions (Appendix F) exclude orbital configurations with extreme eccentrici-ties and periastron angles; Equations 16,18 are only ap-proximate; and the effective templates obtained with Equations 19, 20 may not match the desired shapes ex-actly. To verify the coverage of the template bank, we generate transit models with full orbital parameter set, calculate their two-dimensional parametrization (Equa-tions 16,18), match them to the corresponding template from the bank, and compute the inferred planetary ra-dius from the best-fit amplitude. The distributions of the obtained errors are shown in Appendix G. From the prior-weighted transiting planet parameter set, and with the grid density used in the fit, in 95% of the cases the error is below 3%. The large error cases correspond to eccentricities e > 0.9. The transit depth is not set by Rp/R ⋆ alone; it also de-pends on the impact parameter and the limb darkening (e.g. Csizmadia et al. 2013). Since b is one of the tem-plate parameters, this dependence is largely captured. Neglecting this would lead to systematically overesti-mating radii for small b and underestimating for large 

b. In addition, we use the ( b, u)-informed initial guess on the planetary radius to generate the templates (Sec-tion 2.2). Planetary radius Transit epoch —The data are folded with the known pe-riod, and the transit epoch t0 is the position of the transit within the folded window. As mentioned in Section 2.2.2, we compute the estimator (Equation 9) in the Fourier domain, which by the convolution the-orem yields ˆλ(t0) for every integer bin shift. We then marginalize over t0 in a window around the maximum-likelihood epoch, with a uniform prior on t0, by replacing 9the Gaussian mixture over epochs with a single Gaus-sian of the same variance centered at the maximum-likelihood amplitude (Appendix H). Epochs at which the estimated amplitude is negative (anti-transits) are excluded from the window, consistently with the prior 

λ ≥ 0. In practice, above the detection threshold of our search (matched-filter SNR > 7 for the folded whitened signal; Thompson et al. 2018; Ivashtenko & Zackay 2025) the weights are dominated by the maximum-likelihood epoch, and marginalization is nearly identical to maxi-mization over t0.A non-integer shift in t0 (sub-bin shift) cannot be han-dled by the convolution theorem. It is negligible for transits much longer than a bin; for short transits it changes the binned transit shape, and in such cases we compute the model at several sub-bin shifts.  

> Mask-aware Fourier domain matched filter —

The matched-filtering estimator (Equation 9) can be computed in the Fourier domain even if the whitened folded binned flux has time-dependent variance or a mask filtering out sev-eral bins. This arises because unequal numbers of folded flux points contribute to each bin. In extreme cases, such as a small number of transits or a period close to an integer multiple of the cadence, some bins can remain completely empty and need to be masked. We include a mask vector m with zeros on bad bins and one on good bins in the weight vector in the in-ner product Equation 10, v = m/σ2, where σ2 is the per-bin variance, and vector division means per-element division. We then compute the numerator and the denominator of Equation 9 for any integer shift ( t) using the convo-lution theorem in the Fourier domain, 

⟨d, ht⟩ (t) = F−1 [F[d ◦ v] ◦ F ∗[h]] , (21) 

⟨ht, ht⟩ (t) = F−1 [F[v] ◦ F ∗[h ◦ h]] , (22) where F and F−1 denote the Fourier transform and the inverse Fourier transform, asterisk denotes complex con-jugation, and ◦ denotes per-element multiplication. The 

⟨h, h⟩ (t) term is now a vector and not a scalar as it would be without v, since it needs to be computed for every shift between the model h and the weight vector. Still, computing it has the same computational complex-ity as computing ⟨d, h⟩, so the cost is not significantly increased. For the cases where bin-wise variances are similar and there are no masked bins in the area where the whitened transit power is concentrated, we perform the fit without weights.   

> Prior distribution of λ—

Since the likelihood is Gaussian in λ with a λ-independent width, its Jeffreys prior is the improper uniform prior (uniform prior whose integral diverges). We use a prior flat in λ but bounded, in order to avoid diverging weights. For real targets, we set the upper bound of the prior in a way that it has no significant influence on the posterior shape. The lower bound at zero truncates the posterior mass at negative amplitudes, which matters only at very low SNR. In our simulation (Section 3.1), we use a narrow prior, since we also use it to sample the true values from it for calibration. As an alternative to uniform priors, it is possible to use the currently known occurrence rates, e.g. Zhu & Dong (2021). However, the occurrence rates are not known precisely enough to change the inference significantly in most cases. In principle, since we plan to use the derived posteriors for re-evaluating occurrence rates, a more ac-curate approach would be to estimate the population radii distribution self-consistently. In this approach, the prior is iteratively adjusted and used to re-evaluate the resulting occurrence until the two converge.

Target preparation 

We perform the parameter estimation on folded whitened binned light curves, after masking invalid ca-dences. An example of such a light curve for one of the targets, together with the corresponding best-fit tem-plate, is shown in Appendix A. We use the long-cadence Simple Aperture Pho-tometry after Pre-search Data Conditioning (PDC-SAP) flux (Smith et al. 2017) from Kepler DR 25 (Thompson et al. 2016) available on the MAST portal: doi:10.17909/T9488N. Our pre-processing performs sev-eral first steps of the search pipeline described in Ivasht-enko & Zackay (2025), including mean subtraction, out-lier rejection, bad cadence masking, normalization, low-frequency trend subtraction, and whitening. Folding is performed after whitening. The whitening filters of the quarters of the data are averaged to create an effective filter that is used to whiten the templates used for fit-ting. It is important to whiten both the data and the model before matching them (Li et al. 2019; Ivashtenko & Zackay 2025), otherwise the result can be biased. We note that using folded data instead of the full ephemeris carries information loss due to differences between the whitening filters of different quarters. In Appendix I, we discuss this effect and show that it is not significant for our target sample (3.2). For binning, we use a bin width of half the Kepler ca-dence to better resolve the transit shape. We upsample the average whitening filter accordingly. We account for heteroscedastic variances in the individual bins resulting from uneven number of measurements in each bin. In order to avoid fit bias due to absent power in these frequencies, we apply a high-pass filter on both the data and the templates before the fit. Specifically, we use tapering with Tukey window applied both in Fourier and in time domain to make the filter have finite response length. We attenuate frequencies below ≈ 0.38 day −1,i.e. timescales longer than 128 Kepler long cadences (≈ 2.6 days). The preprocessing (whitening, high-pass filtering, bin-ning) can itself introduce errors, on which posteriors are usually implicitly conditioned. We estimated its poten-tial error budget by running it on simulated light curves and obtained values of the order of 1%, which is not significant compared to typical Rp/R ⋆ posterior widths. 2.3.6. Inference process 

In this work, we sample the effective parameter space using grids. This is not essential to the algorithm; we chose grids for simplicity, for ease of calibration, and to inspect the posterior across the whole parameter space. More discussion about it can be found in Section 4. We evaluate the posterior on a coarse (log τ , b) grid (60 × 30, ∆ log τ = 0 .1) for all u, and then on a fine grid (800 × 30, ∆ log τ = 0 .0075) restricted to the region where the coarse posterior is concentrated. For each u

and each b, the fine cells are retained within the contigu-ous range of coarse log τ cells around the column maxi-mum whose posterior exceeds 1% of that maximum, ex-tended by one coarse cell on each side; the union of these ranges over all u is then evaluated on the fine grid. The threshold is applied per b column, so the pre-selection reduces the cost only along τ and never discards impact-parameter values. Both grids are uniform in log τ (natural log) over [−6, 0] and non-uniform in b: the cells are uniform in the transformed variable btransf = a(1 − (1 − c · b)m)with a ≈ 1.75, c ≈ 0.99, m = 0 .2, chosen such that 

btransf (1) = 1 and the cell width shrinks by a factor of 30 from b = 0 to b = 1. This increases the resolution towards large b, where the transit model changes more rapidly with b.In our algorithm, the steps are executed per target as the following sequence: - Obtain the stellar parameters and their priors, pre-pare the whitened folded binned light curve. - From the distributions of T⋆, log g⋆, Z⋆, we prepare the u grid and prior (Figure 2). - From P and the distributions of R⋆, M⋆, i, e, ω,prepare ( τ, b ) priors on a coarse grid that can be interpolated. - Prepare two grids of ( τ, b ). - For each u, compute posteriors on the coarse ( τ, b )grid, identify where the posterior is localized and repeat the process with a fine grid. - Marginalize over the grid parameters to compute the final ( Rp/R ⋆)2 distribution. An example of one processed Kepler target is pre-sented in Appendix A.

RESULTS 3.1. Performance of the method in simulation 

To validate the method, we conduct a simulation and recovery of known planetary radii. We use the noise spectrum shape of an arbitrarily selected Kepler target (KIC 002695110). We use a period of ≈ 44 .2 days, so that a planet would have 39 transits during Kepler ob-serving time. We sample the orbital parameters of the synthetic transits according to the priors described in Section 2.3.3. For planetary radii, we use a flat prior (Rp/R ⋆)2 ∼ U [0 .01 2, 0.04 2]. We simulate 10 4 folded 11 whitened transit light curves and run our inference on them.  

> p-value distributions of the true radii —

To validate our method, we consider the distributions of the p-values (the one-sided survival function) of the true radii in the posteriors reported by our algorithm (Cook et al. 2006; Talts et al. 2018). If the true radii are sampled from the same prior as used for inference, a correct algorithm should result in a uniform distribution of these p-values. The nearly flat distribution presented in the upper panel of Figure 3 shows that our method is unbiased and properly cap-tures the width of the posterior. In case of a bias, the p-value distribution would have a slope. If the poste-rior width were too narrow or too wide, the distribution would have excess mass in the edges or in the center. We note that this validation technique shows the self-consistency of the method and is valid for arbitrary pos-terior and prior shapes. An alternative way to charac-terize the performance of the method could be to look at the measurement error distribution, however its ex-pected distribution depends on the specific shape of the posterior and the prior. We also note that while this calibration is necessary, it is not sufficient to validate the method, as an algorithm returning the prior would pass this validation. There-fore, in Appendix D we additionally compare the poste-rior width distribution with the theoretical expectation.  

> Measurement error distributions —

For additional illustra-tion, we also present the distribution of the normalized measurement error, which is the deviation of the best-fit value from the truth weighted by the posterior half-width (Figure 3, lower panel). For a Gaussian poste-rior, the distribution of these normalized errors should be unit normal. In our case, the posteriors are generally not Gaussian. One of the reasons for it is the uniform prior edge effect that truncates the posteriors, resulting in larger normalized error. This effect only appears in the calibration simulation since we had to use the same prior for truth sampling and inference, to ensure con-sistency and verify the uniformity of the p-values distri-bution. In the real-data inference, the prior edges will be far from the inferred values, and this effect will not appear. Another case of large normalized errors appears for extreme values of eccentricity or impact parameter. Fig-ure 3 additionally depicts the normalized error distribu-tion excluding systems with large eccentricities and im-pact parameters or significant prior edge effect. As can be seen, in these cases the normalized error distribution is compatible with the standard normal.

Comparison to Kepler radii 

We applied the algorithm to a set of 419 selected Ke-pler Objects of Interest (KOI) and compared the re-sults to the values listed in the KOI table (Akeson et al. 2013b). We selected all the KOIs with periods 12 between 30 and 500 days, a planet-to-star radius ratio 

< 0.1, transit duration < 1 day, and that have stellar parameters in the Gaia catalog (Vallenari et al. 2022). These selections were made to match the regime of long-period low-SNR candidates and correspond to the search pipeline (Ivashtenko & Zackay 2025) whose triggers are eventually to be processed with this algorithm. Follow-ing the search pipeline, we exclude cases with a maxi-mum single-event statistic of > 7. We also exclude KOIs with multiple-event statistic MES < 7.75 (MES 2 < 60), below which the KOI catalog is contaminated by false alarms (Thompson et al. 2018). Additionally, we filter out KOI dispositioned as false positives. Since the KOI table does not provide the full poste-riors but only upper and lower errors corresponding to 0.16 and 0.84 posterior quantiles, we derive the same quantile values from our posteriors. A scatter plot com-paring the KOI table values and our derived values is presented in Appendix B. The upper panel of Figure 4 shows the p-values (one-sided survival function) of the KOI table best-fit radii in our posteriors. In the lower panel, we show the normal-ized radii difference distribution. We consider the dif-ferences between the KOI and our best-fit radii and nor-malize them either by the KOI or our posterior width. Two KOIs with least-squares-only fits and no reported uncertainties are excluded from the panels normalized by the KOI uncertainty. The p-value distribution is mostly close to uniform, apart from about 5% of the systems where the KOI ta-ble radius is significantly larger than our posterior pre-dicts. As shown by the dashed line in Figure 4, many of the low p-values come from the systems where the KOI-table impact parameter is > 0.95. Due to the impact parameter-radius degeneracy, larger b leads to larger es-timated radii. For some targets (e.g. KOI-4890.01), KOI table re-ports b > 1 (grazing configurations, relevant for large planets), which our algorithm does not allow. Our method therefore also truncates the corresponding high-radius tail of the posteriors. This effect is visible in the lower panel of Figure 4: the excess of positive differ-ences is smaller when normalized by the KOI uncertain-ties than by ours. The outliers of the normalized-error distribution are mainly cases where our and the KOI-table values of b disagree. For some of them, the Kepler Data Validation values of b differ from the KOI-table values as well (for example, KOI-4890.01, KOI-2535.01, KOI-1145.01). For the most part, the normalized error spread is similar to the standard normal distribution, which is consistent with the two estimates agreeing within their stated uncertainties. However, since both analyses use the same data, their results are correlated, so the differ-ence distribution should be narrower than the standard normal and the p-values should concentrate around 0.5 rather than be flat. The observed spread indicates that discrepancies in methods and assumptions between KOI table and our algorithm are not negligible, despite the broad agreement of the results. We regard this compar-ison as a consistency check, while the calibration of the method rests on the simulations of Section 3.1.

DISCUSSION 4.1. Computational cost 

As a measure of computational cost, we use the num-ber of transit model generations and likelihood compu-tations that we need to perform per target. Quoting wall time is not meaningful, since it depends on the im-plementation of the method, programming language and the computing resources used. The goal of this work is to validate the concept of the method and not optimize its technical implementation. In the current implemen-tation, not optimized for speed, our run on Kepler KOIs resulted in the median CPU time on a one-thread node of 48 minutes.  

> Reference costs —

For reference, we quote computational costs from several previous works, in which some model parameters were fixed to their assumed values. The Ke-pler MCMC procedure (Rowe et al. 2014) generated four Markov chains of 10 6 samples each, for each planetary candidate. It sampled 6 parameters, assuming circu-lar orbits and fixing limb darkening coefficients to their tabulated values. In Niraula et al. (2017) and Del Ser & Fors (2020), the same assumptions were used, and ∼ 10 6

samples were required. Alexoudi (2022) kept the limb darkening fixed and the orbit circular but included three free time polynomial terms, requiring 6 × 10 5 samples. In Maxted (2023), the orbit was fixed to circular and a four-parameter limb-darkening law was used, requiring 

∼ 10 5 samples.  

> Current costs in this work —

In this work, we sampled the effective parameters using grids. In this deliberately conservative approach, we had to make about 10 5 tran-sit model and likelihood computations per target. Our method assumes neither a circular orbit, nor exactly known stellar parameters, nor theoretical limb dark-ening coefficients. Thus, at equal or smaller cost, we marginalize over more parameters to get a more realis-tic Rp/R ⋆ posterior.  

> Projected cost limit —

We have not optimized the grid. We used the same grid cell sizes to cover targets with different SNR, while low-SNR targets could be processed with a coarser grid. As can be seen in Figure 6, we also compute likelihoods for a large fraction of grid cells with negligible posterior weight. Less conservative adaptive grids could provide an order of magnitude reduction in the number of models and likelihoods that need to be computed. Using grids is not an essential part of the method. The ( u, τ, b ) space can be sampled with MCMC or any other way. Such sampling will be more efficient since it will not compute likelihoods in regions of parameter space with negligible posterior mass. This method extends directly to the posterior of the planetary radius in absolute units, which the occurrence-rate inference ultimately needs. Appendix C shows that this requires modifying only the prior computation and the marginalization equation, with no additional likeli-hood evaluations. The specific implementation of this method using grids also allows, in principle, computing the evidence integral. It can be useful in calculating the evidence ra-tio distinguishing planetary signals from false positives and false alarms (Matesic et al. 2024). The dimensionality reduction used in this work can be taken further. Here, we used analytical dimension-ality reduction based on the approximate equations for transit duration and impact parameter. These two pa-rameters are strongly correlated, meaning that a more efficient parametrization can be found. For example, following the idea of Roulet et al. (2019), one can use principal component analysis (PCA) to de-rive numerically the basis of non-physical templates re-producing any transit model in the most efficient way. In Wadekar et al. (2024), this approach was taken even fur-ther, combining analytical tools and machine learning. This allowed dimensionality reduction for more general cases when a lower-dimensional manifold is embedded into a higher-dimensional degenerate parameter space. Such a case can be a bottleneck for affine-invariant en-semble samplers that handle linear degeneracies well but not curved ones. 14 Beyond transit light curves, the same idea can be applied to the microlensing signals which also suffer from degeneracies when multiple parameter combina-tions lead to the same model. Similarly to transits, the shape of a microlensing model is relatively simple, and it should be possible to parametrize it with a smaller parameter basis.

CONCLUSION We presented a method for computing marginalized posterior distributions of the planet-to-star radius ra-tio for low-SNR transiting planets. The method re-duces the 11-parameter transit inference problem to an effective 4-parameter space ( τ, b, u). The likelihood in λ = ( Rp/R ⋆)2 is computed analytically, the tran-sit epoch is marginalized using the convolution theo-rem, and the remaining orbital and stellar parameters are compressed into template priors that preserve their probability mass. The resulting posterior therefore re-tains the error budget of all the nuisance parameters, including eccentric orbits and stellar parameter uncer-tainties, while the transit model and likelihood are com-puted only for the templates. The method operates on whitened folded light curves, mitigating biases from the correlated noise. We validated the method on 10 4 simulated systems, obtaining a uniform distribution of the p-values of the true radii and posterior widths that agree with an in-dependent semi-analytical prediction. Most of the er-ror budget comes from the poorly constrained orbital parameters (under our broad eccentricity prior), while the limb darkening uncertainty is subdominant. Applied to 419 faint long-period Kepler Objects of Interest, the method yields radius ratios broadly consistent with the KOI table values, however the spread between the two methods is broader than expected. The derived posteriors are intended for the hierarchi-cal occurrence rate inference of small long-period Ke-pler planets detected by our search pipeline (Ivasht-enko & Zackay 2025), where each candidate contributes its full radius posterior rather than a point estimate. The same dimensionality-reduction approach can be ex-tended with numerically constructed template spaces. It can be applied to other survey-scale inference problems, such as PLATO and Roman transit candidates, and can be adapted to microlensing events. POSTERIOR WIDTH DISTRIBUTION VALIDATION The output of our method for a single system is the marginalized posterior of Rp/R ⋆, and here we validate its width, which is half the distance between the 0.16 and 0.84 quantiles. This width is a random variable depending on the noise realization of the data, so we examine its distribution. Using the simulated systems from Section 3.1, we compare the distribution of the 

Rp/R ⋆ posterior widths reported by our method with a theoretical prediction. The theoretical prediction (out-lined below) uses the same priors and the same transit model as our pipeline, but involves no template bank, no low-dimensional reparameterization, no linearization in λ, no representation of the λ posterior as a Gaus-sian mixture, and no common code with the pipeline. It thus additionally validates the assumptions made in our pipeline and ensures it introduces no significant biases. In Figure 8, we compare the distributions of the rel-ative posterior widths obtained from our pipeline run (Section 3.1) and from the theoretical evaluation. We bin the result by SNR and present the distribution for multiple systems with different parameters sharing the same SNR bin. All the distributions show a general agreement between our pipeline and the theoretical ex-pectation. We also plot the median uncertainty values and the reference lower thresholds of 0.5/SNR. This lower threshold is the uncertainty expected if all the nuisance parameters are fixed to their true values. As can be seen, there is a significant difference between the ac-tual medians and this lower threshold, arising from the uncertainties in the nuisance parameters. Appendix E further explores the uncertainty budget of the nuisance parameters. The theoretical prediction for the Rp/R ⋆ posterior width and its distribution arises from evaluating the in-tegral over the nuisance parameters in Equation 1. The integral in Equation 1 has to be evaluated on a grid of 

Rp/R ⋆ and over the noise realizations, which drive the variation of the posterior width. We evaluate the 6-dimensional integral over θ by Monte Carlo over the prior, using importance sampling. Computing the full integral brute-force with loops over 

Rp/R ⋆ and noise realizations would be computationally heavy, so we use two simplifications. First, we use the fact that the model s is a smooth function of Rp/R ⋆, comprising ( Rp/R ⋆)2 depth scaling and slow changes in the transit shape. ASSUMPTIONS OF THE ALGORITHM The method is tailored to marginalized radius poste-riors of small, long-period, low-SNR planets from our 19 search (Ivashtenko & Zackay 2025). For this problem, we make the following simplifying assumptions: - We assume there is no transit timing, duration, or depth variations. We take the period as exactly defined from the search. - We assume the transits to be symmetric in time. This is not true for high-eccentricity transits with some periastron angles. - We assume the impact parameter to be a valid description for a transit, meaning that the sky-projected planet trajectory is straight and sym-metric. This assumption also fails for extreme val-ues of eccentricity. - We assume that star-planet distance in transit sig-nificantly exceeds the stellar radius. It is true for the planetary periods that we consider ( > 30 days) and dwarf stars. - We assume that the planet-to-star radius ratio is sufficiently small that the impact parameter re-mains < 1, and that the planetary radius affects the transit light curve primarily through the tran-sit depth, with a smaller effect on the ingress and egress. - We condition the analysis on the assumption that the signal is a planet and not an eclipsing binary. - We neglect any light emitted by the planet. - We do not infer stellar parameters from the light curves. We use external stellar parameters and neglect correlations between them. - We neglect blending effects, assuming that the flux is due to the target star, relying on the correction of the Kepler light curves for crowding. - We do not model possible spot crossings or other contaminants. We also assume that the bad cadences were removed in the search or post-processing, and all the signal considered here is due to transits. - We do not explore the posterior dependence on the preprocessing and whitening procedure. - We limit the analysis to the parameters introduced in Section 2.1 and do not consider other effects, such as, for example, gravity darkening. Some of these assumptions are linked to our parametrization not describing adequately cases of ex-treme eccentricities, as is demonstrated in Appendix G. We assume these cases to be rare and not to con-tribute significantly to the analysis. Other assumptions are linked to the fact that we work with low-SNR signals, therefore many effects are not well-measurable.

TEMPLATE BANK ERROR In this Appendix, we verify the precision of the effec-tive orbital parameters approximation and the template bank approach (Section 2.3.3). For this, we consider the amplitude of the expected transit models measured with the approximate templates. The model amplitude is defined as the estimate (Equation 9) with d = strue 

and h = sbank , where s is a transit model (Equation 6). Amplitude error is the difference between the amplitude for a given template and a perfect match amplitude. A perfect match would give an amplitude of 1 and error of 0. An error of 1 means that the measured radius squared is twice the true one. We generate noiseless transit models using the orbital parameter prior from Section 3.1. From the parameters of each model, we calculate analytical effective orbital parameters (Section 2.3.3) and find the corresponding template in the template bank. We then use Equation 9 to measure the transit amplitude using this template. In the left panel of Figure 10, we show the distribution of the amplitude errors when our template bank method is applied to the sampled transit population. We plot a survival distribution showing how frequently we get an error larger than a given value. As can be seen, for 95% of the cases, we get errors < 3%, which corresponds to < 2% error on the radius. In rare cases, we can get significant errors, measuring a few times larger ampli-tudes. The right panel of Figure 10 shows that these cases correspond to large eccentricities. In the right panel of Figure 10, we show the 95% quan-tile of the error distribution as a function of orbital coor-dinates, where e is eccentricity, and ω is periastron argu-ment. For every cell in the diagram, there is a distribu-tion of amplitude measurement errors obtained for the transits from this cell. We report the value of the error corresponding to the 95% quantile of this distribution. As can be seen, all the values of > 5% are located at ex-treme eccentricities, e > 0.9. The ω values of the large-error cases also correspond to the asymmetric-transit configurations excluded by the symmetry assumption in Appendix F.
