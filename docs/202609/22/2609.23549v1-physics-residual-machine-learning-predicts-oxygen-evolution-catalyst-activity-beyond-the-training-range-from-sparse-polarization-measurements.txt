Abstract 

Screening oxygen-evolution catalysts on combinatorial libraries requires deciding which candidates receive the remaining measurements. The deciding activity lies beyond each candidate’s measured potential window and often above every activity recorded during fitting. We predict it by physics-residual machine learning: the Tafel equation extrapolates the candidate’s own measured current and slope, a learned residual attenuated with feature-space distance corrects the magnitude, and an applicability-domain score identifies predictions above the training range before measurement. In a separately fabricated 322-candidate library, 282 above the training maximum, two measurements per candidate gave a mean absolute error of 0.203 mA cm −2 against 1.330 for the selected data-driven machine-learning model. Errors inside the training range remained comparable, and 35 labelled catalysts were enough to fit it. In two independent datasets the same construction lowered the overpotential error by 29 to 52%. Campaigns can therefore shorten each measurement and still rank the most active compositions. 

# Introduction 

Water electrolysis stores electrical energy in hydrogen, and the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) at the anode accounts for a large part of the energy cost of that conversion [1, 2]. The search for better OER catalysts is increasingly carried out on combinatorial libraries, in which many compositions are prepared together and measured electrochemically under identical conditions [3, 4]. Screening on these platforms requires a computational decision: given the measurements already collected on a new library, which candidates should receive the measurements that remain [5]? Active learning, Bayesian optimization and autonomous laboratories answer this question by selecting the next candidate to measure, and the activity of each selected candidate is then measured in full [6–9] (Supplementary Table 27). Answering this question without measuring each candidate in full requires predicting, for each candidate, a quantity that has not been measured from quantities that have. A candidate’s composition and an early part of its polarization response are obtained at low cost, whereas the quantity used to decide which candidates are measured further, the current density at the operating potential (the endpoint activity), becomes available once that candidate has been measured up to that potential [3]. Shortening the measurement of each candidate to an early part therefore requires predicting a later value of the same measurement, a prediction reported for the cycle life of battery cells, the capacity of battery cathodes and the lifetime of electrocatalysts [10–14] (Supplementary Table 28). For the activity of OER catalysts, three conditions apply simultaneously to such a prediction when the remaining measurements are assigned, and the first two are extrapolations of two different quantities. 1) Extrapolation in potential : the required current density lies at the operating potential at which candidates are ranked [3], above the window in which each candidate was measured. 2) Extrapolation in activity : a newly prepared library can contain candidates more active than any measured before, and finding those candidates is the purpose of the campaign [3, 5], so the activities of interest lie above the range of the training labels. 3) Scarce measurements : shortening the measurement of each candidate leaves 1arXiv:2609.23549v1 [cs.LG] 20 Sep 2026 Table 1: Relation of representative prior work to catalyst-material prediction, prediction-model construction and sparse-response extrapolation. few response points per candidate, and few catalysts in a campaign have been measured up to the operating potential [6, 8]. Four lines of work address parts of the problem. A) Composition-descriptor, graph and physics-guided property models calculate target properties accurately enough to guide screening [4, 15–17]; assessed on random data partitions they report higher accuracy than they reach on test sets drawn from outside the training distribution [18, 19]. B) Methods for small training sets modify the estimator through joint learning, mixtures of experts and in-context foundation models [20, 21], but they learn the activity itself from the labelled catalysts, so their predictions depend on labelled catalysts in the activity range of the new candidates. 

C) Methods designed for targets outside that range describe a new candidate by its offset from a training example [22, 23], so the reference value of each prediction is a training label. D) Physics-informed neural networks add a known physical relation to the loss function, the output or the architecture of a learning model [24–26] and have been applied in electrochemistry to device states and to fully measured response curves [27–29] rather than to one catalyst measured in part; when the relation enters only through a loss term [30, 31], the trained network computes its output without evaluating it. Hybrid models combine a physical term with a learned component [32–37]; in these models the physical term is specified in advance or obtained from other samples, not from measurements of the candidate being predicted. Extrapolation to a later value of the same measurement, prediction above the range of the training labels and fitting from few labelled catalysts are therefore addressed in separate lines of work. Table 1 summarizes them alongside this study. To address these issues, we predict the activity of each candidate with a physics-residual machine-learning construction built on that candidate’s own measurements: the Tafel equation extrapolates the current measured at the highest observed potential to the operating potential, and a residual learned by machine-learning model, attenuated with feature-space distance from the fitted candidates, is added to that extrapolation. An applicability-domain score, defined in Methods, reports before any endpoint is measured which predictions extend above the training range. The extrapolation carries the prediction to the operating potential (condition 1), and because it takes its reference value from the candidate’s own measured current and slope rather than 2from a training label, the physical relation is evaluated with each candidate’s measurements and the predicted activity is not bounded by the activities recorded during fitting (condition 2). The learned residual accounts for the difference between the extrapolation and the measured activity, not for the activity itself, so it needs no labels in the activity range of the new candidates and few labelled catalysts suffice to fit it (condition 3); together these properties address the limitations A) to D). In a separately prepared library whose activities lie mostly above the training range, the construction predicted the current density at the operating potential from two measurements per candidate with a mean absolute error of 0.203 mA cm −2, against 1.330 mA cm −2 for the data-driven model selected by the same leave-one-library-out validation, and with 0.191 against 2.486 mA cm −2 for the candidates whose activity lies more than two interquartile ranges of the logarithmic training activities above the training maximum (the high-displacement candidates). This work makes four contributions. First , it provides an endpoint-activity prediction accurate enough to allocate the measurements remaining in a newly prepared library, including when the activity to be predicted exceeds every training label and when the fitting set holds as few as 35 labelled catalysts. The applicability-domain score identifies, before any endpoint is measured, the candidates whose prediction extends above the training range, and the same model is applied to candidates inside and above it. Second , it shows that the endpoint-error advantage arises where the two extrapolations combine: with two response measurements the data-driven models reach errors similar to those of the construction inside the training range, where only the potential is extrapolated, and errors 13 to 18 times larger for the high-displacement candidates, where potential and activity are extrapolated together. For those candidates the construction has a lower endpoint error than each of the data-driven models at every number of response measurements from one to seventeen. Third , it separates the error component that each of the two terms removes, which gives a basis for deciding which part to express as a physical relation and which part to learn when the same construction is built for another problem. At two and three measurements the Tafel extrapolation alone orders the candidates, with Spearman correlations of 0.98 and 0.97 for the high-displacement candidates, and the learned residual reduces the remaining error in activity magnitude. Fourth , it shows that the construction is not specific to one measurement campaign: with two or more response measurements, it reduced the endpoint overpotential error relative to the data-driven model selected by the same procedure in two OER datasets collected independently in other laboratories with other measurement protocols, and in both datasets its error stayed within 0.3 mV of the lowest error reached by any of the compared models [7, 8, 38, 39]. A screening campaign can therefore shorten the measurement of every new candidate to an early part of its polarization response and use the predicted activities, including those above the training labels where the candidates worth advancing lie, to choose which candidates receive the measurements that remain. 

# Results 

Experimental design: a held-out library whose activities lie mostly above the largest training label 

The Ni–Pd–Pt–Ru dataset [3] contains 4,026 candidate measurement sites in twelve materials libraries. In fabrication order, libraries 1–5, the development libraries, supplied 1,685 candidates for fitting and model selection, and library 6 supplied 322 candidates for evaluation. No candidate from library 6 entered fitting, preprocessing choices or hyperparameter selection. Libraries 7–12 provided a later evaluation within the activity range already represented after library 6 was added to the fitting set. Table 2 lists every dataset and its validation role, and Supplementary Note 1 defines the evaluation populations and metrics. For each evaluation candidate, one, two, three, five, nine or seventeen current-density measurements were retained from the response window of 1.40 to 1.55 V versus the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE); the number of retained measurements is the observation budget. Current density at 1.70 V was withheld as the activity endpoint (Fig. 1a). Every observation budget requires response extrapolation: budgets of two or more retained a 0.15-V gap from the highest observed potential, whereas the midpoint-only one-measurement design required a 0.225-V gap. The physics-residual model received elemental fractions and the retained response measurements. Its non-negative Tafel slope combines the median slope of the complete response windows of the fitting libraries, the global slope prior, with each candidate’s own slope over its two highest observed potentials (Methods). With this slope the Tafel equation extrapolated each partial response from the highest observed potential to 1.70 V. A learned residual used composition and response features to correct systematic candidate-level deviations, and a feature-distance function reduced this correction outside the fitted domain. Ridge regression, extremely randomized trees (Extra Trees), histogram gradient boosting and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) received the same composition and sparse-response inputs. Every model of the primary comparison (Table 3), the physics-residual model included, received one setting chosen by the same leave-one-library-out validation within libraries 1–5 and used at every observation budget (Supplementary Note 3). Ridge regression, the family selected most often by the same validation when the four families were compared, is the data-driven comparator. In the comparisons with neural-network and MatEx models, the compared models were selected at each observation budget on chronological validation libraries (Methods). 

The prediction is accurate enough to allocate the remaining measurements 

Two response measurements were sufficient to predict the activity distribution of the complete held-out library (Fig. 2a). The physics-residual model gave an MAE of 0.203 mA cm −2 and a Spearman correlation of 0.992 across all 322 candidates. At a 20% advancement fraction, that is, when the 20% of candidates with the highest predicted activity are advanced, it recovered 58 of the 65 highest-activity candidates (precision 0.892), and its selected-set regret, the mean measured activity of the 65 most active candidates minus that of the 65 advanced candidates, was 0.017 mA cm −2. The data-driven model gave an MAE of 1.330 mA cm −2, 6.6 times 4higher, with Spearman correlation 0.991, precision 0.938 and regret 0.012 mA cm −2: both models order the complete library with Spearman correlations above 0.99, and the physics-residual model also predicts the activity magnitude (Table 3 lists every model on all four metrics). The Tafel term alone recovered 57 of 65 candidates, and its MAE of 0.541 mA cm −2 was 2.7 times that of the complete physics-residual model. The physical term and the learned term remove different error components 

Removing the learned residual separated activity ordering from the prediction of activity magnitude (Fig. 4). The Tafel term alone is the physics-residual model without its learned residual, so the comparison isolates that residual. In the two-measurement high-displacement subgroup, the Tafel term alone gave an MAE of 0.735 mA cm −2, Spearman correlation 0.983, precision 0.931 and regret 0.012 mA cm −2. Adding the learned residual reduced MAE to 0.191 mA cm −2 while selecting the same 29 candidates, with a Spearman correlation of 0.984. At three measurements the residual reduced MAE from 0.620 to 0.266 mA cm −2, with Spearman correlations of 0.969 and 0.964 and the same precision of 0.931. With five, nine and seventeen measurements the Tafel term alone reached MAEs of 0.261, 0.179 and 0.172 mA cm −2, so once a longer part of the response is measured the Tafel extrapolation by itself predicts the activity magnitude (Extended Data Table 1). The Tafel extrapolation of each candidate’s own measurements therefore orders the candidates above the training range, with Spearman correlations of 0.97 to 0.98, and the learned residual corrects the activity magnitude when the measured part of the response is short. A non-negative affine calibration of the Tafel term, fitted on development predictions, left the Tafel ordering unchanged but gave MAEs of 2.328 and 2.125 mA cm −2 at two and three measurements. A monotone isotonic calibration with non-negative linear tails, selected on the development libraries, gave 0.882 and 0.694 mA cm −2. The learned residual reduced the error below this calibration by 0.692 and 0.428 mA cm −2,with paired 95% intervals of 0.612–0.766 and 0.333–0.520 mA cm −2. The improvement therefore requires a candidate-dependent correction rather than a global rescaling of the Tafel response. A negative-slope control produced an error above 4 mA cm −2 at every budget from two to seventeen measurements, confirming that the physical response direction is required for an accurate activity magnitude. Learning the residual rather than the activity also made neural-network training more consistent. In a fixed network architecture with identical initial weights and training schedule, the residual target increased the mean cosine similarity between training and validation gradients by 0.110 (95% interval, 0.050–0.180) across six observation budgets, increased the correlation of mean-centred predictions across random initializations by 0.0062 (0.0047–0.0075) and reduced the across-seed coefficient of variation of validation MAE at every budget (Extended Data Fig. 6). Across the high-displacement subgroup, libraries 7–12 and all six budgets, the neural physics-residual network had the best least favourable value of all four metrics against a directly trained network selected from the same architecture search space, under both development selection rules; with the four-metric percentile score these were MAE 2.349 against 3.548 mA cm −2, Spearman correlation 0.761 against −0.372, precision 0.681 against 0.000 and regret 0.096 against 2.158 mA cm −2 (Supplementary Table 13). Identical-input networks and equal-size hyperparameter searches are compared in Supplementary Note 16 and Supplementary Table 24.

# Discussion 

Physics-residual learning makes activity prediction above the training range usable in combinatorial screening campaigns by using a short response from each new catalyst as the input to the Tafel extrapolation of an unmeasured activity endpoint. The partial polarization response gives each candidate its own measured current–potential slope, so the endpoint is predicted by extrapolating that response with the Tafel relation and adding a learned material correction. With two measurements this construction lowered the endpoint error from 1.330 to 0.203 mA cm −2 relative to the data-driven model selected by the same validation procedure, in a library in which 282 of 322 endpoint activities exceeded the training maximum. Unlike hybrid models whose physical term is fitted to training data [33, 35, 37], the physical term here passes through each candidate’s own measured current and contains no endpoint label, so its value is not bounded by the training activities. Because the learned residual accounts for the difference between this extrapolation and the measured activity rather than for the activity itself, few labelled catalysts suffice to fit it: for candidates more than two training interquartile ranges above the training maximum, Ridge regression fitted to all 1,685 labelled catalysts had 6.3 and 4.8 times the endpoint error of the construction fitted to 35 at two and three measurements. Besides battery cycle life and electrocatalyst lifetime, predicting a later value from an early part of the same measurement has been used successfully to stop unpromising network configurations early in hyperparameter search, by extrapolating their partial learning curves [40], and to forecast the degradation of an electrolyser from its initial measurements [29]; the present construction extends this principle to the prediction of catalyst activity above the training range (Supplementary Note 19). The construction therefore applies to OER datasets from other laboratories and measurement protocols. Two settings of the present analysis can be chosen for each campaign. The global slope prior uses the complete response windows of the fitting libraries, which are recorded because these catalysts are measured up to the endpoint; estimated from the sparse fitting measurements alone, it gives the same or higher Spearman correlation and precision and an endpoint error below that of the data-driven model at every budget (Supplementary Note 6). The candidate slope is fitted over the two highest observed potentials, which lie 0.009 V apart at seventeen measurements; the span of this window can be chosen for the noise level of a campaign, and a window spanning the complete 0.15 V kept the median endpoint-error advantage at seventeen measurements at 1.46 to 1.90 mA cm −2 for noise standard deviations up to 0.10 in log 10 current (Supplementary Table 5). In the independent datasets the least favourable values come from the smallest evaluation and from one measurement: in the 21-candidate FastCat evaluation a Ridge regression fitted directly to the overpotential has the best least favourable values, and in DASH Extra Trees has the lowest 12 least favourable MAE, 11.94 against 16.60 mV, both at one measurement (Table 4). The construction and its applicability-domain score can allocate measurements in prospective catalyst libraries, and the same extrapolation of a measured response with its physical relation can be applied to time-resolved transients and to stability and durability endpoints. Keeping the Tafel extrapolation as an explicit term of the model and learning material-specific corrections to it predicts activity above the training range from two measurements per candidate. 

# Methods 

Ni–Pd–Pt–Ru OER data preparation 

The OER source is Zenodo record 14891704 (CC BY 4.0) [43], associated with Ref. [3]. Thirty-eight comma-separated files deposited by the source authors containing energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) composition, activity and linear-sweep voltammetry (LSV) data were downloaded. All file sizes and MD5 checksums were verified against metadata returned by the Zenodo API. Composition, activity and LSV identifiers were joined within each library. The resulting table contains 4,026 candidates and a common 771-point potential grid spanning 1.030–1.800 V versus RHE. The activity reported in the source files is numerically identical to the LSV current density at 1.700 V. Seventeen observation potentials were placed as evenly as the source 1-mV grid allowed from 1.40 to 1.55 V. For budgets 2, 3, 5, 9 and 17, potential indices were uniformly spaced and included both window endpoints; the one-measurement design used the midpoint. Current density at 1.70 V was never included as an input. Elemental fractions of Ni, Pd, Pt and Ru and log 10 currents at the selected potentials form the data-driven input. Leave-one-library-out folds predicted each of libraries 1–5 from a fit to the other four and supplied the setting of every model in the primary comparison. Chronological folds, which fitted libraries 1–2 and predicted 3, fitted 1–3 and predicted 4, and fitted 1–4 and predicted 5, supplied the out-of-fold predictions used for calibration and the budget-specific selection in the neural-network and MatEx comparisons; for evaluation on libraries 7–12 a fourth fold, which fitted libraries 1–5 and predicted library 6, was added (Supplementary Note 17). The fit to libraries 1–5 was evaluated on library 6, and the fit to libraries 1–6, with the same settings, on libraries 7–12. Library 6 has the predefined role of activity-range extrapolation; libraries 7–12 are separately fabricated evaluation libraries within the training range, and neither enters the leave-one-library-out selection.

Leave-one-library-out model selection 

Every model of the primary Ni–Pd–Pt–Ru comparison received one setting, used at all observation budgets. Each of libraries 1–5 was predicted from a fit to the other four. Within each predicted library and each budget of 2, 3, 5, 9 and 17 measurements, the configurations of one model class were ranked by lower MAE, higher Spearman correlation, higher precision@ k and lower selected-set regret, each configuration was scored by the mean of the four percentile ranks, and the highest-scoring configuration was recorded. The 25 recorded values of each parameter were averaged and assigned to the nearest grid value; regularization strengths and learning rates were averaged on a logarithmic scale and hidden-layer sizes were assigned their most frequent value. The one-measurement budget is excluded from this average for every model; at that budget the Tafel term has no candidate-specific slope. The physics-residual model was selected in two stages. The Tafel term was selected first among the configurations without a learned residual, which gave a local-slope weight of 0.25 over the two highest observed potentials; used without a residual, it is the Tafel term alone. The residual scale, attenuation strength, Ridge regularization and feature-distance quantile of Eq. 4 were then selected among the configurations that share this Tafel term, which gave β = 0 .5, λ = 1, α = 10 and q = 0 .95. A learned residual was present in 24 of the 25 recorded configurations; λ and α were averaged over these 24 and q over those with λ > 0. The same procedure applied to each data-driven family gave Ridge regularization 0.1, Extra Trees with minimum leaf size 8 and feature fraction 0.8, histogram gradient boosting with L2 penalty 1 and minimum leaf size 20, an MLP with two hidden layers of 32 units and weight penalty 0.01, and CatBoost with 300 iterations, depth 6, learning rate 0.03 and L2 regularization 3. When the four primary data-driven families were scored together, Ridge regression was recorded in 15 of the 25 selections and is the data-driven comparator. No activity from library 6 or later libraries entered any of these choices. 

Reduction of the labelled fitting set 

Fitting sets of 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 40 and 100% of the 1,685 development candidates were drawn without reference to measured activity. Each of libraries 1–5 was permuted once per replicate and each smaller set consists of the first candidates of that permutation, so a smaller set is contained in every larger set within the same replicate and the proportion of candidates drawn from each library is the same at every size. Twenty replicates were drawn at each reduced fraction from base seed 20260909; the complete set was fitted once. Every model kept the setting selected on the complete development libraries, so that only the number of labelled catalysts differed between conditions. The global slope prior was estimated from all seventeen response-window measurements of the sampled fitting candidates, as in the primary analysis. No library-6 candidate was used for fitting or selection, and predictions are the mean over the five fixed model seeds. Endpoint accuracy is reported on the 144 candidates more than two training interquartile ranges above the training maximum; precision at a 20% advancement fraction is reported over all 322 library-6 candidates. Each of the four data-driven families is reported separately. 

Physics-residual parameter sweep 

The parameter-robustness sweep varied the local-slope weight over {0, 0.1, 0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 , 1}. For budgets greater than one, the local slope used the unique choices among the final two measurements, final three measurements and all available measurements; the one-measurement model used the global slope prior. Ridge residual regularization was α ∈ { 1, 10 , 100 }, residual scale was β ∈ { 0, 0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 , 1}, residual-attenuation strength was λ ∈ { 0, 0.25 , 1, 2, 5, 10 } and feature-distance quantile was q ∈ { 0.90 , 0.95 , 0.99 }. Algebraically identical cases were stored once: β = 0 defines one Tafel-term setting per combination of local-slope weight and candidate-slope potentials, and q is omitted when λ = 0. The resulting grid contains 14,089 configurations across the six observation budgets. Every configuration was scored on the leave-one-library-out folds by the four-metric percentile score described above, averaged over the five predicted libraries. At each budget, the configurations in the top fifth of this score were compared on library 6 and libraries 7–12 with the data-driven comparator at its selected 14 setting, and the fraction of configurations with favourable point estimates in all four metrics was recorded for every combination of residual scale and attenuation strength. Every sweep fit used seed 17. 

Slope-prior, comparator and calibration controls 

The slope-prior information control uses the identical fitted model settings and seeds but re-estimates the global slope from only the observation-budget potentials in every fitting curve. It is defined for budgets of at least two because a slope is not identifiable from one measurement. No library-6 target enters this re-estimation or any model choice. The CatBoost and unconstrained-response comparison fits libraries 1–5 with the selected settings and evaluates libraries 6–12 with the same fits. CatBoost is selected from 24 configurations over 300 or 500 iterations, depths 4, 6 or 8, learning rates 0.03 or 0.05 and L2 regularization 3 or 10 by the leave-one-library-out procedure; final predictions average seeds 17, 29, 43, 71 and 101. An unconstrained log-linear response uses the local-slope weight and candidate-slope potentials of the Tafel term but permits either slope direction and omits the learned residual. This comparison separates the extrapolation of the measured response from the restriction of its slope to non-negative values. For the calibration controls, predictions from the three chronological development folds were pooled only after each validation library had been predicted without entering its fit. A non-negative affine map from unconstrained-response predictions to measured log 10 activity was then fitted to these out-of-fold values. Every blended slope of the Tafel term in the evaluation data without added noise is positive at two and three measurements, so the unconstrained-response predictions equal those of the Tafel term at these budgets and the calibrations apply to the Tafel term. Positive slope makes the map rank preserving by construction. A nonlinear global control fits an increasing isotonic map inside the development response range and non-negative linear tails outside it. Tail fractions 0.10, 0.20, 0.30 and 0.50 were compared by leave-one-library-out development MAE; the selected fraction is fitted on all development predictions. The six contrasts compare response extrapolation with development-selected data-driven regression, non-negative-slope with unconstrained response, affine and nonlinear calibration separately with unconstrained response, and the physics-residual model separately with the affine and nonlinear global controls. Twenty thousand paired candidate bootstrap replicates were evaluated for every contrast at two and three measurements. 

Identical-input neural comparison and learning diagnostics 

The identical-input neural comparison used one scalar-response MLP design for direct and physics-residual learning. Both families received the four elemental fractions, the same sparse currents and the same candidate-specific Tafel extrapolation, and within an architecture they had the same trainable parameter count, initialization, optimizer, loss and training schedule. The direct network learned standardized target log 10 current. The physics-residual network learned the standardized difference between that target and the Tafel extrapolation, and the selected correction was multiplied by a residual scale and an optional feature-distance attenuation before addition to the extrapolation. No physics-penalty term or synthetic training data was introduced. Thirty-two architectures (width 16–128, depth one to four) and 114 residual-correction configurations were searched; final predictions averaged seeds 17, 29, 43, 71 and 101 and used the rounded median development-fold best epoch bounded to 40–500, and the complete search space is given in Supplementary Note 16. For the library-6 evaluation the settings of both families were selected at each observation budget on validation libraries 3–5 and libraries 1–5 supplied the final fit; for libraries 7–12, library 6 joined the development folds and the final fit. The equal-size hyperparameter-search analysis sampled the same 4, 8, 16 or 32 architectures for both families and repeated selection on development data 25 times, so both families had the same number of selectable configurations at every search size. Paired-bootstrap intervals use 3,000 resamples. Matched learning diagnostics used a fixed two-layer, width-32 ReLU architecture. Libraries 1–4 formed the training fold and library 5 the validation fold; the direct network, without a Tafel input, and the physics-residual network began from identical weights for five seeds and trained for 320 epochs. At twelve fixed checkpoints, with dropout disabled, the cosine similarity between training and validation gradients and between the gradients of two disjoint library-stratified training halves was computed, together with the correlation of mean-centred predictions between each seed and the ensemble of the other four and the across-seed coefficient of variation of validation MAE. Intervals use 10,000 bootstrap replicates of complete seed clusters over the six observation budgets (Supplementary Note 13). 15 Independent OER datasets 

The FastCat source is the official Technical University of Denmark (DTU) release, version 1, under the MIT licence [38]. Its 101 autonomous catalyst experiments contain elemental fractions and archived constant-current records. Steady overpotentials at 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 and 50 mA cm −2 were extracted by averaging the final 20 s after resistance correction; the published four-point activity summaries use 5, 10, 20 and 50 mA cm −2. For the FastCat evaluation, the chronological sequence assigns 48 experiments to fitting, 12 to configuration selection, 20 to a buffer of intermediate experiments that no model receives, and the latest 21 to evaluation; 19 of the 21 evaluation compositions do not occur in the fitting, configuration-selection or buffer sets. The final fit uses the first 60 records. Tafel predictions of η10 extrapolate each candidate’s own η–log 10 j response from its highest observed current density, using η5 alone, η2 and η5, or η1, η2 and η5; the candidate slope is fitted over all observed current densities, and the global slope prior is the median candidate slope of the fitting responses at 1, 2 and 5 mA cm −2, so no endpoint label enters the Tafel term. The weighted slope is constrained to be non-negative. The local-slope weight, Ridge residual regularization, residual scale and feature-distance attenuation are selected at each observation budget on the fitting and configuration-selection subsets. Every model receives the elemental fractions, eight composition descriptors (mixing entropy, number of elements, and mean and standard deviation of atomic number, electronegativity and d-electron count) and the sparse overpotentials, and the Ridge, Extra Trees, histogram gradient boosting and MLP models use the same development-data selector. Every model receives the elemental fractions, composition descriptors, the synthesis and measurement conditions recorded in the source table and the sparse overpotentials. Batch 1 fits every configuration of the grid, batch 2 selects one configuration by the four-metric percentile score, and each of batches 3–5 is evaluated after refitting the selected configuration on every strictly earlier batch. Rationale for baseline selection 

Ridge regression tests linear extrapolation from composition and sparse response. Extra Trees tests a strong nonlinear ensemble whose leaf predictions are bounded by fitted targets [47]. Histogram gradient boosting supplies a distinct nonlinear tree learner, and the MLP tests a flexible continuous data-driven response. These four families form the primary data-driven comparison and include linear, tree-ensemble and neural-network regressors. CatBoost supplies a separate modern regularized boosting benchmark [48]. The primary grids span regularization strength, leaf size, feature fraction and network width; CatBoost independently varies depth, learning rate and L2 regularization. Each family is selected independently by the leave-one-library-out procedure, and the data-driven comparator is the family recorded most often when the four primary families are scored together. The selection score weights endpoint error, ordering, top-set recovery and regret equally, so the comparator is not chosen for any one of the outcomes on which the models are compared. Results for the four primary families are given in Table 3, Extended Data Table 1, the Source Data and Supplementary Fig. 1; the separate CatBoost benchmark is reported in Supplementary Note 8 and Supplementary Fig. 2. Detailed procedures for the identical-input neural comparison and for the MatEx Bilinear Transduction comparisons [22, 23] are provided in Supplementary Notes 15, 16 and 17. In these analyses every compared model, the physics-residual model included, is selected at each observation budget on chronological validation libraries with matched inputs; the equal-size searches and the control with two fixed configurations per family also match the number of configurations.

Robustness protocols and computation 

Response-measurement-placement robustness uses the selected OER model settings with one fixed model seed. For each of 2, 3, 5 and 9 measurements, uniformly spaced, contiguous low-potential, contiguous high-potential and twelve fixed random designs are evaluated. The random seed is 260826. Noise robustness uses the uniform 2-, 3- and 17-measurement designs, adds Gaussian noise to test log 10 currents only and repeats every 17 non-zero noise level twelve times with seed 260827. A slope-window analysis applies this protocol at two, nine and seventeen measurements with the candidate slope fitted over the two highest, the three highest and all observed potentials, one perturbation draw per replicate being shared by the three windows. The fitting data, model families and hyperparameters remain fixed. Primary stochastic Ni–Pd–Pt–Ru final models use seeds 17, 29, 43, 71 and 101, and candidate predictions are averaged across seeds. Ridge and Tafel-term settings contain no stochastic parameters, so their predictions are identical across these five seeds. No test target is used in estimator, hyperparameter or seed selection. All reported fits ran on CPU. Python, NumPy, pandas, SciPy, scikit-learn and PyTorch versions are fixed in the archived requirements file, and random seeds and input and output hashes are recorded in the result manifests.

# Supplementary Note 1: Datasets, experimental roles and definitions 

The primary material-library inventory contains 4,026 experimentally measured candidate records in twelve independently fabricated Ni–Pd–Pt–Ru materials libraries (Table 2), and FastCat and DASH add 351 experimental catalyst records, so the study uses 4,377 measured candidate experiments. The Ni–Pd–Pt–Ru oxygen-evolution reaction (OER) libraries provide a complete response grid for the primary analysis, in which the unmeasured endpoint activity is predicted from a partially measured response. In this work, the number of response measurements , also called the observation budget, is the number of current measurements available in the fixed 1.40–1.55-V window before the endpoint is predicted; one to three measurements define sparse observation. Activity-range displacement is the distance of an evaluation target on the log 10 scale beyond the most favourable boundary of the training range, normalized by the training-target interquartile range (IQR): above the maximum for higher-is-better targets and below the minimum for lower-is-better targets. Physics-residual model denotes a predictor whose physical term, a Tafel extrapolation, passes through the candidate’s own measured current and uses the candidate’s own slope, and to which a learned residual, attenuated with feature-space distance from the fitted candidates, is added. It is a form of physics-informed machine learning in which the physical relation is one of the two terms summed to give the output and is evaluated with each candidate’s own measurements. For every OER condition, k = ⌈0.20 n⌉. For OER current-density targets, higher values are favourable: precision@ k is the fraction of the k candidates with largest measured values recovered among the k largest predictions, and selected-set regret is the oracle-set mean minus the predicted-set mean. Exact boundary ties use fractional selection weights. For the FastCat and DASH overpotential targets, lower values are favourable: the oracle and predicted sets contain the k lowest values, regret is the predicted-set mean minus the oracle-set mean, and exact boundary ties are broken by a fixed candidate order. These quantities complement MAE and Spearman correlation by distinguishing endpoint accuracy, global order, top-set recovery and the difference between the mean activity of the best candidates and that of the advanced candidates.

Notes 5, 6, 9, 11 and 12 report every model under every evaluation condition, robustness to measurement placement and observation noise, composition-region resampling, the physics-residual settings and the dependence on the number of measurements. Electrode potentials are reported versus the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). 

# Supplementary Note 2: OER data integrity and activity-range dis-placement 

The OER source contains energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) composition, activity and complete linear-sweep voltammogram (LSV) files for twelve materials libraries. Thirty-eight files deposited by the source 29 authors were downloaded from Zenodo record 14891704. Every file matched the MD5 value and byte size in the Zenodo record. The deposited activity tables contain 4,026 rows, compared with 4,030 reported in the source article’s final-dataset description; the analysis uses the deposited candidate records. The joined table contains 4,026 unique identifiers and a common 771-point grid from 1.030 to 1.800 V versus RHE. Elemental fractions sum to 100%, every retained observation and target current is positive, and the activity value in the source tables equals the corresponding LSV current at 1.700 V for every candidate. The observation window is 1.40–1.55 V. Uniform designs include both endpoints for budgets greater than one; the one-measurement design uses the midpoint. Candidate current at 1.70 V is never supplied to a model, and in every analysis evaluation activities enter only after all predictions of an evaluation condition have been recorded. Supplementary Table 1 lists the fixed potentials on the 17-measurement reference grid. The number of response measurements changes how many currents are recorded within the same 1.40–1.55-V window. Budgets of two or more retain a 0.15-V gap from the highest observed potential, whereas the midpoint-only one-measurement design requires a 0.225-V extrapolation.

# Supplementary Note 3: Model development and baseline models 

Every Ni–Pd–Pt–Ru model setting was chosen by leave-one-library-out validation within libraries 1–5 and is used at every observation budget (Methods). The data-driven families were Ridge regression, Extra Trees, histogram gradient boosting and a multilayer perceptron (MLP). They represent linear extrapolation, randomized nonlinear partitions, sequential nonlinear partitions and a flexible continuous neural response, respectively. The Tafel term alone extrapolates a positive-slope Tafel-shaped relation. The physics-residual model adds a learned residual to that relation and attenuates the residual as standardized feature distance exceeds a fitting quantile. Because one setting is used at every budget, endpoint error and candidate ranking at different budgets are obtained from the same model. The Tafel term was selected first among configurations without a learned residual, and the residual among configurations sharing this Tafel term. The 25 values recorded for each parameter over five predicted libraries and five budgets were averaged into one setting used at every budget (Methods); every recorded value is given in the accompanying result files. A single response point admits no candidate-specific slope, so the local-point count is capped at the budget and the local-slope weight then has no effect. A negative-slope response with the same slope weight supplies a deliberately misspecified control. Supplementary Table 2 lists the selected setting of every model. All stochastic Ni–Pd–Pt–Ru final predictions average seeds 17, 29, 43, 71 and 101, and the Source Data provide every leave-one-library-out score and every candidate-level evaluation prediction. Library 6 provides the predefined held-out activity-extrapolation evaluation. Libraries 7–12 provide fixed evaluations on separate experimental libraries and do not participate in model selection.

# Supplementary Note 7: Statistical design and reproducibility 

An intersection–union test requires every one of the four metrics to change in the favourable direction simultane-ously, and reports the largest of the four metric-specific probabilities. A nonparametric-combination test instead pools the four metric-specific probabilities into one aggregate value and reports directional evidence across them. The candidate-level paired bootstrap resamples the target and all compared predictions with shared index draws: 5,000 in the primary, neural-network and MatEx comparisons, 3,000 in the identical-input and equal-size searches and 20,000 for the calibration contrasts. Each metric is recomputed within a resample. Bootstrap percentile intervals at the 95% level are reported, and one-sided bootstrap P values test the favourable direction. Holm correction is applied across the six observation budgets separately for each metric in the high-displacement subgroup. Ni–Pd–Pt–Ru OER bootstrap MAE uses current density in mA cm −2; FastCat and DASH use overpotential in volts, reported in mV. Library roles are fixed before fitting: the fit to libraries 1–5 is evaluated on library 6 and the fit to libraries 1–6 on libraries 7–12. All analyses run on a CPU; package versions are fixed in the archived requirements file, and seeds and file hashes are recorded in the archived manifests.

51 Supplementary Note 17: Chronological within-range comparison with MatEx 

This experiment compares chronologically selected physics-residual learning on candidate prioritization with the MatEx implementation of Bilinear Transduction [22, 23], described in Supplementary Note 15, after the target-activity range is represented in the fitting libraries. Chronological validation fits libraries 1–2, 1–3, 1–4 and 1–5 and validates on libraries 3, 4, 5 and 6. Final models fit libraries 1–6 and evaluate libraries 7–12. Tafel-augmented MatEx (MatEx+Tafel) is the primary comparator: it receives the same composition fractions, sparse current-density response measurements and Tafel extrapolation as the physics-residual model, the extrapolation being computed from each candidate’s own measured response with the global slope prior. The parameter search combines 32 fixed or Sobol-sampled network architectures with 114 residual-correction configurations at each observation budget. The selection rule, applied only to the chronological development libraries, chooses the configuration whose smallest improvement over MatEx+Tafel among the four metrics (MAE reduction, Spearman gain, precision@ k gain and regret reduction) is largest; the two highest-ranked settings are then refitted with three seeds. At each observation budget this comparison of 3,648 physics-residual configurations with the two MatEx+Tafel configurations measures the performance obtained by selection on the chronological development libraries. A separate control compares two fixed configurations per family, two residual networks and two MatEx networks, with the same configuration count, a batch size of 256, 8,000 optimizer updates, 2,048,000 supervised prediction targets processed and the same seeds (17, 29 and 43). A control with physics-residual networks of width 320 and 448, whose 209,921–415,745 trainable parameters bracket the 283,200–299,616 of MatEx+Tafel, compares the families at comparable parameter count. Exact parameter counts and training records per update are reported in Supplementary Table 26. After equal averaging over the six observation budgets within each material library and then over the six libraries, the chronologically selected physics-residual models give benefits of −0.067 and +0 .023 mA cm −2

for MAE and regret, +0 .059 for Spearman correlation and +0 .073 for precision@ k. The Spearman, precision and regret benefits are positive in every material library, each with a one-sided exact material-library sign-flip 

P = 1 /64, and the four-metric nonparametric-combination test, in which each metric’s library effects are divided by their root-mean-square value, gives PNPC = 1 /64. With each model selected by its own four-metric percentile score, the benefits are −0.065, +0 .068, +0 .087 and +0 .027 for MAE, Spearman correlation, precision@ k and regret, with the same sign-flip and nonparametric-combination P values of 1/64. Ranking and selection are evaluated within each library, the unit in which candidates are advanced, and every library receives equal weight. The control with two fixed configurations per family is reported as a global effect and at five response measurements in Supplementary Table 25, and its selected configurations in Supplementary Table 26. 54 Supplementary Note 18: Performance as the fitting set is reduced 

Fitting sets holding 35, 50, 85, 169, 337, 674 and 1,685 of the development candidates were drawn without using measured endpoint activity. Each of libraries 1–5 was permuted once per replicate and each smaller set consists of the first candidates of that permutation, so a smaller set is contained in every larger set within the same replicate. Twenty replicates were drawn at each of the six reduced sizes; the complete set was fitted once and reproduces the primary analysis. Every model kept the setting selected by leave-one-library-out validation on the complete development libraries. No library-6 candidate was used for fitting or selection. Values below are medians over the 20 replicates at the six reduced sizes. Endpoint accuracy is reported on the 144 high-displacement candidates of the primary analysis, defined relative to the complete libraries 1–5; precision at k = 65 is reported over all 322 library-6 candidates (Extended Data Fig. 2). Over the 48-fold reduction from 1,685 to 35 labelled catalysts the physics-residual endpoint error over the six reduced sizes was between 0.218 and 0.401 mA cm −2 with two response measurements and between 0.274 and 0.380 mA cm −2 with three, against 0.191 and 0.266 mA cm −2 at the complete fitting set. The Spearman correlation changed by at most 0.003, being 0.983–0.984 at two measurements and 0.964–0.967 at three, and precision was 0.892–0.908 and 0.954, respectively. The Tafel term alone gave endpoint errors between 0.715 and 0.748 mA cm −2 at two response measurements and between 0.599 and 0.632 mA cm −2 at three, and its Spearman correlation and precision were identical at every size within each response budget, because the global slope prior adds the same term to the logarithm of every candidate’s predicted current. The component that determines the candidate ranking therefore uses no measured endpoint activity. The four data-driven families gave endpoint errors between 1.69 and 4.01 mA cm −2 across the reduction. Increasing the fitting set 48-fold lowered their endpoint error by at most 1.08 mA cm −2, reached by the multilayer perceptron at three response measurements, whose Spearman correlation over the same increase rose from 0.578 to 0.946. 56 Supplementary Note 19: Comparison with published work in three groups 

Supplementary Tables 27–29 compare the present study with published work in three groups: catalyst- and materials-discovery workflows and accelerated tests, studies that predict an unmeasured endpoint from part of a measurement or outside the training distribution, and models that combine a physical term with a learned component. The studies were identified by searches of Crossref, arXiv, Semantic Scholar and publisher websites through 18 September 2026 for catalyst-discovery workflows, prediction from partial measurements, prediction outside the training distribution and models that combine a physical term with a learned component, and each table entry was taken from the text of the publication. In the workflows of Supplementary Table 27 that reduce the number of candidates alone, the target property of each selected candidate is measured in full. Savings within each candidate have been obtained for the capacity of battery cathodes at later cycles and for the stability and lifetime of electrocatalysts, by predicting a later quantity from an early part of each test or from faster measurements. The present study predicts the activity of each catalyst candidate at a potential above its measured window and uses these predictions to choose the candidates that are measured up to the endpoint, so both the measurement within each candidate and the number of candidates measured up to the endpoint are reduced. In Supplementary Table 28, the studies that predict a later endpoint from an early part of a measurement concern battery cells and battery cathodes, and the study that also tests lifetimes above the training range partitions one test campaign. Benchmarks of prediction outside the training distribution partition existing datasets by their highest property values, by structural clusters or by low-density regions of the structure or property distribution. The present study combines a partial measurement of each candidate, a predicted endpoint outside the measured window, activities above the training maximum that arose in a separately fabricated library, and top-set selection metrics. In Supplementary Table 29, the physical term is computed from measurements of the item being predicted in the degradation forecast of one electrolyser, in learning-curve extrapolation for hyperparameter search and in the inference of kinetic parameters from the measured current–potential response of an electrode. In the other models it is fitted to training data, predicted by a network or Gaussian process trained on other samples, or calculated for the new molecule at a lower level of theory.
