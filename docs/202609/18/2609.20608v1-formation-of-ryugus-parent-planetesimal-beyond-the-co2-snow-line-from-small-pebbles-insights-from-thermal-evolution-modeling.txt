Abstract 

Astronomical observations of planet-forming circumstellar disks indicate that planetesimals form from 0.1-mm- to 1-cm-sized dust aggregates, commonly called “pebbles.” When such pebbles accrete beyond the water snow line, the resulting icy planetesimals undergo water–rock di ff erentiation and develop porous pebble-pile cores whose voids are saturated with liquid water. Circulation of this water enhances heat transport in the core, suppressing the temperature rise caused by the decay of radionuclides. In this study, we constrain the accretion age and constituent pebble size of Ryugu’s parent planetesimal by modeling the thermal evolution of icy planetesimals and comparing the results with the precipitation ages and temperatures of aqueously formed minerals identified in samples returned from asteroid Ryugu. Our numerical results suggest that Ryugu’s parent planetesimal accreted within 2.0 Myr of the formation of calcium–aluminum-rich inclusions. The inferred early accretion age supports the hypothesis that Ryugu’s parent planetesimal formed earlier than most chondrule-bearing carbonaceous chondrite parent planetesimals, potentially explaining the absence of chondrules in Ryugu samples. We also found that the core of the parent planetesimal was composed of pebbles no larger than a few millimeters. Such small pebble sizes are consistent with theoretical predictions for planetesimals formed beyond the CO 2 snow line.

1. Introduction 

Hydrous carbonaceous (C-type) asteroids are thought to be the primary source of water and volatiles for the Earth and terrestrial planets (e.g., Genda, 2016; Krijt et al., 2023). The JAXA Hayabusa2 spacecraft explored the near-Earth C-type as-teroid (162173) Ryugu and delivered the collected samples to Earth. These samples are dominated by hydrous phyllosilicates and contain coarse-grained minerals formed through aqueous alteration (e.g., Nakamura, E. et al., 2022; Nakamura, T. et al., 2023; Yamaguchi et al., 2023). The mineralogical, petrological, and chemical characteristics of the Ryugu samples closely re-semble those of CI (Ivuna-type) carbonaceous chondrites (e.g., Ito et al., 2022; Nakamura, T. et al., 2023; Yokoyama et al., 2023a) as well as samples collected from another C-type as-teroid, (101955) Bennu (e.g., Lauretta et al., 2024). Ryugu is a 1-km-sized rubble-pile asteroid thought to have originated from the collisional disruption of a parent planetesimal with a radius greater than 10 km (e.g., Sugita et al., 2019; Watanabe et al., 2019; Okada et al., 2020). The timing and duration of fluid ac-tivity within the parent body have been constrained by 53 Mn–

Fur-thermore, the internal temperature of the parent planetesimal has been estimated from equilibrium oxygen isotope fractiona-tion between dolomite and magnetite (Yokoyama et al., 2023a; Kawasaki et al., 2026). The parent planetesimal have formed through the accumu-lation of dust aggregates in the solar protoplanetary disk (e.g., Morbidelli et al., 2024). Astronomical observations of extra-solar protoplanetary disks have revealed that 0.1-mm- to 1-cm-sized dust aggregates are abundant in the midplanes of disks (e.g., Miotello et al., 2023). These aggregates, commonly re-ferred to as “pebbles,” are therefore considered the building blocks of planetesimals. Various mechanisms have been pro-posed for the accumulation of pebbles (e.g., Lesur et al., 2023), and the size and composition of the resulting planetesimals de-pend on the specific processes by which pebbles are accumu-lated (e.g., Johansen et al., 2014). The size of pebbles evolves through sequential collisions within the disk (e.g., Arakawa et al., 2023), and thus varies with both time and location in the disk (e.g., Dr ˛ a˙ zkowska et al., 2023). For a planetesimal composed of rock and ice, water–rock dif-ferentiation occurs when the internal temperature reaches the arXiv:2609.20608v1 [astro-ph.EP] 17 Sep 2026 melting point of ice. If pebbles survive the di ff erentiation pro-cess without being destroyed, the resulting rocky core would not be consolidated but instead form a pebble-pile structure, with water filling the pores between the aggregates (Figure 1). Water circulation enhances heat transfer within such a porous core (e.g., Grimm and McSween, 1989; Young et al., 2003; Tang et al., 2023), and the thermal history of the planetesimal di ff ers significantly depending on whether or not water circula-tion occurs. Moreover, the e ffi ciency of heat transfer depends on the permeability of the porous pebble-pile core, which in turn is a function of the pebble radius (e.g., Bland et al., 2009; Dullien, 2012). Therefore, it is of great interest whether the thermal history of Ryugu’s parent planetesimal can constrain the size of pebbles present during the first few million years of the solar protoplanetary disk. In this study, we investigate the thermal history of Ryugu’s parent planetesimal based on the precipitation ages and temper-atures of dolomite in the returned samples. The precipitation conditions of dolomite found in the Ryugu samples are sum-marized in Section 2. An evolutionary model for icy planetes-imals is introduced in Section 3. We perform one-dimensional numerical simulations to investigate the temperature and struc-tural evolution of icy planetesimals (Section 4), and compare the results with sample analysis data reported by Kawasaki et al. (2026). From these comparisons, we derive constraints on the pebble radius ( rpeb ), the planetesimal radius ( Rp), and the ac-cretion age ( tacc ) of Ryugu’s parent body. We also present an analytical solution for the temperature evolution at the center of planetesimal (Section 4.3). This study o ff ers new insights into the formation and thermal evolution of icy planetesimals in the early solar system. 

2. Precipitation Ages and Temperatures of Dolomite in Ryugu Samples 

The Ryugu samples contain abundant and large aqueously formed carbonates, including dolomite, which can be dated to determine the timing of fluid activity. In this study, we use the data for the A0058 and C0002 samples reported in Kawasaki et al. (2026) and compare them with our numerical results. The A0058 and C0002 samples were collected from the first and second touchdown sites, respectively. The 53 Mn– 53 Cr ages of dolomite in the Ryugu samples have been determined either by in situ secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) analyses (Nakamura, E. et al., 2022; McCain et al., 2023; Yokoyama et al., 2023a) or by whole-rock sample analyses (Yokoyama et al., 2023b; Tanaka et al., 2024). Since the precipitation age can vary among individual grains, in situ SIMS analyses are suitable for determining the 53 Mn– 53 Cr ages of single dolomite grains. However, Sugawara et al. (2024) claimed that the SIMS-based Mn–Cr ages of Ryugu dolomite reported in earlier stud-ies were a ff ected by artificial errors caused by the use of inap-propriate standards for data correction. To address this issue, Sugawara et al. (2024) synthesized homogeneous crystalline dolomite to be used as standard materials, and evaluated the rel-ative sensitivity factor (RSF) of Mn /Cr for SIMS analysis, that is, the ratio of Mn /Cr measured by SIMS to the true Mn /Cr ra-tio. Using this improved standard, Kawasaki et al. (2026) deter-mined the Mn–Cr ages of dolomite in the Ryugu samples. They analyzed dolomite grains from two Ryugu samples, C0002 and A0058, and found that their precipitation ages are 2 .0+0.6 

> −0.6

Myr and 4 .1+0.9 

> −0.8

Myr after the formation of calcium–aluminum-rich inclusions (CAIs), respectively. The precipitation temperature of dolomite is estimated based on the equilibrium oxygen isotope fractionation between dolomite and magnetite. Dolomite and magnetite grains are occasionally found in close proximity within the same litho-logical context in Ryugu samples, and their oxygen isotopic compositions suggest that they precipitated from the same aqueous fluid under oxygen isotopic equilibrium conditions (Yokoyama et al., 2023a; Kawasaki et al., 2026). Therefore, the temperature-dependent nature of equilibrium oxygen iso-tope fractionation can be used to infer their precipitation tem-peratures. Using this approach, the precipitation temperatures of dolomite in the C0002 and A0058 samples were estimated to be (365 ± 21) K and (310 ± 10) K, respectively (Yokoyama et al., 2023a; Kawasaki et al., 2026). In addition to constraints on its precipitation age and tem-perature, dolomite is thought to have formed during the retro-grade cooling stage of the parent planetesimal. Fujiya et al. (2023) conducted carbon and oxygen isotope analyses of cal-cite and dolomite in Ryugu samples. They found that varia-tions in both carbon and oxygen isotope ratios in dolomite are smaller than those in calcite, suggesting that dolomite precip-itated when the aqueous fluid and silicates were approaching oxygen isotope equilibrium, likely during retrograde cooling of the parent body. Kita et al. (2024) also performed oxygen iso-tope analyses of carbonates in Ryugu samples and found that dolomite and breunnerite, along with some magnetite, precipi-tated during the late stage of aqueous alteration, when oxygen isotopic equilibrium had nearly been achieved between the fluid and solid phases. This finding further supports the conclusion of Fujiya et al. (2023).

3.1. Structure evolution 

Figure 1 illustrates the schematic evolution of the internal structure of the parent planetesimals. Initially, planetesimals are undi ff erentiated, and their interiors consist of a mixture of anhydrous silicates and H 2O ice (Panel (a)). The internal temperature gradually increases mainly due to the decay heat of short-lived radioactive nuclide 26 Al. Once the temperature reaches the melting point of ice, Tmelt = 273 K, structural evo-lution begins through water–rock di ff erentiation (Panel (b)). We also assume that hydrothermal reactions forming phyllosilicates occur at T = Tmelt . Because the sedimentation timescale for 1-mm-sized pebbles is ≲ 1 yr (Wakita and Sekiya, 2011), dif-ferentiation is considered to occur instantaneously. We further assume that the resulting pebble-pile core is composed of phyl-losilicates. As the radiogenic heating diminishes over time, the internal temperature begins to decline. This leads to the top-down freezing of the water mantle (Panel (c)), followed by the progressive freezing of water within the pebble-pile core (Pan-els (d) and (e)). The time evolution of each layer’s depth is cal-culated by accounting for the heat consumption and release as-sociated with ice melting /freezing and aqueous alteration (i.e., phyllosilicate formation). Further details of our numerical mod-eling are provided in Supplementary Materials (Texts S1–S4 and Figure S1). We assume that the density is homogeneous within each layer. The bulk density of the undi ff erentiated layer is given by ¯ρ = χsil ρsil + (1 − χsil )ρice , (3) where ρsil = 3100 kg m −3 and ρice = 1000 kg m −3 are the material densities of anhydrous silicate and ice, respectively. The volume fraction of anhydrous silicate in the undi ff erenti-ated layer, χsil , is given by χsil = fsil ¯ρ/ρ sil , where fsil is the mass fraction of anhydrous silicate in the undi ff erentiated layer. In this study, we adopt fsil = 0.5 as the fiducial value, which corresponds to χsil = 0.244 and ¯ ρ = 1512 kg m −3. The bulk density of the water /ice mantle is equal to its material density. The material density of water is set to ρwtr = 1000 kg m −3.We assume that all pores within the undi ff erentiated layer are completely filled with H 2O ice. The pebble density is set to ρpeb = 1800 kg m −3,which corresponds to the average density of Ryugu samples (e.g., Nakamura, T. et al., 2023). We adopt ϕstr = 80% as the fiducial value, which corresponds to the random packing of polydisperse spheres (e.g., Grott et al., 2020). Accordingly, the volume fraction of phyllosilicates within the core is χphy = ϕpeb ϕstr = 0.554, and the bulk den-sity of the pebble-pile core is given by 

ρcore = χphy ρphy + (1 − χphy )ρwtr , (4) which yields ρcore = 1886 kg m −3 for ϕstr = 80%. The assumption of a pebble-pile structure within the core would be valid if the pebbles can withstand disruption caused by gravitational compression. Pebbles with a packing fraction of ϕpeb ≥ 60%, composed of submicron-sized grains, exhibit yield strengths exceeding 10 MPa (e.g., Malamud et al., 2024), suggesting that the pebbles in the core would remain intact (or at least survive) gravitational compression within planetesimals with radii Rp ≲ 200 km (see Section 3.2 and Equation (5)).

4. Results 

Here, we present several numerical results that either suc-ceed or fail in explaining the precipitation ages and tempera-tures of dolomites in two Ryugu samples, namely, the C0002 and A0058 grains. We treat rpeb , Rp, and tacc as free param-eters and find that the observed precipitation ages and tem-peratures can be reproduced across various combinations of (rpeb , Rp, tacc ). Note that fice = 0.5 and ϕstr = 80% are fixed throughout Section 4. The dependence on these parameters is discussed in Supplementary Materials (Text S6, and Figures S2 and S3). 

4.1. Fiducial case 

We present our fiducial model, which reproduces the pre-cipitation ages and temperatures of dolomite observed in two Ryugu samples (C0002 and A0058 grains; see Section 2) within a single parent planetesimal. Figure 2 shows the thermal evolu-tion of the parent planetesimal with the fiducial parameter set: 

rpeb = 0.7 mm, Rp = 60 km, and tacc = 1.0 Myr. Di ff erentia-tion begins at 0 .18 Myr after accretion, and a pebble-pile core with a radius of Rc = 49 .1 km has formed (Figure 2(a)). The temperature at the center of the core reaches Tcenter = 425 .4 K at t = 1.49 Myr (i.e., 0 .49 Myr after accretion; Figure 2(b)). The saturation pressure of H 2O at 425 .4 K is lower than the hy-drostatic pressure at the center for Rp = 60 km, allowing water to remain in the liquid phase within the core. 4.4. Thermal evolution for the case without water circulation 

A key physical process considered in the present study is the enhancement of heat transfer within the core due to e ffi cient wa-ter circulation. If water circulation is neglected, the peak tem-perature at the center exceeds the threshold required to maintain liquid water. Figure 7 shows the thermal evolution of planetes-imal with Rp = 20 km and tacc = 1.0 Myr. In this case, we set Nu = 1 within the pebble-pile core, instead of using the value of Nu derived from Equation (7). Taking water circulation into account and assuming rpeb = 1 mm, the temporal evolution of 

Tcenter satisfies the condition for the presence of liquid water (Figure 4(d)). In contrast, without water circulation, Tcenter ex-ceeds the threshold, and liquid water can no longer exist at the core center. Given that the accretion age of the parent plan-etesimal of the C0002 grain is tacc < 2.0 Myr and its radius is Rp ≫ 10 km, water circulation within the pebble-pile core likely played a critical role in suppressing the temperature rise and sustaining liquid water. 

5. Discussion 

5.1. Accretion age and pebble radius of the Ryugu’s parent planetesimal 

We summarize the model-derived constraints on the accre-tion age and pebble radius of Ryugu’s parent planetesimal. Fig-ure 8 presents the results for various combinations of tacc and 

rpeb , assuming a fixed planetesimal radius of Rp = 60 km. The shaded region indicates the range of tacc and rpeb values con-sistent with the thermal evolution of Ryugu’s parent planetes-imal. To reproduce the precipitation ages and temperatures of both the C0002 and A0058 grains, the parent planetesimal must have accreted earlier than 2 .0 Myr after CAI formation, and the radius of the constituent pebbles should have been smaller than 2 mm. In addition to the constraints on the precipitation ages and temperatures, dolomite in Ryugu samples is thought to have formed during the retrograde cooling stage of the parent plan-etesimal (Fujiya et al., 2023; Kita et al., 2024). To satisfy this additional constraint, the accretion age of the parent planetesi-mal would need to be earlier than 1 .5 Myr after CAI formation. These constraints on the accretion age are consistent with those reported by Kawasaki et al. (2026), regardless of di ff er-ences in the thermal evolution modeling. Unlike CI chondrites and Ryugu, most carbonaceous chondrites contain chondrules. The forma-tion ages of chondrules in the majority of carbonaceous chon-drites, including CO (Ornans-type), CM (Mighei-type), and CV groups, range from 2 .2 to 2 .8 Myr after CAI formation (e.g., Nagashima et al., 2017; Fukuda et al., 2022), while those in CR (Renazzo-type) chondrites formed at ≳ 3.5 Myr (e.g., Schrader et al., 2017; Tenner et al., 2019). Since chondrules must have formed before the accretion of their parent bodies, the accre-tion ages of these chondrite parent planetesimals must postdate 2.8 Myr after CAI formation. In contrast, Ryugu’s parent plan-etesimal accreted within 2 .0 Myr, and likely before 1 .5 Myr after CAI formation. This substantial age di ff erence may ex-plain the near-absence of chondrules in Ryugu samples (e.g., Kawasaki et al., 2022; Nakamura, T. et al., 2023; Nakashima et al., 2023; Yamaguchi et al., 2023; Kawasaki et al., 2025). We acknowledge that the range of rpeb values consistent with the thermal evolution is sensitive to the parameters assumed in our calculations. For example, in our fiducial model, we fixed the volume packing fraction of pebbles within the pebble-pile structure at ϕstr = 80%; however, the suitable range of rpeb shifts to smaller values when a lower ϕstr is adopted (see Supplemen-tary Section S6). Taking into account the uncertainty in ϕstr , we provide a conservative constraint that the pebble radius within the parent body was likely smaller than 5 mm. 

5.2. Size and birthplace of Ryugu’s parent planetesimal 

Ryugu’s parent planetesimal is thought to have accreted in an orbit di ff erent from Ryugu’s current near-Earth orbit, and its original size should have been larger than the current size of Ryugu. Dynamical and spectral properties of Ryugu indicate that it likely originated from the Eulalia or New Polana asteroid families in the inner main asteroid belt (e.g., Walsh et al., 2013; Tatsumi et al., 2021). The radii of the largest asteroids in the Eulalia or New Polana families are approximately 20 km and 30 km, respectively, suggesting that the radius of the original parent planetesimal was at least larger than 20 km (e.g., Naka-mura, T. et al., 2023). This size constraint is consistent with the necessary condition for the presence of liquid water within the core at T ≈ 365 K (Figure 1(g)). The birthplace of the parent planetesimal is thought to be be-yond the H 2O and CO 2 snow lines in the solar protoplanetary disk, possibly beyond the orbit of Jupiter. Nakamura, T. et al. (2023) performed synchrotron nano-computed tomography on a large pyrrhotite crystal in the Ryugu C0002 sample and dis-covered fluid inclusions. These inclusions contain CO 2-bearing water with sulfur species and nitrogen- and chlorine-bearing or-ganic compounds, indicating that Ryugu’s parent planetesimal accreted beyond the H 2O and CO 2 snow lines, similar to the case of CM chondrite (Tsuchiyama et al., 2021). In addition, the relative abundances of CAI-derived and chondrule-derived anhydrous primary minerals in Ryugu and CI chondrites dif-fer from those in other carbonaceous chondrites, but closely re-semble those observed in cometary dust grains, indicating that their parent planetesimal(s) likely accreted in the outer solar system far beyond the Jupiter’s orbit (Kawasaki et al., 2022). This hypothesis is also supported by Fe nucleosynthetic iso-topic anomalies observed in Ryugu and CI chondrites, which are distinguishable from those of other carbonaceous chondrites (Hopp et al., 2022). If the parent planetesimal formed be-yond the CO 2 snow line, dust grains would have been coated with CO 2 ice, which is less sticky than H 2O ice (e.g., Arakawa and Krijt, 2021; Fritscher and Teiser, 2021). 5.3. Mud convection as an alternative scenario 

In our numerical model, the peak temperature readily ex-ceeds the threshold for retaining liquid water when a small value of rpeb is chosen. The internal structure and temperature evolution for the parameter set rpeb = 0.2 mm, Rp = 60 km and tacc = 1.0 Myr are shown in Figure S4 in Supplementary Materials, where the peak temperature reaches approximately 700 K. However, this does not imply that the building blocks of the parent planetesimal must have had rpeb > 0.2 mm. In our model, we assume that pebbles settle and form a porous pebble-pile core upon ice melting. This assumption is valid for cases where rpeb ≫ 0.1 mm (Wakita and Sekiya, 2011). In contrast, if rpeb ≲ 0.1 mm, “mud convection” is ex-pected to occur within the planetesimal (e.g., Bland and Travis, 2017; Travis et al., 2018). Mud convection refers to e ffi cient heat transfer driven by fluid convection dynamically coupled with fine dust grains. The threshold value of rpeb separating pebble-pile core formation and mud convection likely depends on the balance between gravitational settling and stirring by convecting fluid. Based on the numerical results of Bland and Travis (2017), we assume this threshold to be on the order of 0.1 mm. Therefore, the model presented in this study is no longer applicable when rpeb ≲ 0.1 mm, and in such cases, the temperature increase within the planetesimal would be sup-pressed by mud convection.

5.5. H2 gas and unsaturated pores within the core 

In this study, we neglect the production of gaseous H 2 dur-ing phyllosilicate formation. However, in reality, H 2 gas is produced through aqueous alteration (Kurokawa et al., 2022; Nakamura, T. et al., 2023), and a portion of it would be retained within the planetesimal. The retention of H 2 gas is essential to reproduce the oxidation state of iron: if all H 2 gas had escaped from the system, the oxidation product of metallic Fe would not be magnetite (Fe 3O4) but hematite (Fe 2O3), which is not found in the major lithology (e.g., Nakamura, T. et al., 2023). Some of the produced H 2 gas would also be transported outward within the planetesimal (Shibuya et al., 2024), and the amount of re-tained H 2 gas a ff ects the assemblage of precipitating minerals. This assemblage also depends on the local water-to-rock mass ratio, a point we discuss further in Supplementary Text S7. We speculate that H 2 gas could be retained within nm- or 

μm-sized pores in pebbles. Using X-ray computed nanoto-mography, Tsuchiyama et al. (2024) identified numerous μm-scale pores and cracks in the Ryugu samples. Dobric˘ a et al. (2023) also observed abundant nanopores with facets and dis-locations in spherulitic magnetite using transmission electron microscopy. The retention of H 2 gas within pebbles indicates the presence of intra-pebble pores that are unsaturated with wa-ter. Water could evaporate in these unsaturated pores, leading to the formation of evaporite minerals, such as chlorides and sulfates, observed in Ryugu samples (Matsumoto et al., 2024). 

6. Summary 

Recent astronomical observations of planet-forming circum-stellar disks have revealed that planetesimals formed from 0.1-mm to 1-cm-sized dust aggregates, commonly referred to as "pebbles." Icy planetesimals that formed beyond the water snow line were composed of both rock and ice. A rocky core would form through water–rock di ff erentiation once the internal tem-perature exceeds 273 K. Given the presence of pebbles, the resulting rocky core after di ff erentiation is expected to have a porous pebble-pile structure with voids saturated by liquid wa-ter (see Figure 1). Water circulation through these voids en-hances heat transfer within the core, thereby suppressing the temperature rise caused by the decay of radioactive nuclides. Since the e ffi ciency of water circulation depends on the ra-dius of the constituent pebbles, the pebble size can be con-strained by analyzing extraterrestrial materials that records the aqueous alteration history of its parent planetesimal. Indeed, aqueously formed minerals, particularly dolomite, found in samples returned from asteroid Ryugu provide valuable insights into the formation and evolutionary history of its parent plan-etesimal (see Section 2). Kawasaki et al. (2026) determined the precipitation ages and temperatures of dolomite in two Ryugu samples, C0002 and A0058. Using these constraints, we de-rived plausible estimates for the accretion age and pebble radius of Ryugu’s parent planetesimal. We numerically investigated the thermal evolution of icy planetesimals while accounting for their structural evolution (see Section 4). Our model includes the formation of a pebble-pile core following ice melting and incorporates enhanced heat transfer within the core due to water circulation (see Section 3.3). We found that the precipitation ages and temperatures of dolomite in both the C0002 and A0058 grains can be repro-duced if the parent planetesimal accreted earlier than 2 .0 Myr after CAI formation and had a pebble radius smaller than 2 mm (see Figure 8). Regardless of di ff erences in the details of ther-mal evolution modeling, our estimated accretion age aligns with the findings of Kawasaki et al. (2026), suggesting that Ryugu’s parent planetesimal accreted earlier than the major-ity of chondrule-bearing carbonaceous chondrite parent bodies (e.g., Fukuda et al., 2022). Furthermore, a pebble radius smaller than 2 mm is consistent with theoretical predictions for plan-etesimals that accreted beyond the CO 2 snow line (e.g., Naka-mura, T. et al., 2023), where pebbles are less adhesive upon collision (e.g., Okuzumi and Tazaki, 2019).
