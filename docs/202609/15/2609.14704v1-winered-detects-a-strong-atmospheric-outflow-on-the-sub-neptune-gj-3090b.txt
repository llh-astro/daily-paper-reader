ABSTRACT
Sub-Neptunes are the most common short-period (P < 100 d) planets known. A major goal for the
field is to build a comprehensive understanding of their compositions and evolutionary histories, and
an important tool for achieving this goal is metastable helium (He∗) transmission spectroscopy, which
traces atmospheric escape. In this paper, we report the detection of He∗in the atmosphere of the
sub-Neptune GJ 3090b at high spectral resolution. We observed two consecutive transits of this planet
with Magellan II/WINERED, and detected peak He∗absorptions during transit of 2.033 ± 0.086%
(23.6σ) and 1.619 ± 0.122% (13.3σ), respectively.
The first transit was affected by a stellar flare
near egress while the second transit was unaffected. The signal is variable in amplitude and deeper
than anticipated by the JWST/NIRISS observations of E.-M. Ahrer et al. (2025). We do not observe a
significant Doppler shift, and the signal has a FWHM of 25 km s−1, implicating photoevaporation. Our
outflow modeling suggests that Zatm ≲100× solar, as the observed He∗amplitudes are challenging
to achieve in a metal-rich atmosphere. To match the muted transmission spectra from JWST and
VLT/CRIRES+, GJ 3090b’s lower atmosphere is likely covered by aerosols. Finally, we found that
GJ 3090b has an atmospheric lifetime of ∼300 Myr, much shorter than previously reported. This
adolescent (∼1 Gyr) sub-Neptune is currently in a transformative phase of photoevaporative evolution.
1. INTRODUCTION
More than half of the stars in our Galaxy host a
short-period (P < 100 d) planet between the sizes of
Earth and Neptune (R⊕< Rp < 4R⊕) (A. W. Howard
et al. 2012; C. D. Dressing & D. Charbonneau 2013;
J. L. Bean et al. 2021).
The distribution of plane-
tary radii in this size range is bimodal (B. J. Fulton
et al. 2017):
smaller, ostensibly rocky planets with
Rp ≲1.5R⊕(“super-Earths”) are separated from larger
2R⊕≲Rp ≲4R⊕“sub-Neptunes” by a radius valley
between roughly 1.5R⊕and 2R⊕. The structures, com-

common small planets are important open problems in
exoplanet astronomy as they are challenging to infer
from mass and radius alone (e.g. L. Zeng et al. 2019;
R. Luque & E. Pall´e 2022; J. Teske et al. 2025).
Transmission spectroscopy can provide insight into
the structures and compositions of small exoplanets.
Sub-Neptune transmission spectra from JWST have al-

and TOI-421b (N. Madhusudhan et al. 2023; B. Daven-

while the higher-MMW atmospheres may be suggestive
of water-rich bulk compositions, i.e. the “water world”
scenario (E. R. Adams et al. 2008; L. A. Rogers et al.
2011; B. Bitsch et al. 2021; R. Luque & E. Pall´e 2022;
A. Chakrabarty et al. 2026). Fractionating atmospheric
loss (C. Cherubim et al. 2025; A. Louca & Y. Miguel
2025; M. Valatsou et al. 2026) and geochemical inter-

Schlichting & E. D. Young 2022; W. Misener et al. 2023;
A. Gupta et al. 2025; K. Heng et al. 2025) may also af-

A larger sample size
would help to distinguish between some of these possi-
bilities, but most sub-Neptune transmission spectra are
featureless, which could either be due to high MMWs,
high-altitude aerosols, or both (e.g. N. L. Wallack et al.
2024; J. Teske et al. 2025).
Planetary evolution in the 1R⊕< Rp < 4R⊕regime
also remains debated. The radius valley was predicted
by thermal escape models assuming H2/He envelopes
atop rocky cores (E. D. Lopez & J. J. Fortney 2013;
J. E. Owen & Y. Wu 2013), but different thermal escape
mechanisms can explain the data equally well, including
XUV-driven photoevaporation (J. E. Owen & Y. Wu
2017) and core-powered mass loss (S. Ginzburg et al.
2018; A. Gupta & H. E. Schlichting 2020). The relative
contributions of these mechanisms are challenging to dis-
tinguish using population statistics alone (J. G. Rogers
et al. 2021), and they may sculpt the small planet popu-
lation together rather than operating in isolation (J. E.
Owen & H. E. Schlichting 2024; W. Misener et al. 2026).
Moreover, multiple groups have argued that the valley
need not be fully evolutionary; it could result from fun-
damental limits to gas accretion for small cores (E. J.
Lee & N. J. Connors 2021; E. J. Lee et al. 2022; J.
Nielsen et al. 2025) or from the inward migration of
“water worlds” (R. Burn et al. 2024; J. Venturini et al.
2024). The abundance of theoretical interpretations for
the radius valley highlights a need for additional empir-
ical constraints on sub-Neptune evolution.
We can make progress on illuminating fundamental
small planet properties by studying their upper atmo-
spheres using the metastable He 10833 ˚A (He∗) triplet
(L. Nortmann et al. 2018; J. J. Spake et al. 2018). He∗
traces atmospheric escape (A. Oklopˇci´c & C. M. Hirata
2018; G. Ballabio & J. E. Owen 2025), which makes
it a valuable probe of planetary evolution. For exam-
ple, the He∗line shape is sensitive to outflow veloci-
ties and can be constrained precisely (M. Zhang et al.
2023b), so He∗observations can directly distinguish be-
tween photoevaporation (for which flow speeds are typi-
cally faster, ≳10 km s−1; R. A. Murray-Clay et al. 2009;
J. E. Owen et al. 2023) and core-powered mass-loss (for
which flow speeds are typically slower, ∼2 −3 km s−1;
S. Ginzburg et al. 2018; W. Misener et al. 2025). Addi-
tionally, He∗is less likely to be detected in high-MMW
atmospheres (Zatm ≳100Z⊙), like those expected in the
water-world scenario (J. G. Rogers et al. 2026). Such at-
mospheres are predicted to have small scale heights, low
abundances of helium, and much weaker outflows due to
metal and/or molecular cooling (M. Salz et al. 2016; S.
Vissapragada et al. 2024; A. Garc´ıa Mu˜noz et al. 2025;
T. Yoshida & E. Gaidos 2025; M. Zhang et al. 2025; D.
Kubyshkina et al. 2026; A. R. Taylor et al. 2026). He∗
detections can therefore help break degeneracies in the
interpretation of featureless transmission spectra.
With these applications of He∗in mind, we initi-
ated the WINERED Helium Consortium, which aims
to constrain He∗absorption in a large sample of
sub-Neptune-sized exoplanets.
WINERED (Y. Ikeda
et al. 2022; S. Otsubo et al. 2024) is a high-resolution
(R ∼68, 000) near-infrared spectrograph mounted on
Magellan-II/Clay and has already demonstrated good
precision on-sky for He∗transit spectroscopy (S. Viss-
apragada et al. 2024; C. Cherubim et al. 2026). Our goal
is to constrain He∗absorption (or lack thereof) to better
than 0.2% precision in the line core for ≳30 targets. We
selected this precision goal because most sub-Neptune
He∗detections to date have been around ∼1% in am-
plitude (J. Orell-Miquel et al. 2024), and 0.2% precision
would correspond to a 5σ detection in this case.
In this paper, we report a high-resolution detection
of strong He∗absorption on GJ 3090b, a relatively
low-density sub-Neptune (Mp = 4.5 ± 0.5M⊕, Rp =
2.2 ± 0.1R⊕, ρp = 2.4 ± 0.3 g cm−3) on a P = 2.85
day orbit around an M2V star (giving it an equilib-
rium temperature of Teq = 723 ± 13 K; J. M. Almenara
et al. 2022; P. Lamontagne et al. 2026).
The system
is young; J. M. Almenara et al. (2022) estimate a gy-
rochronological age of 1.02+0.23
−0.15 Gyr. GJ 3090b is also
one of the best-known sub-Neptunes for transmission
spectroscopy, with a Transmission Spectroscopy Metric
(E. M. R. Kempton et al. 2018) of 182+27
−23.
Previous
JWST/NIRISS observations revealed escaping helium
in its atmosphere (E.-M. Ahrer et al. 2025), but nei-
ther JWST nor ground-based VLT/CRIRES+ observa-
tions could confidently detect molecular absorption from
the lower atmosphere (L. T. Parker et al. 2025). In Sec-
tion 2, we describe our observations and subsequent data
reduction process, and in Section 3, we describe the re-
sults of the observations. We compare the He∗absorp-
tion signal to models in Section 4 to get a sense for the
planetary outflow physics, and we conclude and discuss
the broader implications of our results in Section 5.

2. OBSERVATIONS AND DATA REDUCTION
We observed two consecutive transits of GJ 3090b on
2024 September 22 and 2024 September 25 with Magel-
lan II/WINERED (Y. Ikeda et al. 2022; S. Otsubo et al.
2024). For both observations, we operated WINERED
in HIRES-Y mode with the 100 µm slit to maximize
resolving power. We took continuous 180 s exposures
with an ABBA nodding strategy (with a nodding am-
plitude of 5′′) to subtract the telluric OH emission lines
and to mitigate the effects of persistence between expo-
sures. Our observations lasted from 06:08 UT to 10:00
UT on the first night (airmass 1.05 to 1.54 with mini-
mum airmass 1.05) and from 01:40 UT to 06:29 UT on
the second night (airmass 1.06 to 1.57 with minimum
airmass 1.05). The predicted mid-transit times (using
ephemerides from J. M. Almenara et al. 2022) were 08:51
UT and 05:20 UT, respectively, with uncertainties less
than 3 min, so the T14 = 1.28 hr transit events were cov-
ered comfortably with ample baseline. In all, 64 spectra
were obtained on the first night, and 80 spectra were
obtained on the second night.
We then reduced the data using the method from
S. Vissapragada et al. (2024) with some updates that
we summarize briefly here. We used the public WARP
pipeline (S. Hamano et al. 2024) to obtain a one-
dimensional spectrum (with wavelengths in vacuum) for
each exposure in order 163, which contains He∗.
We
removed one spectrum with low S/N on the first night.
We also noticed that the detector persistence had not yet
settled at the beginning of the second night of observa-
tions, so we dropped the first 10 spectra for that night
to avoid biasing the out-of-transit template. Excluding
these frames, the median per-pixel S/N was 142.4 on the
first night and 146.3 on the second night.
WINERED spectra exhibit a systematic flux differ-
ence between spectra taken at the A and B positions,
which is often clearly seen around 10810 ˚A when using
the nominal nod positions (e.g. C. Cherubim et al. 2026).
To estimate the systematic, we divided each spectrum
by its median, and then took the ratio of the median-
normalized A and B spectra for each AB and BA pair.
We then averaged the systematic spectra obtained for
all AB and BA pairs to obtain a higher S/N estimate of
the effect. Finally, we multiplied all spectra taken at the
B position by the estimate to correct the systematic off-
set. This correction removed variations correlated with
nod position in the final timeseries spectrum.
We then continuum-normalized the spectra. We first
constructed a line mask, starting by averaging together
all median-normalized spectra from a given night and
masking a broad region around the helium line from
10830 ˚A to 10835 ˚A. We then iteratively fit a third-
order Chebyshev polynomial to the combined spectrum,
calculated the median absolute deviation (MAD) from
the polynomial residuals, and added pixels more than
1×MAD below the polynomial to the line mask. This
procedure was iterated 11 times to generate a global line
mask for each night. Then, we applied the global line
mask to each individual spectrum and fit a third-order
Chebyshev polynomial to the line-free regions. Next, we
refined the wavelength solution by fitting for a shift be-
tween the observed positions of the telluric H2O absorp-
tion lines and model telluric lines (using the HITRAN
interface hapi; R. V. Kochanov et al. 2016; I. E. Gor-
don et al. 2022). The wavelength shifts were typically
≲0.02 ˚A on the first night and ≲0.03 ˚A on the second
night. Once the wavelength solutions were refined, we
masked telluric H2O lines and any residuals from OH
sky subtraction before shifting the data into the stel-
lar rest frame. We then constructed a stellar template
using out-of-transit exposures (selected with the J. M.
Almenara et al. 2022 ephemerides).
In Figure 1, we show the stellar template for each
transit in the upper panels. We then divided all of the
spectra by the appropriate stellar templates to obtain
the normalized timeseries spectra shown in the middle
panels of Figure 1. We observed a flare that overlapped
with egress during the first transit (Appendix A), so
we masked out all flare-affected data (the final 17 spec-
tra) when constructing the out-of-transit template and
planetary transmission spectrum for the first night. We
shifted all of the in-transit spectra into the planetary
rest frame using the known orbital properties of GJ
3090b (P. Lamontagne et al. 2026).
We took the av-
erage of the in-transit spectra and divided by the out-
of-transit template to obtain the planetary transmission
spectrum for each night, which are shown in the bottom
panel of Figure 1.

3.2. Comparison to E.-M. Ahrer et al. (2025)
Recently, E.-M. Ahrer et al. (2025) reported a 5σ de-
tection of He∗in GJ 3090b at R ∼650 using JWST
NIRISS/SOSS. These authors anticipated a shallower
signal (1.0 ± 0.2%) at high resolution than we observed.
To calculate the expected high-resolution signal, they
used a Gaussian with 0.75 ˚A FWHM to model the in-
trinsic signal, which is somewhat narrower than what
we observed with WINERED. To compare the results
on more equal footing, we convolved our GP-subtracted
WINERED transmission spectra with NIRISS’s line-
spread function and interpolated the result onto the
pixel-level grid from E.-M. Ahrer et al. (2025).
We
find an expected NIRISS signal of 1304 ± 38 ppm for
the first night and 1174 ± 37 ppm for the second night.
Each observation exceeds the NIRISS observations (E.-
M. Ahrer et al. 2025 find 434 ± 79 ppm) by at least
5σ, and the averaged observation exceeds the reported
NIRISS measurement by 8.5σ, adding some weight to

were taken six months apart. The NIRISS signal does
not appear to vary significantly across these transits,
but the limited single-transit NIRISS SNR makes it dif-

would have been 130 ppm if observed by NIRISS, unde-
tectable at the achieved single-transit precisions.
4. OUTFLOW MODELING
Next, we compared our observed He∗signal to the
predictions of multiple outflow models, focusing first on
models in the literature. E.-M. Ahrer et al. (2025) per-
formed detailed non-isothermal modeling of the He∗sig-
nal using the ATES code (A. Caldiroli et al. 2021; F.
Biassoni et al. 2024) and found an expected outflow sig-
nature of 3.5% and mass-loss rate of
˙M = 1010.1 g s−1,
assuming a 90-10 H/He ratio (by number) and using
a scaled MUSCLES (A. Youngblood et al. 2016) spec-
trum of GJ 832 as a proxy. While E.-M. Ahrer et al.
(2025) state that this corresponds to an atmospheric
lifetime of ∼50 Gyr (using Mp/ ˙Mp ≈50 Gyr), the at-
mospheric mass (assuming it is predominantly H/He)
is at most 4.3% of Mp for this sub-Neptune (J. M. Al-
menara et al. 2022; P. Lamontagne et al. 2026), so the
true atmospheric lifetime in their models is fatmMp/ ˙Mp,
only about 2 Gyr at most. The sunset model grid (D.
Linssen et al. 2025) predicts a He∗signal of 3.76% and a
mass-loss rate of 1010.0 g s−1 assuming a solar H/He ra-
tio, but using the scaled MUSCLES spectrum of GJ 176
as an XUV proxy instead of GJ 832. Both models agree
well on the expected He∗absorption signal despite differ-
ences in methodology, but both models also significantly
overpredict the signal compared to the observations.
We performed our own modeling of GJ 3090b us-
ing pyTPCI (R. Rosener et al. 2025) and sunbather (D.
Linssen et al. 2024).
A critical difference for our ap-
proach is the XUV spectrum.

spectrum to match observables.

Especially in younger systems, H/He can change signif-

The planetary atmospheric metallicity Zatm
also
makes a major difference in the He∗models. The pre-
dicted He∗signal collapses at high metallicities, as an-
ticipated by previous works (e.g. T. Yoshida & E. Gai-
dos 2025; M. Zhang et al. 2025; M. Saidel et al. 2025).
L. T. Parker et al. (2025) reports the classic degeneracy
between a high-metallicity atmosphere and an aerosol-
covered atmosphere for GJ 3090b, which is largely bro-
ken by the He∗measurement. If the atmospheric metal-
licity was much greater than 100× solar, the He∗signal
would be unobservable (Figure 2) unless the atmosphere
was also very helium-rich. Instead, it is more likely that
the lower atmosphere of the planet is obscured by an
aerosol layer. This is in some tension with E.-M. Ahrer
et al. (2025), who find a much higher Zatm in their self-
consistent retrievals on the JWST/NIRISS observations
(they find ≳100×solar metallicity at 3σ for clouds at

relies on fitting on a largely featureless spectrum, and

upper and lower atmospheric metallicities need not be

whereas the lower-atmospheric metallicity can be larger.
However, for GJ 3090b the mass-loss rate far exceeds the

meaningfully changes the interpretation of GJ 3090b’s
evolutionary state.
With a planetary mass of 4.5M⊕
and a maximum H/He inventory of roughly 4.3% (J. M.
Almenara et al. 2022; P. Lamontagne et al. 2026), GJ

small envelope mass fraction.
5. CONCLUSIONS
The metastable He 10833 ˚A triplet (He∗) is a power-
ful tracer of atmospheric loss processes and atmospheric
composition in transiting exoplanets. We initiated the
WINERED Helium Consortium to survey He∗in sub-
Neptunes, a planetary population for which atmospheric
loss processes and atmospheric composition are both
matters of debate. Our goal is to survey He∗across a
broad statistical sample (aiming for ≳30 sub-Neptunes)
at high precision (aiming for 0.2% precision). As part of
the Consortium, we observed two consecutive transits of
the low-density sub-Neptune GJ 3090b and found clear
evidence for a strong planetary outflow. We detected a
peak absorption of 2.033 ± 0.086% (23.6σ) on the first
night (during which we also observed a stellar flare) and
1.619 ± 0.122% (13.3σ) on the second night. Our high-
SNR detections on each night demonstrate that Magel-
lan II/WINERED is capable of precise He∗constraints
on the smallest exoplanets.
The scale of the outflow is remarkable for a small
exoplanet: the helium triplet is opaque out past 50%
of the planetary Roche radius, indicating a vigorous
outflow.
The He∗signal is not Doppler-shifted, and
also varied slightly (2.8σ) between our two observations.
Our measured absorptions are 8.5σ deeper than the re-
cent NIRISS He∗measurement from E.-M. Ahrer et al.
(2025), further supporting the case for variability. GJ
3090b therefore adds to the growing sample of variable
He∗absorption signals (M. Zhang et al. 2022a; G. Guil-
luy et al. 2024; W. G. Levine et al. 2024; R. Allart et al.
2025; K. A. Bennett et al. 2026; C. Cherubim et al. 2026;
A. Gressier et al. 2026; M. Radica 2026), although addi-
tional data would be helpful in confirming this finding.
The width of the feature is broad (25 km s−1), con-
sistent with a hot photoevaporative outflow rather than
a cooler core-powered wind, as predicted for a planet
of this size, mass, and equilibrium temperature by W.
Misener et al. (2026). All sub-Neptune He∗detections
thus far exhibit similar broadening (M. Zhang et al.
2023b; J. Orell-Miquel et al. 2024; M. Zhang et al.
2025). However, G. Ballabio & J. E. Owen (2025) cau-
tion that this may result from detection bias: they ar-
gue that core-powered flows may be relatively poor en-
vironments for populating the metastable state. More
detailed simulations of He∗absorption in more realis-
tic (i.e. non-isothermal, W. Misener et al. 2025) core-
powered winds, coupled with the large statistical sample
of outflow measurements that we will collect, may help
to place stronger constraints on the role of core-powered
mass loss across the sub-Neptune population.
We made one-dimensional hydrodynamical simula-
tions of the outflow using pyTPCI (R. Rosener et al.
2025) and sunbather (D. Linssen et al. 2024).
When
making the simulations, we took into account X-ray
constraints from eROSITA (E. Magaudda et al. 2022)
and both FUV and NUV constraints from GALEX (L.
Bianchi et al. 2017) to more accurately capture the high-
energy SED for this young, active star.
Our models
demonstrate that the atmospheric metallicity is unlikely
to greatly exceed ∼100× solar. This breaks the clas-
sic metallicity-aerosol degeneracy for GJ 3090b: non-
detections of molecular features in this planet’s lower
atmosphere (E.-M. Ahrer et al. 2025; L. T. Parker et al.
2025) are more likely due to aerosol coverage than high
metallicity.
The hydrodynamical simulations also suggest that the
mass-loss rate is about an order of magnitude larger than
previously anticipated for this system, on the order of
1011 g s−1.
Combined with the fact that the H2/He
inventory of this planet is at most only 4.3% (J. M.
Almenara et al. 2022; P. Lamontagne et al. 2026), we
find that GJ 3090b has an envelope loss timescale of
fenvMp/ ˙Mp ∼300 Myr.
This is two orders of mag-
nitude shorter than the 50 Gyr timescale quoted by

where photoevaporation is starting to significantly de-

A. THE STELLAR FLARE
During the first transit of GJ 3090b, the helium triplet brightened suddenly in the stellar rest frame just before egress.
There were no stoppages or changes in strategy for this observation at any point, and the weather conditions were also
quite stable throughout the first night. A natural astrophysical explanation is a stellar flare. Flares are not uncommon
in He∗observations of M stars (e.g. B. Fuhrmeister et al. 2020; S. Kanodia et al. 2022), and GJ 3090 is known to
be active with log(R′
HK) = −4.416 ± 0.048 (J. M. Almenara et al. 2022) and X-ray luminosity LX = 1027.96 erg s−1
(E. Magaudda et al. 2022). To confirm that this event was indeed a flare, we also investigated the Paschen γ and δ
lines, which are covered by WINERED HIRES-Y in orders 161 and 176, respectively. Both of these lines also trace
flares (e.g. B. Fuhrmeister et al. 2023). We reduced the data in these orders similarly to the metastable helium order,
and we integrated all three lines in a ±0.5˚A window around each of the line positions in the stellar rest frame for
each spectrum. The resulting spectrophotometric light curves (normalized by the pre-ingress baseline) are shown in
Figure 3. All three tracers show an impulsive brightening followed by a slow decay, confirming that this is indeed a
stellar flare.

B. MODELING DETAILS
We modeled the outflow of GJ 3090b using sunbather (D. Linssen et al. 2024) and pyTPCI (R. Rosener et al. 2025).
Both models have their individual shortcomings, which we hoped to overcome by combining multiple approaches.
Briefly, sunbather couples the NLTE photoionization code Cloudy (G. J. Ferland et al. 1998, 2017; M. Chatzikos
et al. 2023) with the 1D Parker wind code p-winds (L. A. Dos Santos et al. 2022). This model assumes an initial
isothermal Parker wind (with an assumed mass-loss rate and thermosphere temperature) calculated with p-winds and
then determines the non-isothermal temperature structure using Cloudy, iterating until the temperature structure is
converged. This approach converges relatively quickly, but outputs depend sensitively on the assumed Parker wind
properties. On the other hand, pyTPCI is a self-consistent outflow modeling approach based on The PLUTO-Cloudy
Interface from M. Salz et al. (2015). Here, the hydrodynamics code PLUTO self-consistently treats the outflow launching
using heating and cooling rates calculated with Cloudy. We take the outflow density, velocity, temperature and He∗
profiles from pyTPCI and perform radiative transfer in p-winds (in an equivalent method to the sunbather radiative


Helium in GJ 3090b
11
transfer approach) to produce model He∗spectra. Although the wind-launching process is treated more realistically
by pyTPCI, the run time is much slower, and convergence is a challenge especially in high-metallicity outflows (R.
Rosener et al. 2025).
All of our models used the planetary, stellar, and orbital parameters from P. Lamontagne et al. (2026) along with
the scaled XUV spectrum of GJ 176 described in the main text. We assessed the influence of three different uncertain
model parameters: the planet mass Mp, the hydrogen-to-helium number ratio H/He, and the planetary atmospheric
metallicity Zatm. Our nominal model has the mass fixed at the P. Lamontagne et al. (2026) value and H/He and Zatm
fixed to solar. We then re-ran the models at ±1σ on Mp, at H/He ratios of 99/1 and 80/20, and Zatm of 10, 100,
200, and 300× solar. Due to the differing model strategies, each model also requires some individual assumptions. For
sunbather, we assume an initial isothermal Parker wind at ˙M = 1011 g s−1 (based on an ATES model run; A. Caldiroli
et al. 2021) and an initial temperature of T0 = 4500 K. This is roughly consistent with what is observed for
˙M in
the self-consistent pyTPCI models. For pyTPCI we adopted a lower-boundary number density of 1014 cm−3. We adopt
the same scheme as R. Rosener et al. (2025) where we run the model for at least t = 100 flow times (all numbers
are in flow time units of Rp/(1 km s−1)) before turning on advection, after which we attempt to run to t = 1000
flow times (but stop short if the metastable helium number density stops changing by more than 5%). For the slow
metal-rich cases, we instead aim to run the model for t = 5 flow times before turning on advection, after which we
run to t = 50 (again stopping short if the metastable helium number density is converged). For the higher-metallicity
runs, the initial timestep used was much smaller. Model parameters and outputs for the pyTPCI runs are summarized
in Table 2.
The predicted He∗signatures for all models are shown in Figure 2, and we show the model velocity and metastable
helium number density profiles in Figure 4 and Figure 5, respectively. The helium triplet behaves fairly similarly
between the two models. In neither model does the mass uncertainty significantly affect the model, whereas H/He
makes a major difference across both grids. The nominal models also anticipate a stronger He∗signature than the
high-Zatm models in all cases. One difference between the models is that as Zatm increases, He∗drops monotonically
in sunbather, whereas it increases and then decreases in pyTPCI. This is because the sunbather models are pinned to
the initial
˙M assumption, whereas the pyTPCI models are self-consistently including the heating and process into the
wind-launching. At modest Zatm ∼10× solar the metal heating results in a stronger
˙M, whereas at Zatm ≳100×
solar, metal cooling results in a decreased ˙M (M. Zhang et al. 2025). Another key difference between the models is the
predicted broadening, which arises from how the flow behaves at the lower boundary. In Figure 5, lower-metallicity
pyTPCI models show a persistent helium “bump” close to Rp, while the Parker wind models are smooth. The bump
occurs before the flow is fully accelerated, which leads to the predicted He∗signatures from pyTPCI having multiple
velocity components. Although the multicomponent signature appears to be a worse fit to the data than the smoother
signature predicted by sunbather, higher resolving power (e.g. M. C. H. Leung et al. 2025) is necessary to assess these
differences carefully.
