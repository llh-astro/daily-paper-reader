ABSTRACT We present new constraints on a candidate third companion in the 61 Cygni binary system—one of the closest neighbors to our solar system (3.5 pc), the first stars to have a measured parallax, and two of the most favorable targets for habitable zone exo-Earth searches with future direct imaging missions. P. Kervella et al. (2022) first identified the candidate based on a 4.4 σ significance tan-gential velocity anomaly in the system determined using Hipparcos and Gaia DR3 astrometry of the A and B components. We combine this astrometric evidence with constraints from Gaia DR3 RUWE 

measurements, MMT/Clio Lp adaptive optics imaging, nearly four decades of radial velocity (RV) measurements (including ∼2 years of new NEID RVs presented here), and N -body dynamical sta-bility calculations of both stars in a Monte Carlo simulation framework to estimate the candidate companion’s mass and semi-major axis. Our analysis reveals that all of the available data are com-patible with an ≈ (8 ± 3) MJup super-Jupiter in an ≈ (8 ± 3) au orbit, with a broad range of allowed eccentricities and mutual inclinations. It cannot be determined which component of the binary the candidate orbits. Using the Sonora Flame Skimmer evolutionary models, given the system’s mature age of (6 .0 ± 1.0) Gyr, we estimate a companion effective temperature of (282 ± 55) K and radius of (1 .02 ± 0.02) RJup . Finally, we evaluate the stability of terrestrial planets in the habitable zone (HZ) of 61 Cygni AB in the presence of the giant planet candidate using N -body simulations. A companion semi-major axis ≲5 au, irrespective of the planet-planet mutual inclination, and between ≈5 au and 10 au, for misaligned orbits (40 ◦ ≲ imut ≲ 140 ◦), severely disrupts the HZ. Our results highlight the need to confirm the planet candidate and characterize its orbit to inform future exo-Earth searches with missions such as SHERA, HWO, and LIFE.

INTRODUCTION Giant planets play a critical role in the formation, evolution, and habitability of terrestrial exoplanets (e.g. S. N. Raymond 2006). At early times, they can restrict the inward migration of protoplanetary cores that would deplete accretion material in the habitable zone (HZ; A. Izidoro et al. 2014, 2015). They may facilitate the de-livery of life-supporting volatiles to terrestrial planets by influencing the rate of cometary and/or minor body impacts (J. Horner & B. W. Jones 2008, 2009; S. N. Raymond & A. Izidoro 2017; J. Venturini et al. 2020; S. R. Kane & E. L. Miles 2025). A giant planet near the HZ can also destabilize potential terrestrial planets in the region over long timescales through dynamical per-turbations (e.g., M. Noble et al. 2002; S. R. Kane et al. 2024; C. Beichman et al. 2025). Thus, it is necessary to detect and characterize, or place limits on the presence of, any massive planet(s) in nearby systems most likely to be targeted in the search for habitable exoplanets. At a distance of 3.5 pc, the 61 Cygni system (here-after 61 Cyg; Table 1) is one of the nearest neighbors to our solar system. The main sequence components 61 Cyg A (K5V, ≈ 0.68 M⊙) and 61 Cyg B (K7V, 

≈ 0.63 M⊙) together form an eccentric visual binary in a long-period orbit ( e ≈ 0.44, a ≈ 87 au, P ≈ 707 year; M. R. Giovinazzi et al. 2025). 61 Cyg’s age is estimated to be 6 .0 ± 1.0 Gyr (P. Kervella et al. 2008), similar to our solar system’s age of ∼4.6 Gyr. The system exhibits the 6 th largest proper motion, first estimated by G. Piazzi (1806) (G. Fodera Serio 1990), among all stars cataloged by Gaia ( ≈ 5.′′ 3/yr; Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023). This proper motion measurement led to one of the most significant achievements in astrophysics, the first determination of a stellar parallax (F. Bessel 1838). Nearly two centuries later, 61 Cyg AB continue to hold importance in astrophysics. They are two of the closest and most favorable targets (Tier A; E. Mama-jek & K. Stapelfeldt 2024) to search for, directly im-age (amenable to near 100% search completeness; B. S. Gaudi et al. 2020), and detect biosignatures (G. N. Ar-ney 2019) in HZ Earth-analogs with future missions such as the Searching for Habitable Exoplanets with Relative Astrometry (SHERA; J. L. Christiansen et al. 2026) mis-sion concept, the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO; L. D. Feinberg et al. 2026), and the Large Interferometer for Exoplanets (LIFE; S. P. Quanz et al. 2022) mission. 61 Cyg AB have been frequently targeted in the search for exoplanets (K. A. Strand 1943, 1957; A. N. Deich &

The paper is thus organized as follows. Section 2 summarizes the astrometric evidence for a companion in the 61 Cyg system and presents archival imaging ob-servations as well as improved sensitivity estimates to planets in the system with new NEID RV observations. Section 3 combines absolute astrometry, RV, imaging, and stability calculations to place new constraints on the orbital and physical properties of the companion in the system. Section 4 investigates the stability of HZ terrestrial planets in the presence of the companion in-ferred from astrometry. Section 5 lists our conclusions. Finally, Appendix A presents the fits to the complete RV datasets of 61 Cyg AB.

Us-ing these 2D maps, they construct one-dimensional (1D) sensitivity curves for various percentiles in sensitivity as a function of separation. In our analysis, we use the 50th percentile curve, which corresponds to the median (97% completeness) sensitivity across all position angles at a fixed separation. A. N. Heinze et al. UPDATED CONSTRAINTS ON COMPANION PROPERTIES In this section, we combine the astrometric evidence for a companion in the 61 Cyg system together with existing imaging observations, new radial velocity mea-surements, the Gaia DR3 RUWE value of each star, and dynamical stability calculations in a Monte Carlo sim-ulation framework to obtain new constraints on the or-bital and physical properties of the candidate compan-ion, which we refer to as “61 Cyg b” for convenience. The calculations discussed below are performed sepa-rately for 61 Cyg A and B assuming the companion or-bits either one of the stars. 3.1. Orbital Parameter Sampling Procedure 

We begin by uniformly sampling the companion semi-major axis (SMA, a) at 10 6 points in the range 1–50 au. Next, for each sampled value of the companion’s semi-major axis, we draw the companion’s mass ( m) from a normal distribution defined by the companion mass-SMA function compatible with the observation of the orbital velocity anomaly (Figure 1). The eccentricity (e) of each sampled orbit is assigned by sampling the population-level eccentricity distributions of giant plan-ets and brown dwarfs. For 1–10 au orbits, we use the mass-dependent eccentricity distributions inferred by G. J. Gilbert et al. (2026, see their Figure 2) using the California Legacy Survey sample (L. J. Rosenthal et al. 2021) combined together with Hipparcos-Gaia as-trometry. For > 10 au orbits, we use the giant planet (≤ 15 MJup ) and brown dwarf ( > 15 MJup ) eccentricity distributions inferred by V. Nagpal et al. (2023) using the B. P. Bowler et al. (2020) sample, specifically, the case that employs a truncated Gaussian hyperprior on the beta distribution parameters when performing hier-archical Bayesian modeling. The companion’s sky incli-nation ( i) is uniformly sampled from a cosine distribu-tion consistent with random orbital orientation. The re-maining orbital elements of the companion (argument of periastron ω, longitude of ascending node Ω, and time of periastron passage parameter τ 28 ) are sampled from uni-form distributions spanning their possible values. The sampled orbits are evaluated for dynamical sta-bility over a timescale of 1 Myr, where previous studies of planets in binaries showed that instabilities typically occur on the secular timescale (B. Quarles & J. J. Lis-sauer 2016; B. Quarles et al. 2020, 2026) . We use the 

N -body simulation package Rebound [4.4.6] (H. Rein & S.-F. Liu 2012) to evolve three-body systems consist-ing of the 61 Cyg AB system along with the putative companion. The posterior outputs from the above anal-ysis provides the correlated initial conditions for these stability simulations, which include the masses (of all three bodies) and the orbital elements of the compan-ion and stellar orbits. The system is evolved using the binary orbital plane as the reference, where both or-bits start at their respective pericenter (mean anomaly, MA = 0 ◦). The orbits of the companion and binary be-gin misaligned (in both the eccentricity and inclination vectors) so that any bias from our initial mean anomaly is negligible.

Our simulations for these three-body systems use the adaptive ias15 integrator (H. Rein & D. S. Spiegel 2015), where we set a timestep floor at 10 −4 times the companion’s initial orbital period. We deem an initial condition as unstable when a simulation reaches the fol-lowing conditions: (1) companion collides with the host star; (2) companion eccentricity exceeds 0 .95; or (3) companion’s distance exceeds the distance to the La-grange point L1. The simulation uses the “direct” col-lision detection method within Rebound using the radii: 0.667 R⊙ for 61 Cyg A, 0 .594 R⊙ for 61 Cyg B, and 1.1 RJup for the candidate companion. We limit the companion eccentricity because tidal effects could be-come important for such orbits over long timescales, which we are not including in our numerical model, as well as for numerical convenience. Companion or-bits that go beyond the middle Lagrange points L1 are chaotic and ultimately unstable in most instances (P. A. Wiegert & M. J. Holman 1997; B. Quarles et al. 2020). 4. STABILITY OF TERRESTRIAL PLANETS IN THE HABITABLE ZONE 61 Cyg AB have been identified as prime targets to search for habitable zone exo-Earths with future mis-sions such as SHERA, HWO, and LIFE. We perform 

N -body simulations with the Rebound [4.4.6] (H. Rein & S.-F. Liu 2012) package to assess how the inclusion of the candidate giant planet affects terrestrial planets in the empirical habitable zone of the host star. This is done by evolving a system of 100 massless test particles in the empirical habitable zone along with the candidate giant planet and binary companion for their respective orbital parameters. These parameters are drawn from the final set of triaged orbits determined in Section 3.3. As a result, it is a sample that spans a wide range of correlated parameters in the candidate giant planet’s ec-centricity, semi-major axis, and mutual inclination. The simulation results are expected to be similar for both stars since the candidate giant planet’s properties (Fig-ure 4) and the stellar spectral type are similar. Here, we perform the calculations assuming the candidate giant planet orbits 61 Cyg A. The empirical habitable zone uses the flux received by Venus (1 .91 S⊕) and Mars (0 .32 S⊕) as the inner and outer limits (J. F. Kasting et al. 1993). For 61 Cyg A, we find that the inner and outer distances are 0 .28 au and 0 .68 au, respectively, using the zeroth-order formula that depends on the host star’s luminosity L/L ⊙ and the effective flux received Seff at that distance. The simulations are evolved for the same timescale of 1 Myr used for the dynamical stability simulations, but use the 

TRACE integrator instead of ias15 .The orbital periods of the test particles are much shorter, where the TRACE integrator allows us to eval-uate their initially Keplerian orbits more efficiently at the beginning of the simulation. The WHFast part of the TRACE algorithm uses a fixed timestep of 0 .025 × the orbital period at the inner edge of the habitable zone (i.e., 2 .5% of the shortest orbital period). A test parti-cle is removed from the simulation if: (1) it collides with either star or the candidate planet, (2) it has an eccen-tricity that exceeds 0 .6 for at least 100 yr, or evolve to a distance beyond the L1 Lagrange point. These removal criteria are effectively the same as those we used for our stability simulations, but the second criterion is a more targeted application of a limiting eccentricity based on the habitability concerns (E. Bolmont et al. 2016). Due to our removal criteria, using the ias15 is slower and un-necessary because the test particles are removed before they evolve to very high eccentricity. There are 4 particles per ring and 25 rings that are uniformly spaced throughout the habitable zone. The test particles begin from an initially circular orbit that is coplanar with the binary orbit, while the mean anomaly of the test particles are offset by half a phase relative to the neighboring ring. This offset pattern produces a more uniform area of test particles throughout the habitable zone. The resulting metric at the end of the simulation is the fraction of the original habitable zone particles remaining. If there are 0% remaining, then the candidate giant planet is likely efficient at disrupting planet formation from occurring within the habitable zone (F. Marzari & H. Scholl 2000; P. Th´ ebault et al. 2006; S. N. Raymond 2006; P. Th´ ebault et al. 2008). In contrast, if there is a large percentage remaining, then the habitable zone can be perturbed but can otherwise still potentially host terrestrial planets. CONCLUSIONS In this work, we presented new constraints on the properties of a candidate companion, identified using Hipparcos-Gaia DR3 astrometry, in the 61 Cygni AB bi-nary system by combining absolute astrometry, RV mea-surements, archival direct imaging data, and dynamical stability calculations for the two stars. The key take-aways are summarized below: 1. New NEID RV data extends the RV moni-toring baseline of 61 Cyg AB to 37 years. 

We performed a comprehensive analysis of RV constraints on possible companions in the system using RVSearch , with the addition of new pub-licly available data from APF, MINERVA, and NEID, and an updated HIRES RV catalog from J. T. Teklu et al. (2025) which extends the HIRES coverage through October 2022. No signals at-tributable to a third companion were robustly de-tected. We performed injection-recovery tests and generated RV detection completeness maps as a function of companion eccentricity to help con-strain the Hipparcos-Gaia candidate’s properties in our joint analysis. 2. Regions of dynamical stability for the can-didate companion exist in the binary sys-tem. Using N -body simulations performed over a 1 Myr timescale, we found that only near po-lar (80 ◦ ≲ imut ≲ 100 ◦) or large semi-major axis (≳ 12 au for the prograde and ≳16 au for the retrograde cases) orbits are dynamically excluded for the candidate in the 61 Cyg AB system. The remaining parameter space allowed stable configu-rations for the candidate companion around either star. 3. A cold super-Jupiter candidate in a solar system scale orbit is consistent with all data in hand. We simulated orbits randomly oriented in space, consistent with the tangential velocity anomaly signal, and removed those inconsistent with the Gaia DR3 RUWE , RV and MMT/Clio 

Lp direct imaging limits, and dynamical stabil-ity limits for the two stars. The final triaged or-bit sample constrained the candidate companion’s (mp, a) to (8 .5 ± 3.0 MJup , 8.2 ± 3.0 au) if orbiting 61 Cyg A, and (8 .2 ± 3.0 MJup , 8.5 ± 2.7 au) if or-biting 61 Cyg B. For an age of (6 .0 ± 1.0) Gyr, we found an effective temperature of (282 ±55) K and radius of (1 .02 ±0.02) RJup for the candidate using the Sonora Flame Skimmer evolutionary models. 4. The giant planet candidate may dynami-cally disrupt terrestrial planets in the HZ of 61 Cyg AB. We populated the empirical HZs of 61 Cyg A and B with massless test particles and evolved them together with the giant planet can-didate and stellar binary (accounting for the new orbital constraints) over a 1 Myr timescale using 

N -body simulations. We found that if the giant planet candidate’s semi-major axis is ≲ 5 au, then, irrespective of the mutual inclination between the test particles and the giant planet candidate, the HZ is heavily disrupted. Between ≈5 au and 10 au, the giant planet candidate can also destabilize the HZ for misaligned orbits (40 ◦ ≲ imut ≲ 140 ◦) due to von Ziepel-Lidov-Kozai oscillations. The upcoming release of Gaia DR4 is expected to im-prove the precision of 61 Cyg AB’s proper motion by a factor of three to four 29 and include an astrometric timeseries for the two stars. A follow-up study to this paper will incorporate the above data into our analysis framework with the goal of improving the significance of the astrometric signal and parameter constraints for the candidate companion. Additional RV and/or direct imaging observations will be required to confirm or rule out the candidate given that the expected orbital period
