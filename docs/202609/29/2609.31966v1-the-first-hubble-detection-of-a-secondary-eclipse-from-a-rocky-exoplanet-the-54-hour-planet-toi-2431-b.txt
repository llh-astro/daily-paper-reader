ABSTRACT We present an HST/WFC3 G141 phase-curve observation of the ultra-short-period rocky planet TOI-2431 b and detect its secondary eclipse in the near infrared. Combined with the TESS optical eclipse, these measurements constrain the planet’s dayside brightness temperature, reflected light contribution, albedo, and heat redistribution. INTRODUCTION Lava worlds provide access to a regime of rocky plan-ets with no analog in our solar system. We define lava worlds as planets with radii Rp ≲ 1.9 R⊕ and substellar temperatures Tirr > 1500 K (B. P. Coy et al. 2026). These strongly irradiated planets are expected to be tidally locked, leading to permanently hot daysides that can sustain a permanent magma ocean. Evaporation from the molten surface can generate a rock-vapor, or silicate, atmosphere, in equilibrium with the underlying melt (L. Schaefer & B. Fegley 2009; A. L´ eger et al. 2011; Y. Miguel et al. 2011; Y. Ito et al. 2015; E. S. Kite et al. 2016; K.-H. Chao et al. 2021; O. Herbort et al. 2020; C. P. A. van Buchem et al. 2023). This creates a di-rect connection between the observable atmosphere, the molten surface, and the rocky interior. Secondary eclipses and phase curves constrain the reflective and thermal properties of exoplanets. The eclipse measures the dayside planet-to-star flux ra-tio, while the phase variation constrains the day-night brightness contrast, nightside emission, and the longitu-dinal offset of the brightest region relative to the substel-lar point. These observables probe heat redistribution and atmospheric dynamics, although weak redistribu-tion alone does not establish the absence of an atmo-sphere (e.g., V. Parmentier & I. J. M. Crossfield 2018). At optical and near-infrared wavelengths, the measured dayside flux of the hottest rocky planets can include both reflected starlight and thermal emission (e.g., M. L´ opez-Morales & S. Seager 2007; L. Malavolta et al. 2018; M. Mansfield et al. 2018). Combining TESS or other optical data, like from Kepler or CHEOPS, with WFC3/G141 observations provides a useful wavelength combination: here TESS (0.6 – 1.0 μm) probes the short-wavelength side of the planetary thermal contri-bution, where reflected light can also significantly con-tribute, whereas the WFC3/G141 (1.1 – 1.7 μm) band-pass overlaps with the thermal emission peak of hot rocky exoplanets. For example, a 2500 K blackbody

Similar combinations of optical and infrared observations have been used in the past to constrain the relative contributions of re-flected light and thermal emission (e.g., M. Mansfield et al. 2018; S. Zieba et al. 2022). At longer infrared wave-lengths, thermal emission becomes dominant. Addition-ally, infrared emission spectroscopy can probe deviations from blackbody emission, caused by a rock-vapor, or sili-cate, atmosphere. In particular, models of silicate atmo-spheres predict prominent SiO and SiO 2 features near 9 and 7 μm, respectively, which could constrain the com-position of the underlying melt and should be accessible with JWST, in particular MIRI LRS (M. Zilinskas et al. 2022, 2023; C. P. A. van Buchem et al. 2026). Existing observations indicate that lava worlds may differ from their colder, similarly sized counterparts. Whereas planets smaller than 1.9 R⊕ and with Tirr ≤

2000 K typically show temperatures and spectra consis-tent with low-albedo bare surfaces or relatively thin at-mospheres ( <10 bar) (e.g., L. Kreidberg & K. B. Steven-son 2025; L. Kreidberg et al. 2019; I. J. M. Crossfield et al. 2022; T. P. Greene et al. 2023; Q. Xue et al. 2024; M. Weiner Mansfield et al. 2024; R. Luque et al. 2025; N. H. Allen et al. 2025; P. Wachiraphan et al. 2025; M. Gillon et al. 2026; S. Zieba et al. 2026), sev-eral lava worlds observed to date have dayside brightness temperatures significantly below their zero-albedo, no-redistribution limits, namely HD 3167 b (B. P. Coy et al. 2026), TOI-431 b (C. Monaghan et al. 2025), 55 Cancri e (R. Hu et al. 2024), K2-141 b (S. Zieba et al. 2022), and TOI-561 b (J. K. Teske et al. 2025) (see Figure 4 for their irradiation temperatures). Infrared observations of 55 Cnc e with Spitzer and JWST indicate an atmo-sphere, in particular due to the low dayside temperature observed for the planet and its potentially variable day-side flux (B.-O. Demory et al. 2016; I. Angelo & R. Hu 2017; S. J. Mercier et al. 2022; R. Hu et al. 2024; J. A. Patel et al. 2024). Those low brightness temperatures indicate either effective heat transport, reflective sur-faces or clouds, or a combination of these effects. Fur-thermore, optical observations tell us more about the reflective properties of these lava worlds. For example, HST phase curve of TOI-2431 b 3the deep optical eclipse observed by Kepler for Kepler-10 b was attributed to an unusually reflective surface (N. M. Batalha et al. 2011; D. Rouan et al. 2011). Multi-wavelength observations are needed to fully characterize these planets and distinguish between various processes. TOI-2431 b is a favorable target for such follow-up observations. The planet orbits a nearby late-K dwarf every 5.4 hours, making it the shortest-period planet known with both measured radius and mass (K. H. Tas ,

et al. 2026). It has a radius of 1 .53 ± 0.03 R⊕, a mass of 6.2 ± 1.6 M⊕, and a bulk density of 9 .4 ± 2.5 g /cm 3. Its density is somewhat higher than expected for an exactly Earth-like composition but remains consistent, within the uncertainties, with a predominantly rocky planet (K. H. Tas , et al. 2026). The system age is poorly con-strained, 2 .0+9 .1 

> −1.7

Gyr (K. H. Tas , et al. 2026). If the sys-tem is in fact old, TOI-2431 b may have a more advanced stage of global interior solidification than younger lava worlds (M. Sastre et al. 2026). The planet’s irradiation or substellar temperature, Tirr , is about 2849 K, imply-ing that a substantial fraction of the planet’s dayside is expected to be molten (A. L´ eger et al. 2011; K.-H. Chao et al. 2021; M. Zilinskas et al. 2022). The appar-ent brightness of the host star ( Ks = 7.55 mag; M. F. Skrutskie et al. 2006) and the favorable planet-to-star contrast, quantified by an Emission Spectroscopy Met-ric (ESM; E. M.-R. Kempton et al. 2018) of 27 (K. H. Tas , et al. 2026), make TOI-2431 b one of the most favor-able rocky planets for thermal-emission and phase curve characterization. In this paper, we present the analysis of an HST/WFC3 G141 phase curve of TOI-2431 b and com-bine it with optical photometry from TESS. We de-tect the planet’s transit and secondary eclipse in both bandpasses and report the first secondary eclipse from a rocky exoplanet with HST. The TESS and HST ob-servations together provide complementary constraints on the planet’s dayside emission, reflected light contri-bution, and day-night contrast. A planned full-orbit JWST/MIRI LRS phase curve as part of GO 8864 (PI: L. Dang, L. Dang et al. 2025) will extend the wavelength coverage to the mid-infrared, where the emission of the planet is dominated by the thermal component over the reflected one.

OBSERVATIONS AND DATA ANALYSIS 2.1. HST Observations 

We observed a phase curve of TOI-2431 b with the Wide Field Camera 3 (WFC3) aboard the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) using the G141 grism, which covers ap-proximately 1.1 – 1.7 μm. The observations were taken on February 4, 2022 as part of General Observer (GO) Program 16660 (PI: S. Quinn; S. Quinn et al. 2021) and spanned 11 HST orbits. We used round-trip spa-tial scan mode, alternating between forward and reverse scan directions (P. McCullough & J. MacKenty 2012; D. Deming et al. 2013; H. R. Wakeford et al. 2013). Spatial scanning distributes the observed light across a larger detector area, allowing for longer integrations while avoiding saturation and therefore improving the observing efficiency. The two scan directions exhibit a direction-dependent offset, which we account for in our light curve fitting model. The observations were taken with the 512x512 subar-ray ( SQ512SUB ) and had a scan rate of 0.223 arcsec/sec, corresponding to 1.7 pixels/sec. We used the SPARS25 read-out mode with NSAMP = 7 (eight reads, includ-ing the initial zeroth read), yielding an integration time of 138.38 seconds for the full up-the-ramp integration. Each HST orbit began with an undispersed direct image of the star taken with the WFC3 F139M narrow-band filter. These images were taken to locate the spectral trace. The direct image was then followed by 13 G141 spectroscopic exposures per HST orbit. 2.2. TESS Observations 

TOI-2431 was observed by TESS (G. R. Ricker et al. 2015) in Sectors 31, 42, 43, 70, and 71, spanning Octo-ber 2020 to November 2023. We use the SPOC (J. M. Jenkins et al. 2016) light curves obtained at 120 seconds cadence in Sectors 31, 42, and 43 and at 20 seconds cadence in Sectors 70 and 71. The data were retrieved from MAST using the lightkurve package ( Lightkurve Collaboration et al. 2018). 2.3. PACMAN HST Reduction 

We downloaded the calibrated WFC3 IR Intermediate MultiAccum (IMA) files from the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST) 22 . The files were processed with version 3.7.3 of the calwf3 pipeline, which ap-plies calibrations including dark subtraction, linearity correction, and flat fielding to each individual readout (A. Pagul et al. 2024). We reduced the IMA files with the open-source pipeline PACMAN (S. Zieba & L. Kreid-berg 2022), following the reduction methods developed in previous WFC3 spatial-scan studies (e.g., L. Kreid-berg et al. 2014a,b, 2015, 2018). PACMAN has also been used in several recent WFC3 analyses (see e.g., N. Bach-mann et al. 2025; K. A. Kahle et al. 2025). We take the difference between consecutive non-destructive reads and treat each difference image as an independent subexposure. We mask pixels for which

4data-quality bits 4 and 512 are set (A. Pagul et al. 2024), subtract the background, and optimally extract each dif-ference image following K. Horne (1986). We use an ex-traction aperture extending 21 pixels beyond the upper and lower edges of the spectrum and sum the extracted difference spectra to obtain the final 1D spectrum for each exposure. We wavelength-calibrate the spectra by cross-correlating them with the G141 throughput multiplied by a smoothed PHOENIX stellar spectrum (F. Allard et al. 2012), selected to match the stellar parameters of K. H. Tas , et al. (2026) and obtained using pysynphot ( STScI Development Team 2013). Because part of the spectral trace fell outside the detector, we restrict the broad-band light curve to 1.127–1.570 μm. Additional tests used to select the background, extraction aperture, and wavelength range are described in Appendix A.1. The resulting light curve is shown in panel (c) of Figure 1. We performed a second, independent reduction with 

Iraclis (A. Tsiaras et al. 2016a, 2018). The method-ology of the pipeline is described in detail in previous works and, like PACMAN , this open-source code has been widely used, including to produce the largest population studies of exoplanet atmospheres to date (Q. Changeat et al. 2022; B. Edwards et al. 2023). Crucially, Iraclis 

is distinct from PACMAN as it starts with the raw files and performs its own calibration (dark subtraction, lin-earity correction, etc.) on them. In this reduction, we also split the “up-the-ramp samples” to get the differ-ence between each read and, for consistency with the 

PACMAN reduction, we also extract the white light curve across a wavelength range of 1.127 – 1.570 μm. Subsequently, we fit this light curve using a custom-built code. The transits and eclipses are modeled us-ing pylightcurve (A. Tsiaras et al. 2016b) while the phase curve is also represented by a cosine (i.e., Equa-tion 3). We fix the limb-darkening coefficients and use the Claret 4-coefficient model (A. Claret 2000) which we calculated using ExoTETHyS (G. Morello et al. 2020). Instead of fitting for a/R s, here we fit the stellar ra-dius ( Rs) and mass ( Ms), using Kepler’s third law to determine a. These values, along with the other system parameters ( Porb , i), are taken from K. H. Tas , et al. (2026) and Gaussian priors were used. The systematics models are identical, with the exception of the orbit-long trend (Equation A2). Instead of fitting for D(t), we fit one set of ramp parameters for the first orbit ( r1f or and 

r2f or ) and another set for the subsequent orbits ( r1oor 

and r2oor ). To explore the parameter space, we use 

pymultinest (F. Feroz et al. 2009; J. Buchner et al. 2014). In this case, we do not explore the impact of differ-ent fitting assumptions (e.g., the choice of long-term trend), but instead provide one fit as a direct compari-son. Hence, we use a linear long-term trend, assume that the orbit is circular, and assume that there is no night-side emission or hotspot offset. From this fit, we achieve a WFC3 eclipse depth of 60 ± 14 ppm, consistent with the PACMAN result. We adopt the PACMAN result as the fiducial value because the full model-selection analysis was performed for that reduction. For more information on the Iraclis reduction or the phase-folded Iraclis 

light curve, see Appendix Section B with Figure 5. 2.5. TESS Reduction 

We work with the TESS PDCSAP light curves and keep only data points flagged with QUALITY = 0. We then clean the light curves by removing 5 σ outliers using iterative sigma clipping. We also exclude transits that are cut off at sector boundaries. We then jointly fit all five sectors with a custom-written fitting framework, independent from the HST analysis, by fitting for the 

batman transit and eclipse models and a cosine phase-curve term. We use dynesty to estimate the posteriors of the fitted parameters. We account for time-correlated variability in each TESS sector using a Gaussian process with a Mat´ ern-3/2 kernel implemented with celerite2 (D. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2017). The astrophysical parameters (Porb , t 0, R p/R s, a/R s, i, f p/f s) are shared between all sectors with broad uniform priors. Each sector has an independent flux normalization constant, GP amplitude, GP correlation timescale, and an integration uncertainty rescaler. We also supersample Sectors 31, 42, and 43, which had only 120-second cadence data, by a factor of six. We do not supersample Sectors 70 and 71, which have the higher 20-second cadence data available. Allowing AmpCos to vary with broad uniform priors, and therefore allowing for nightside emission, results in AmpCos = 1 .12 +0 .44

Pseudo-2D rock vapor model 

We construct a pseudo-2D rock vapor model by split-ting the planet into rings at different angles relative to the substellar point. Similar to S. Zieba et al. (2022), we explore stellar zenith angles ranging from 0 deg (sub-stellar point) to 80 deg, where a 90 deg angle corresponds to the terminator region and assume zero incident flux there. We describe the approach in Section E of the Appendix. At each of these stellar zenith angles, we self-consistently calculate the atmospheric surface pressure induced by rock vaporization and the surface temper-ature, using an iterative approach between the climate model ARCiS (C. W. Ormel & M. Min 2019) and the vaporization tool LavAtmos (C. P. A. van Buchem et al. 2023). The magma ocean is hereby modeled as an in-finite reservoir of molten rock composed of a Bulk Sili-cate Earth (BSE) composition taken from H. Palme & H. S. C. O’Neill (2003). This choice is motivated by the fact that, although we know little about the crust compositions of rocky exoplanets (Y. Miguel et al. 2011; C. P. A. van Buchem et al. 2023), the BSE provides a well-characterized Earth-like reference composition. We note, however, that different choices for melt composi-tion can lead to different atmospheric compositions and temperature structures (F. L. Seidler et al. 2024; C. P. A. van Buchem et al. 2026). Elements included in our mod-els are hence: O, Si, Ti, Fe, Ca, Al, K, Na, and Mg. We assume that no secondary volatile atmosphere is present on the planet, motivated by the high dayside tempera-ture, pointing to weak heat redistribution. The elemental abundances and surface pressure from vaporization serve as input parameters to the atmo-spheric modeling tool ARCiS , which runs radiative trans-fer and chemistry to generate temperature structures, emission spectra, and volume mixing ratios of molecular and atomic species. The star-planet system is modeled with parameters as listed in K. H. Tas , et al. (2026). We obtain the stellar spectrum using PHOENIX models and utilize gas phase opacities from the ExoMol database for relevant vapor species derived from the BSE composition (K. L. Chubb et al. 2021a). These species are: Na, SiO, SiO 2, TiO, Fe, K, O 2, O 3, O, MgO. For Fe, Mg, Si, and Ti, we take the opacities from Janssen et al. (submit-ted) computed with pyROX (S. de Regt et al. 2025). All sources and their opacities are summarized in appendix D. We assume a zero surface albedo for TOI-2431 b as well as no additional heat flux from the interior of the planet. The chemistry in the atmosphere is pure equilib-rium chemistry and computed with GGchem (P. Woitke et al. 2018) iteratively with the temperature structure within ARCiS .With this approach, we find that in general, the tem-perature of the atmosphere and surface decreases with zenith angle, see Figure 7. As a consequence of this, the outgassing efficiency decreases as well, leading to thinner atmospheres further away from the substellar point. The maximum surface pressure is approximately 20 mbar at the substellar point. This represents an up-per limit on the equilibrium rock-vapor pressure across the modeled dayside under the adopted assumptions. The partial pressures of the most common species also decrease with increasing zenith angle as shown in Fig-ure 7. Na is the most abundant species throughout the modeled dayside, with O 2 and SiO also among the dom-inant species, and smaller abundances of TiO and Al. The models show atmospheric inversions throughout the dayside, consistent with strong absorption of stellar ra-diation by species such as Na, Fe, and TiO (M. Zilinskas et al. 2022; C. P. A. van Buchem et al. 2026). 3.5. Future JWST observations of TOI-2431 b 

TOI-2431 b will be observed in JWST GO 8864 (PI: L. Dang) with a 10.6-hour full-orbit MIRI LRS phase curve that includes two secondary eclipses. These ob-servations will extend the wavelength coverage to ap-proximately 5–12 μm, where the planetary flux is ex-pected to be dominated by thermal emission. A joint MIRI, HST, and TESS analysis will therefore provide a substantially cleaner constraint on the dayside thermal emission and, indirectly, on the reflected-light contribu-tion in the shorter-wavelength TESS and HST band-passes. In Figure 8, we extend the blackbody models and our pseudo-2D rock-vapor atmosphere model to MIRI LRS HST phase curve of TOI-2431 b 11 wavelengths. The current TESS and HST measurements are not able to distinguish the rock-vapor spectrum from a blackbody. In the mid-infrared, the rock-vapor model predicts wavelength-dependent spectral structure in the MIRI LRS range. This is due to the opacities from species such as SiO and SiO 2, which are expected to produce features near 9 and 7 μm, respectively (M. Zilin-skas et al. 2022, 2023; C. P. A. van Buchem et al. 2026). MIRI LRS observations will therefore be able to mea-sure the thermal contribution of the planet’s emission and the presence of spectral features associated with a silicate atmosphere. PACMAN HST REDUCTION A.1. PACMAN reduction choices 

Here, we report the PACMAN tests that were performed to select the final configuration, which we adopted in Section 2.3, by exploring various background subtractions and apertures. We selected the setup that minimized the RMS of the final best-fit residuals. For the background subtraction, we compared several local background boxes with an estimate based on the median flux of pixels outside the region containing the scanned spectrum. The latter resulted in slightly lower residual RMS and was therefore adopted. For the optimal extraction, we tested different aperture sizes and adopted an aperture extending 21 pixels beyond the upper and lower edges of the spectrum, which minimized the residual RMS. We note that the smoothed spectrum for the wavelength calibration is only used to determine the wavelength shift across the G141 bandpass, and that the calibration is expected to be insensitive to differences in individual stellar absorption lines. Because of an observing setup issue identified after the observations, part of the spectral trace fell outside the detector. For all fits we use dynesty and set nlive init = 2000, dlogz init = 0.0001, and nlive batch = 2000, corre-sponding to the initial number of live points, the initial stopping criterion, and the number of live points used in each subsequent dynamic batch, respectively. Our model comparison yields several initial results from the data. We define ∆ log Z = log Zmodel baseline −

log Zmodel new , such that positive values favor the baseline model. Following the empirical scale presented by R. Trotta (2008), we interpret ∆ log Z ≥ 2.5 as moderate evidence and ∆ log Z ≥ 5 as strong evidence. These thresholds, however, only provide qualitative guidelines rather than strict detection criteria (D. P. Thorngren et al. 2026). We note that other than the comparison between the different visit-long trends themselves, we perform the following model selection tests using the adopted exponential visit-long trend model. 

• We find strong evidence for the presence of the combined eclipse and phase-curve signal. Comparing a model containing a batman eclipse and a cosine phase curve model, to one without either component, we find ∆ log Z =9.7, therefore finding strong evidence in favor of the model containing the planetary emission signal. Also when using a model that only has the phase variation but no eclipse, we find ∆ log Z = 11 .2, therefore finding strong evidence in favor of the eclipse again. 

• We find no evidence for nightside emission or a phase-curve offset. The model with AmpCos = 1 and AmpSin = 0 is preferred over a model in which only AmpCos is free by ∆ log Z = 3 .1, constituting moderate evidence, and over a model in which both AmpCos and AmpSin are free by ∆ log Z = 8 .1, constituting strong evidence. • We test if the inferred phase curve amplitude depends on the adopted visit-long trend. For this we fit for AmpCos and then for the exponential, logarithmic, quadratic, and linear visit-long models. All obtained values for AmpCos are consistent with AmpCos = 1. We then test if the preferred exponential model parameters (e1 and e2) are correlated with AmpCos. We find Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.02 and -0.08, where +1 would imply perfect positive correlation and -1, perfect anticorrelation. Our results suggest no noticeable correlation between AmpCos and the exponential trend. We also check the preference between having a fixed or free AmpCos parameter. The fixed AmpCos model (AmpCos= 1) is preferred by ∆ log Z = 3 .1, 1.4, and 1 .8 for the exponential, quadratic and linear trends respectively. The logarithmic model weakly (and not significantly) favors the free AmpCos model by ∆ log Z = 1 .0. Finally, we find that the inferred eclipse depths for all visit-long trends are consistent between the fixed- and free-AmpCos fits. 

• The first orbit exhibits a stronger ramp, represented by the D(t) parameter in Equation A2. The model including this term is preferred over one with r3 = 0 by ∆ log Z = 24 .0, providing strong evidence for the additional first-orbit term. 14 

• A linear limb-darkening law is only weakly preferred over a quadratic law, parameterized following D. M. Kipping (2013), with ∆ log Z = 1 .9. The evidence difference is inconclusive; we therefore adopt the simpler linear limb-darkening law. Note that the transit depths resulting from the linear and quadratic laws are consistent with each other. Because the transit depth is not used in our following scientific discussion, the choice of limb-darkening law does not affect our conclusions. 

• A circular orbit is moderately preferred over an eccentric orbit, with ∆ log Z = 3 .0. When eccentricity is allowed to vary, it remains poorly constrained, with a 3 σ upper limit of e < 0.19. A circular orbit is also consistent with the short tidal circularization timescale expected for an ultra-short-period planet (J. N. Winn et al. 2018). We therefore set the eccentricity to zero for all following fits. 

• The exponential visit-long trend is preferred over the linear, quadratic, and logarithmic trends by ∆ log Z = 17 .1, 8.1, and 6 .8, respectively. All three provide strong evidence for the exponential trend, which we therefore adopt for the remaining fits. We also considered marginalizing the eclipse depth over the tested systematics models, following the approach of H. R. Wakeford et al. (2016). However, the exponential model carries 99.9% of the total evidence weight compared to the other systematic models, leading to a negligible change in eclipse depth from that of the adopted exponential model.

We analyze how this MIRI LRS measurement would affect the inferred energy budget. We consider two cases. First, we assume thermal emission only and fit for the heat-redistribution factor f and Bond albedo AB . Second, we additionally include reflected light and independently fit f , AB , and the geometric albedo Ag . In the latter case, we do not impose a relation between AB and Ag . Figure 9 compares the resulting posteriors with those obtained from the current TESS+HST measurements alone. For the thermal-only case, adding the simulated MIRI LRS measurement slightly narrows the allowed ranges of both 

f and AB . When reflected light is included, the current TESS+HST measurements permit a considerably broader range of f , AB , and Ag . Adding the simulated MIRI LRS measurement strongly reduces this parameter space and recovers the low-albedo, inefficient-redistribution region used to generate the simulated measurement. For example, the width of the Ag posterior decreases by approximately a factor of two.
