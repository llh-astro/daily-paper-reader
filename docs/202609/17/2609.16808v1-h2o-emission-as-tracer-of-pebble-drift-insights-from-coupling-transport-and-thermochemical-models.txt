ABSTRACT 

Context. The composition of the inner regions of protoplanetary disks is known to change with time due to the delivery of icy grains. Cold H 2O emission is often hypothesized to be a tracer of this pebble drift, as sublimating H 2O ices may be mixed vertically upwards into the surface layers of the disk, where this cold gas may be observed with JWST. However, it is unclear to what extent processes such as photodissociation or the co-delivery of dust, which would obscure the delivered H 2O, may impede such detections. 

Aims. The transport of gas and dust through a disk is often considered with 1D models, whereas more complex 2D models that include a full chemical network are often static in nature. We aim to combine the two modeling approaches to obtain an improved, 2D view of transport in disks, to better understand how these processes can be traced by, for example, H 2O emission as seen with JWST-MIRI. 

Methods. We combine the 1D transport code DiscEvolution with the 2D thermochemical code DALI to create several grids of models in which the gas-phase abundances, dust properties, or both are varied according to the transport model. Several snapshots from a DiscEvolution are taken and post-processed in DALI, considering times from 10 4 to 4 × 10 6 yr. Delivered abundances are mixed up vertically instantaneously, after which the chemistry is run for 10 3 yr. We consider one scenario in which a tra ffi c jam is present inside the H 2O snowline, allowing dust to pile up there, and one scenario in which this tra ffi c jam is not present. 

Results. The transport of both gas and dust leads to significant temperature changes within the disk, which strongly influence line fluxes and ratios. When a tra ffi c jam is present, the delivery of H 2O can indeed proceed unnoticed due to the co-delivery of dust, as the retrieved H 2O mass from LTE slab fits does not change. In addition, the relative strength of cold H 2O lines is not found to be sensitive to this delivery of gas-phase H 2O: it would need to rapidly di ff use outwards and upwards in the disk after sublimation to be observed as such with JWST-MIRI. Instead, the cold H 2O lines can be greatly enhanced by the delivery of only dust to the inner disk when a tra ffi c jam is present, due to the preferential obscuration of the warm and hot H 2O reservoirs by this dust pile-up. Therefore, one can create a spectrum with strong cold H 2O emission (a signature hypothesized to trace the delivery of H 2O ices across the snowline) solely through the delivery of dust rather than H 2O. The case of CO 2/H2O as a tracer is briefly discussed as well, but without any clear trend found. 

Conclusions. Recent work has used the 1500 /6000 K H 2O line ratio as a proxy to determine the pebble mass flux crossing the H 2Osnowline, but we find that the use of this line ratio for this purpose warrants some caution. The line ratio is rather sensitive to the temperature and dust distribution of the disk, and is often influenced not only by the cold H 2O mass, but also by the hot H 2O mass. Both of these can change considerably during the evolution of the disk, which can introduce complexities and trends as a function of time that do not match the true evolution of the pebble flux with time. 

Key words. protoplanetary disks – stars: variables: T Tauri, Herbig Ae /Be – infrared: general – astrochemistry 

1. Introduction 

Young, Earth-like planets are thought to form primarily in the inner regions ( <10 au) of protoplanetary disks, and therefore the chemical composition of their atmospheres and that of the disk should be closely linked (Dawson & Johnson 2018; Öberg & Bergin 2021). However, the composition of the inner disk is ex-pected not to be static with time. Dust grains experience inward radial drift (Weidenschilling 1977), and they carry ices that des-orb into the gas-phase at their respective ice lines. Therefore, the chemical make-up of the gas in the inner regions of the disk will change with time. In particular, this has been shown to a ff ect the carbon-to-oxygen (C /O) ratio of the gas, as well as the O /H and C/H ratios (e.g. Booth et al. 2017; Mah et al. 2023; Sellek & van Dishoeck 2025; Molyarova et al. 2026). The enhancement of the inner disk gas by sublimating ices occurs approximately in a sequence, set by the respective bind-ing energies of the ices. H 2O ice reaches the inner disk first, as it has a relatively high binding energy and therefore a snowline located close to the star (e.g. Minissale et al. 2022). When this ice desorbs in the inner disk, it significantly increases the O /Habundance and thereby lowers the C /O ratio. This is followed by the arrival of CO 2 ices, which have a slightly lower binding energy and therefore travel slightly farther from their ice line to reach the inner disk. The CO 2 ices cause the C /O ratio to start to rise slightly. Over time, the oxygen-rich gas will drain from the inner disk onto the central star, after which it will gradually be replenished instead with advected carbon-rich gas that contains mainly CO and CH 4, two major ice components with a low bind-ing energy. Hence, at late times, the C /O ratio will continue to rise further, potentially even reaching values above unity (Mah et al. 2023; Sellek & van Dishoeck 2025). The inner regions of the protoplanetary disk are best stud-ied in the infrared (IR), as their warm temperatures allow for

the excitation of molecular ro-vibrational lines in the mid-IR from species such as H 2O, as well as CO 2, CO, C 2H2 and HCN (e.g. Pontoppidan et al. 2014). The Mid-InfraRed Instrument on board the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST-MIRI; Rieke et al. 2015; Wright et al. 2015, 2023) has allowed for the chem-ical composition of these regions to be characterized in further detail than before (see, e.g. Henning et al. 2024; van Dishoeck et al. 2023; Arulanantham et al. 2025), with higher sensitivity and spectral resolution than previous space-based instruments. The increased spectral resolution has allowed for the H 2O emis-sion in disks to be characterized in greater detail than before, where lines of di ff erent upper level energies Eup can now be used to identify distinct temperature reservoirs (see, e.g. Gasman et al. 2023; Temmink et al. 2024, 2025; Romero-Mirza et al. 2024; Banzatti et al. 2023, 2025). Additionally, the increased sensitiv-ity has motivated the hunt for less-abundant species and rare iso-topologues, including a tentative detection of H 18 2O (Salyk et al. 2026). One major question that recent works have been trying to answer is whether the aforementioned transport of icy pebbles into the inner disk leaves traces in the IR emission coming from this region. In particular, the H 2O emission has been studied ex-tensively, with recent works proposing that cold ( Eup ≲ 2000 K) H 2O lines may hold some information about the mass flux of icy pebbles crossing the H 2O snowline (Banzatti et al. 2023, 2025; Romero-Mirza et al. 2024; Krijt et al. 2025). This is moti-vated by an empirical correlation between the cold H 2O strength and the dust disk size as measured by ALMA, where smaller, more compact disks (which may be caused by e ffi cient radial drift) show stronger cold H 2O. LTE slab modeling suggests that these lines trace a cold reservoir with temperatures of ∼200 K, close to the sublimation temperature of H 2O. When a large flux of icy pebbles crosses the snowline, the resulting sublimation has been hypothesized to enhance this cold reservoir, more than the warmer H 2O reservoirs that trace temperatures >400 K. This is then thought to potentially translate into an enhancement in flux of cold, Eup ∼ 1500 K H 2O lines with respect to warmer, 

Eup ≳ 3000 K lines (e.g., Banzatti et al. 2023; Temmink et al. 2024, 2025; Vlasblom et al. 2025b). However, the feasibility of observing the delivery of H 2Ointo the inner disk by pebble drift has also been called into ques-tion by recent modeling work. Sellek et al. (2025) and Houge et al. (2025) point out that the delivery of gas-phase H 2O comes paired with the delivery of dust grains, which increase the dust opacity in the IR emitting layers of the disk. Therefore, the H 2Ogas could essentially be “smuggled unnoticed” into the inner disk, as the increase in column density by sublimating ices and the increase in dust opacity by dust grains may very well (par-tially) cancel one another out. Additionally, Houge et al. (2025) and Vlasblom et al. (2025b) demonstrate the importance of vertical mixing and pho-todissociation in the IR-emitting layers of the disk. Vlasblom et al. (2025b) demonstrate using 2D thermochemical models how the cold H 2O lines thought to trace pebble drift are emit-ted from a layer above the snow surface at radii beyond 1 au (i.e., larger radii than the 0.5 au midplane snowline). Thus, these lines do not trace the midplane of the disk, the region where peb-ble drift truly occurs. However, Vlasblom et al. (2025b) demon-strate that disk chemistry alone is incapable of reproducing typ-ical observed line ratios; the H 2O in this layer is photodissoci-ated rapidly, yet its abundance needs to be enhanced by several orders of magnitude. Potentially, this could be achieved through the sublimation of H 2O gas from icy pebbles followed by quickly mixing upwards vertically (and also radially outwards), thus es-tablishing a correlation of the line strength with the pebble flux. However, photodissociation timescales in this surface layer are fast ( ≲1 yr; e.g. Xu et al. 2019; Kanwar et al. 2024), and there-fore whether this enhancement by sublimating ices can last long enough to be observed could be debated. However, if a continu-ous supply is present and a su ffi cient column has built up, H 2OUV-shielding may help (Bethell & Bergin 2009; Bosman et al. 2022a). Therefore, the transport of gas and icy dust grains in proto-planetary disks should be considered in more than just the in-ward radial direction, as processes such as photodissociation, UV shielding, and chemical reactions may be important, espe-cially in the surface layers which are observed with JWST. How-ever, current work typically considers the transport of gas and dust in disk models only in 1D (e.g. Sellek et al. 2025; Mah et al. 2023; Houge et al. 2025). On the other hand, more complex models that self-consistently calculate the 2D chemical abun-dances in the disk using a full chemical network are typically static in nature and do not consider mixing or changing abun-dances with time (e.g. Vlasblom et al. 2025b; Arabhavi et al. 2026). Some e ff orts have been made to combine the two. Most notably, Greenwood et al. (2019) combined the thermochemical code ProDiMo (Woitke et al. 2009, 2024) with the dust evolution model two-pop-py (Birnstiel et al. 2017) to demonstrate how IR fluxes of commonly detected species with Spitzer changed with time as the dust in the disk evolved. However, they consid-ered only the changes this would bring in the dust composition of the disk, not the elemental composition. Turbulent mixing pro-cesses have been implemented into chemical models before (Se-menov et al. 2006; Semenov & Wiebe 2011), and more recently, Woitke et al. (2022) implemented vertical mixing and di ff usion processes into ProDiMo , but did not link this to any physical model of dust and gas transport. In this work, we present a study that combines the 1D transport code DiscEvolution with the 2D thermochemical code DALI such that the e ff ects of both dust and gas transport can be studied with further complexity. In Sect. 2, we present both codes and our approach to coupling them. In Sect. 3, the e ff ects of gas and dust transport on the abundance and emission of H 2Ois presented. In Sect. 4, we discuss the implications for the use of cold H 2O lines as a tracer of pebble drift. We also discuss whether the emission from CO 2, another prominent ice species, may give further insights. Sect. 5 summarizes our main conclu-sions. 

2. Methods 

This work combines the 1D transport code DiscEvolution (Booth et al. 2017; Sellek et al. 2025; Booth et al. 2026) and the 2D ther-mochemical code DALI (Bruderer et al. 2012; Bruderer 2013) to obtain a 2D view of the e ff ects of gas and dust transport to the inner disk. In this section, we introduce both codes and describe how they are linked. 

> 2.1. 1D transport code DiscEvolution

The advection of gas and transport of (icy) dust is modeled using DiscEvolution. The code is set up as described in Sellek et al. (2025), so we refer the reader to that work for the full description and only summarize the most important details here. DiscEvolution adopts the two-population dust model from Birnstiel et al. (2012). The dust consists of a population of small grains, which is assumed to have a fixed size, and follows the 

> Article number, page 2 of 24 Marissa Vlasblom et al.: H 2O emission as tracer of pebble drift

motion of the gas. The population of large grains, on the other hand, is allowed to grow in size up to either a fragmentation barrier, when grains collide at high-enough velocities such that they can no longer grow larger e ffi ciently, or up to a drift barrier, when grains are removed from the disk by radial drift faster than they can grow. This latter population can thus also dynamically decouple from the gas once they grow large enough. The frac-tion of dust mass in each population is given by fsmall and flarge ,which are bench-marked against fragmentation-coagulation sim-ulations (Birnstiel et al. 2012). These suggest that flarge = 0.75 when growth is fragmentation-limited, and flarge = 0.97 when growth is drift-limited. For this work, we run two models with DiscEvolution. The first model is equal to Scenario 1 presented in Sellek et al. (2025). In this model, both the dust and gas evolve according to a viscosity of α = 10 −3, and a ‘tra ffi c jam’ is present: icy grains are assumed to have a higher fragmentation velocity than bare grains ( uf,dust = 1 m s −1 and uf,ice = 10 m s −1). This leads to a pile-up of dust inside the H 2O snowline (see also Pinilla et al. 2016), as grains in this region will have a lower Stokes number and a lower velocity, thus becoming more coupled to the gas. We also run a model where this tra ffi c jam e ff ect is not present. In this case, both the gas and dust still evolve according to a vis-cosity of α = 10 −3, but the fragmentation velocity is now set to 

uf,ice = 10 m s −1 throughout the entire disk. This value is higher than recent laboratory experiments suggest (e.g. Gundlach et al. 2018; Musiolik & Wurm 2019), however this allows us to re-tain the same degree of coupling between the gas and dust in the outer disk for the same evolutionary timescales of the gas. This ensures that radial drift, and the subsequent sublimation of ices at the snowlines, still occurs at the same rate and is una ff ected by this change. In Appendix B, Fig. B.1 presents the combined gas- and ice-phase abundances of the four major species we evolve in this work: H 2O, CO 2, CH 4, and CO, as a function of disk radius at several time steps. This demonstrates how the (gas-phase) abun-dance of each species first builds up locally just within its snow-line due to ice sublimation, after which this gas is viscously advected inwards leading to a uniform enhancement within its snowline. Fig. B.1 also presents the dust surface density at sev-eral time steps for the two scenarios (with and without a tra ffi cjam) considered in this work. 

2.2. 2D Thermochemical code DALI 

The thermochemical code DALI consists of three main steps, first calculating the local UV and IR radiation field and dust tem-perature at all locations in the disk given an input gas and dust density structure. Then, the code self-consistently calculates the gas temperature, chemical abundances of all species in the chem-ical network, and non-LTE excitation for several specified atoms and molecules (which is then used in the heating and cooling bal-ance). Finally, a raytracing tool can be used to obtain line fluxes, spectra and spectral image cubes. All DALI models have a general setup similar to those pre-sented in Vlasblom et al. (2024, 2025b), as they are based on the model for the disk around K5 star AS 209 (see Zhang et al. 2021; Bosman et al. 2022a). Hence, our models use the observed stellar spectrum from those works as input. This spectrum has an eff ective temperature of 4300 K and a bolometric luminosity of 1.4 L⊙. It also contains an FUV component due to its accretion, with an FUV luminosity of 8 × 10 −3 L⊙, which corresponds to a UV-excess produced by an accretion rate between 10 −8 − 10 −9

M⊙ yr −1.The output from our DiscEvolution simulations is then trans-ferred to DALI. This is done by taking the DiscEvolution output at several time steps and transforming each snapshot from the code into a single DALI model. We consider 6 logarithmically spaced time steps between 10 4 and 4 × 10 6 years (see colored lines in Fig. B.1), as well as the initial t = 0 condition (black line in Fig. B.1). The DiscEvolution models start with an initial disk mass of 0.028 M ⊙, which has decreased to 0.021 M ⊙ due to viscous evolution by the latest timestep we consider. However, since we aim to isolate the e ff ects of changing chemical abun-dances and dust properties, and this mass decrease is relatively small, we fix the total disk mass in all DALI models to 0.021 M⊙.We transfer two kinds of output from DiscEvolution to DALI: the chemical abundances and the dust properties. To test the e ff ects of each separately, we create five grids of models (each grid consisting of 7 models corresponding to our 7 time steps). We create one grid using a fiducial DALI setup for the dust properties (see, e.g. Vlasblom et al. 2025b), where the chemical abundances are varied according to the DiscEvolution output. We also create two grids where the chemical abundances are kept constant at a fiducial value, and the dust properties are varied according to our two DiscEvolution simulations (with and without a tra ffi c jam). Finally, we create two grids in which both quantities are varied simultaneously. A summary of all grids pre-sented in this work and their properties is shown in Table 1. In DALI, the dust distribution is modeled using two popula-tions, similar to DiscEvolution. The small dust population fol-lows the same vertical distribution as the gas (Miotello et al. 2016), and has an MRN size distribution (Mathis et al. 1977) with grain sizes between 5 nm and 1 μm. The large dust popula-tion has an MRN size distribution with grain sizes between 5 nm and 1 mm, and has a reduced scale height compared to the small dust population. Though slight di ff erences may exist, we assume that the two dust populations from the two codes directly map to one another. Each dust population has its own fixed dust opacity curve for a standard ISM dust composition (consistent with An-drews et al. 2011; Bruderer et al. 2012). These are kept the same for all models presented in this work, and thus do not account for changes in dust properties with time. We assume a gas sur-face density profile for a viscously evolving disk (Lynden-Bell & Pringle 1974; Hartmann et al. 1998)

Finally, the abundances from H 2O, CO 2, CH 4 and CO as pre-dicted by DiscEvolution (Fig. B.1) are taken as the initial con-ditions for the second step of the code: the chemistry and ther-mal balance. At each radius, the output abundance from Dis-cEvolution is taken, with this same abundance being used for all cells in the vertical direction. The vertical mixing in the disk is assumed to be very e ffi cient and instantaneous. With these ini-tial conditions, the chemistry is run time-dependently for 1000 years. This time was chosen as it is significantly shorter than the time between our DiscEvolution snapshots, meaning that the transport of dust and gas will not have significantly changed the composition or dust distribution of the disk in the meantime. Ad-ditionally, it is long enough for the chemistry in the surface lay-ers to have reached steady-state. Test calculations have shown that this is long enough for the warm, observable surface lay-ers: the main formation reactions of H 2O (discussed in Sect. 3.1) have timescales of only a few years in this layer of the disk, and the photodissociation timescale is equally short in regions where H2O cannot self-shield against the UV radiation (see Vlasblom et al. 2025b). For the remaining atomic species in our chemi-cal network, as well as for the grids in which the abundances are not taken from DiscEvolution, the chemistry for each model is instead started with all species (including hydrogen) in their atomic forms, with an abundance that is constant throughout the disk (see Bosman et al. 2022b; Vlasblom et al. 2024), after which the chemistry is run for 10 3 yr. The chemical network used in this work is from Miotello et al. (2016), a network based on UMIST06 (Woodall et al. 2007) that includes isotope chemistry through the implementa-tion of isotope-selective photodissociation and fractionation re-actions (see Miotello et al. 2014). We amend this network with the changes made in Bosman et al. (2022a,b), which include the eff ects of H 2O UV shielding and more e ffi cient H 2 formation at high temperatures through the inclusion of several three-body re-actions. To generate synthetic spectra of H 2O, 12 CO 2, and 13 CO 2,DALI’s “fast ray-tracer” (see Bosman et al. 2017, Appendix B) is used, assuming a distance of 121 pc and a face-on orientation. The excitation of all molecules was calculated in non-LTE. The molecular data file for H 2O is retrieved from the Leiden Atomic and Molecular Database (LAMDA), including levels with ener-gies up to 7200 K (Tennyson et al. 2001). The line transitions are obtained from the BT2 list (Barber et al. 2006) and the colli-sional rate coe ffi cients come from Faure & Josselin (2008). For CO 2 and its isotopologues, we use the molecular data compiled by Bosman et al. (2017), who retrieve the energy levels, line po-sitions and line strengths from the HITRAN database (Rothman et al. 2013). For 12 CO 2 and 13 CO 2, collisional rate coe ffi cients based on Allen et al. (1980), Nevdakh et al. (2003), and Jacobs et al. (1975) are used. The generated spectra are convolved to

> 2.3. Slab retrievals

Finally, we follow the methods presented in Vlasblom et al. (2025b) and perform retrievals with LTE slab models on our syn-thetic H 2O and CO 2 spectra. We refer the reader to that work for the full details. In summary, the slab models contain three free parameters: the molecular excitation temperature Tex (usu-ally close to the kinetic gas temperature T ), the column density 

N, and the emitting area A (e.g. Tabone et al. 2023; Kamp et al. 2023). Assuming A = πR2eq , the emitting area can be converted to an equivalent slab radius ( Req ). This may correspond to the true emitting radius of the emission, but the emission can also originate from an annulus further out in the disk instead. The emission of CO 2 and its isotopologues are fit with a sin-gle model, as their emission features span a narrow wavelength range. The best-fit model was obtained from a grid of slab mod-els using a χ2 method. H2O, on the other hand, has emission features spanning over a broad range in wavelength and upper level energy Eup , and therefore requires a slightly more sophisticated setup. Follow-ing Temmink et al. (2024), we fit the entire H 2O spectrum from 10-28 μm using three components, where the flux from three di ff erent slab models with decreasing temperature and increas-ing emitting area is combined, and the best fit is found using the Monte Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) implementation em-cee (Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013). We select our fitting win-dows centered on several isolated lines across the wavelength range, which were identified by Banzatti et al. (2025) in their Tables 5 and 6. The e ff ects of mutual shielding of adjacent lines (as done in, e.g., Tabone et al. 2023) are not accounted for in any of our fits, as this is also not accounted for when generating our synthetic DALI spectra. 

3. Results 

> 3.1. Gas temperature structure

Fig. 2 presents the gas temperature in a vertical cut through the disk at 0.2 au (a radius right within the emitting region of warm H2O) for all 5 grids of models at all time steps. Fig. B.4 presents the full 2D gas temperature maps for the two grids with varied abundances and dust (the two rightmost panels of Fig. 2). Con-sidering first the grid in which only the abundances are varied (leftmost panel of Fig. 2), it is clear that the temperature remains relatively constant throughout the di ff erent time steps, with the exception of a small temperature increase around Z/R = 0.09-0.10. This layer in the disk is precisely co-located with the IR emitting area of H 2O (which is indicated in Fig. 2 by the bold regions of the lines). This layer of the disk has been shown to be particularly sensitive to changes in the thermochemistry (see also Woitke et al. 2018; Bosman et al. 2022b; Vlasblom et al. 2024). Namely, it represents the transition from the hot, atomic layer where the gas and dust temperatures are strongly decoupled into the deeper molecular layer where the gas and dust become coupled. In the surface of the disk, the oxygen content of the disk ex-ists primarily in its atomic form due to the photodissociation of H2O. When moving down towards the midplane, this oxygen is gradually transformed from its atomic form in the upper layers first into OH via the O + H2 −−−→ OH + H reaction, and then into H 2O via the OH + H2 −−−→ H2O + H reaction. Around 0.1 Myr in our DiscEvolution simulations, the influx of H 2O, and thereby the influx of oxygen, into the inner regions of the disk has reached its peak (see yellow line in Fig. B.1). As a result, the O + H2 −−−→ OH + H and OH + H2 −−−→ H2O + H con-sume much more of the H 2 in this transition layer than at other time steps, replacing it with atomic H and thereby shifting the H/H2 transition deeper into the disk. At the same time, this extra atomic H can still reform into H 2 via its formation route on the dust grains, which releases heat into the gas (Cazaux & Tielens 2002, 2004). These e ff ects combined lead to the small tempera-ture increase that is observed at 0.1 Myr. By contrast, however, the temperature changes are much larger in the other four grids in which the dust properties are varied between the di ff erent time steps. In the two grids where a tra ffi c jam is present (Fig. 2; top row, middle and right panels), the temperature is seen to first decrease (compare red and yellow lines) due to the build-up of dust in the inner regions of the disk caused by radial drift (see also Fig. 1). As Tgas > Tdust in this layer, this addition of dust leads to an increase in the thermal accommodation between the dust and the gas, therefore lower-ing the gas temperature. At later times, the temperature increases again (compare green and blue lines) due to the removal of dust from the disk by accretion onto the star. In the two grids without a tra ffi c jam (bottom row, middle and right panels), this initial build-up of dust does not occur, and therefore the temperature does not change much at early times either, since not much dust has been removed by drift yet. At late times, once the outer disk runs out of pebbles with which to resupply the inner disk, the lack of a tra ffi c jam in the inner disk leads to very e ffi cient re-moval of dust from the disk (perhaps unrealistically so, since no dust traps are assumed to be present in our models). This causes the temperature to increase very strongly beyond 1 Myr in these models. Comparing the grids in which only the dust is varied to the grids in which both the dust and the abundances are varied, it is clear that the dust properties of the disk have a much larger e ff ect on the gas temperature structure than the chemistry does. Still, the e ff ects do combine, as seen by the small temperature increase seen at early times (0.1 Myr) in the bottom right panel of Fig. 2. These e ff ects also clearly change the depth and temperature from which the H 2O emits in the disk. When a dust pile-up cools the surface layers of the disk, the H 2O emits from further up in the disk, and the reverse is true when the surface layers are heated by a depletion of dust. We note that the H 2O emitting region in the 4 Myr model (purple line) of the bottom-right panel is not indicated in the figure. This is due to the strong increase in temperature leading to the H 2O emitting region moving to larger radii than 0.2 au.

3.3. Cold H 2O diagnostics 

As the delivery of ices to the inner disk has been often proposed to be observable through tracers of the cold H 2O reservoir, it is interesting to consider how these behave in our model grids. In Fig. 5, four lines of interest between 23.7 and 24 μm are shown, which are a key diagnostic for cold H 2O. Two of these lines (the second and fourth lines) have an Eup of ∼3000 K, whereas the other two lines (the first and third lines) have a lower Eup of 

∼1500 K. The relative strength of these four lines is very sen-sitive to the temperature of the gas (e.g., Banzatti et al. 2023, 2025; Temmink et al. 2024). When the cold, ∼200 K H 2O reser-voir contributes strongly to the total flux (e.g. due to a large in-flux of sublimated H 2O ices near the snowline), the 1500 K lines are expected to be bright compared to the 3000 K lines. Fig. 5 presents the relative strength of this quartet of lines, normalized to the flux of the 8 3,6 – 7 0,7 line ( Eup = 1447 K; blue shaded region). Interestingly, the grid in which only the abun-dances are varied (and which should therefore most clearly show the e ff ects of H 2O delivery by pebble drift) does not show any clear variation in this cold H 2O line ratio (leftmost panel). The di ff erence in line ratio is on the order of ≲10%, which corre-sponds to the approximate precision with which individual line fluxes can usually be determined in JWST-MIRI observations at these wavelengths (see, e.g., Banzatti et al. 2025; Arulanantham et al. 2025). Therefore, these variations would not be detectable above typical noise levels. The lack of variation is not surprising, however, as Vlasblom et al. (2025b) demonstrated that the strength of the 1500 K lines is primarily sensitive to a H 2O reservoir further out in the disk (> 1 au), above the snow surface. In our models, however, the midplane H 2O snowline is located at ∼0.5 au, and any enhance-ment in H 2O by drift is only mixed up vertically, thereby staying within 0.5 au. Therefore, the H 2O abundance in this reservoir traced by the cold H 2O lines does not change much in our mod-els, and the relative strength of the 1500 and 3000 K lines also remains unchanged. This is also why the retrieved mass of the cold component showed the smallest changes with time (see Fig. 4). Whereas this is caused by our current modeling assumptions, it also clearly demonstrates what is needed for the H 2O deliv-ered by drift to truly be observed with cold H 2O tracers with

This is to be expected given the lack of a visible change in the cold lines at long wavelengths (Fig. 5). This coincides with the peak of the H 2O influx into the inner disk, and the subse-quent increase in inner disk temperature is likely what causes this change. Hence, the delivery of H 2O into the inner disk is being traced by the hot and warm H 2O lines in this case, rather than the cold lines. When the dust properties of the disk are varied, a stark di ff er-ence between the scenarios with and without a tra ffi c jam is once again observed. When a tra ffi c jam is present, both line ratios in-crease at early times as the dust piles up, and decrease once the dust drains from the disk, with the data points thus moving along a diagonal line up and down in the diagram. In Vlasblom et al. (2025b), a similar trend was identified and linked to an increas-ing or decreasing gas /dust ratio. When the dust piles up at early times, the gas /dust ratio decreases strongly in the inner disk. This most strongly a ff ects the hottest lines that trace the innermost regions, and a ff ects the cold lines that trace furthest out in the disk the least. Hence, both the 3600 /6000 K and 1500 /3600 K line ratios increase, and then decrease again when the dust clears from the inner disk and the gas /dust ratio increases again. When no tra ffi c jam is present, however, no initial decrease in gas /dust ratio occurs, and only the increasing gas /dust with the gradual clearing of dust from the disk remains. Therefore, both line ra-tios decrease over time. This behavior becomes slightly more complicated once the combined e ff ect of dust and gas transport is accounted for. The overall trends described above still hold globally, but the increase in temperature due to the influx of H 2O at 0.1 Myr also seems to have an e ff ect in temporarily decreasing the 3600 /6000 K ratio, making the hot reservoir more prominent than it would be if only a dust pile-up were (or were not) present.

Finally, we also note that, when comparing these line ratios in our models directly to the JWST data in Fig. 6, our model data points generally lie slightly to the right compared to the data for most time steps. These sources do not di ff er significantly from our models in, for example, spectral type or luminosity, so this is not likely to be the cause of this o ff set. Instead, this potentially indicates a lack of hot H 2O emission in our models, which was also identified in the models by Vlasblom et al. (2025b). Still, we stress that the trends observed between the models remain informative, even if there is some complexity that is not yet captured by our models. 

4. Discussion 

> 4.1. Cold H 2O emission as a tracer of the pebble mass flux
> 4.1.1. General considerations

Cold H 2O emission is often associated with the delivery of H 2Oto the inner disk by pebble drift (e.g. Romero-Mirza et al. 2024; Banzatti et al. 2023; Krijt et al. 2025). However, this connection may not be entirely straightforward. When only the gas-phase abundances in the disk are varied, no significant enhancement in cold H 2O emission is seen concurrently with the delivery of gas-phase H 2O to the inner disk. After all, the reservoir from which the cold H 2O lines emit (a layer above the snow surface beyond 1 au, as identified in Vlasblom et al. 2025b) is not enhanced in abundance in our modeling approach, indicating that a more detailed treatment of vertical mixing including outward di ff usion may be important for future work. Instead, the strength of the cold H 2O emission is most strongly impacted by the changes in dust properties when a tra ffi c jam is present. The delivery of gas-phase H 2O is technically not necessary to produce a spectrum with strong cold H 2O, and it is the delivery of dust rather than gas that is being traced by the cold H 2O lines in this case. Since dust likely carries H 2O ice, it is plausible to assume that the strength of the cold emission will still hold some relation to the e ffi ciency of radial drift, and thereby the icy pebble flux reaching the inner disk. Recent work by Romero-Mirza et al. (2024) and Krijt et al. (2025) has proposed to link the strength of cold H 2O emission to a pebble flux crossing the H 2O snowline. In Romero-Mirza et al. At later times, however, the 1500 /6000 K line ratio overestimates the true pebble flux by several orders of magnitude in most grids. We do note that, for the two mod-els with varied dust properties without a tra ffi c jam (light and dark blue solid lines), at 4 Myr the 1500 /6000 K line ratio either very closely approaches A = 1.75 (in the case of the dust-only model) or becomes smaller than this value (in the case of the dust +abundances model). In these cases, the derived cold H 2Omass and pebble flux will become very small or even uncon-strained in the case of the dust +abundance model. We also note that there is no di ff erence in pebble flux between the DiscEvolu-tion simulations with and without a tra ffi c jam. After all, we are measuring the dust mass flux passing through the H 2O snowline at 0.5 au, exterior to which the coupling of the gas and dust (and therefore the drift speeds) is kept exactly the same in both mod-els.

4.1.3. Late times ( ≥1 Myr) 

At late times ( ≥1 Myr; the age range at which T Tauri disks are typically observed), almost all models predict a pebble flux that is much larger than the true value, by up to ∼3 orders of magnitude. Only the grids with varied dust without a tra ffi c jam (light and dark blue solid lines) show a strong decrease in the 1500 /6000 K line ratio at late times. While this is technically indeed reflective of the fact that these disks will have lost a lot of their dust mass due to drift at this point, the decreasing line ratio is once again mainly set by changes in the disk’s optical depth and temperature. At late times, the decreasing dust optical depth leads to an increase in retrieved H 2O mass in both the cold and hot components. Consequently, the disk also heats up strongly, which leads the emitting area of both lines increasing sharply. However, this e ff ect is much stronger for the 6000 K line, as its emitting area is seen to increase by two orders of magnitude, compared with an increase of only a factor few for the 1500 K line. Therefore, the line ratio decreases sharply. When comparing these derived pebble fluxes to those de-rived from JWST-MIRI observations (sample from Krijt et al. 2025 shown in gray diamonds in Fig. 7), a similar phenomenon is observed. Most observed line ratios match our models more closely than the DiscEvolution pebble flux. Additionally, the disks which are found to have a lower predicted pebble flux are ones that host close-in gaps, which are expected to block a large part of the pebble flow. Krijt et al. (2025) demonstrate that full disks, or disks with gaps located further out, generally have a larger 1500 /6000 K line ratio (and thereby predicted pebble flux). Since our models do not include a gap, these disks are pre-cisely the ones which make for the most fair comparison to our work. Hence, there seems to be an inherent mismatch between the predicted pebble flux from the 1500 /6000 K line and the true pebble flux at late times. These findings are also consistent with recent results from Xie et al. (2026), who studied disks in the Upper Scorpius region (age ∼5 Myr) with JWST-MIRI. When comparing the 1500 /6000 K line ratio between the older Upper Scorpius disks and younger disks (age ∼1-3 Myr) from the JDISCS survey, they find no meaningful di ff erence. This is in line with our model predic-tions. Additionally, they note that two of their disks with low 1500 /6000 K ratios are very H 2O-rich, and have high accretion luminosities. Thus, if their H 2O-rich spectra are indeed reflec-tive of a high pebble flux, this lends further credence to the idea that changes in the hot H 2O mass may skew the line ratio to-

4.2. Implications for cold H 2O emission as a tracer of drift 

The mismatch between the true and predicted pebble fluxes at late times warrants some further consideration, to evaluate whether any of the underlying equations (Eqs. 4 and 5) contain limitations that could be modified to account for further com-plexities revealed by this work. We consider here a few di ff erent eff ects that may be important to consider in future work. First, Eq. 4 accounts for the e ff ects of optical depth on the ob-servable cold H 2O mass through the factor η. However, we have demonstrated that the dust optical depth of the disk changes a lot due to the changing dust distribution of the disk with time, and thus this factor is likely not constant with time. Naturally, the value of η as a function of time then relates back directly to the pebble mass flux, as this will determine how fast a disk will lose its dust mass, or how long it may retain it. In fact, η (and thus τ)should depend positively on ˙Mpeb . This will weaken the depen-dence of Mcold on ˙Mpeb , which then also predicts the existence of a ‘worst case scenario’ where there is no dependence of Mcold on ˙Mpeb at all anymore, and the H 2O is smuggled unnoticed. Such a dependence may therefore be important to consider in future work. Second, Eq. 4 assumes all observed cold H 2O to be a prod-uct of sublimation. However, this neglects the e ff ects of the gas-phase chemistry in the disk. The DiscEvolution models produce a strong depletion in gas-phase H 2O in the inner disk at the lat-est time considered in our grids, down to a fractional abundance of ∼ 10 −6 at 4 Myr (see Fig. B.1, purple line in top left panel). However, the gas-phase H 2O abundance in the IR emitting layer of our corresponding DALI models does not deplete this far, in-stead remaining around a fractional abundance of ∼ 10 −4. This is caused by the fact that the gas-phase chemistry in the inner disk will rapidly produce H 2O as long as oxygen is present in some form. While the H 2O abundance in the inner disk decreases strongly at late times in the DiscEvolution models, CO gas from the outer disk replaces it. Therefore, the total amount of oxygen in the inner disk does not decrease as strongly. We demonstrate this in Fig. 7 by showing the mass flux of oxygen (gas and ice) through the H 2O snowline as a function of time (red dotted line). This oxygen mass flux is much more constant with time than the pebble mass flux, as it has only decreased by roughly one order of magnitude at 4 Myr, whereas the pebble mass flux has decreased by nearly three orders of magnitude. Therefore, some background level of cold H 2O can remain present in the inner disk due to gas-phase chemistry, even when the inflow from icy pebbles has ceased almost entirely. Fig. 3 provides more evidence for this through the fact that the H 2Oabundance is higher in the surface layers than in the midplane by 1 Myr. In the midplane, the chemical timescales are longer and therefore the conditions more closely reflect the initial con-ditions from the DiscEvolution models. However, in the surface layers, H 2O is actively and rapidly being formed from other O-bearing species. Therefore, in disks with a low pebble flux, Eq. 4 may overestimate the pebble flux. Instead, the observed cold water mass should be treated as an upper limit on the sublimated cold mass, and thus the derived pebble flux. Finally, the analysis of Krijt et al. (2025) demonstrate the 1500 /6000 K line ratio to be sensitive not just to Mcold H2O, but also to Mhot H2O (see Fig. A.1 in that work). 4.3. CO 2/H 2O as a tracer of drift 

The retrieved H 2O column can be strongly a ff ected by the dust opacity in a tra ffi c jam scenario, and become almost invariant with time. Therefore, Sellek et al. (2025) propose an alternative diagnostic of H 2O delivery: the CO 2/H2O column density ratio. Unlike the enhancement of H 2O, the enhancement of CO 2 is not co-located with the tra ffi c jam in space and time. Therefore, us-ing this column density ratio may help address one of the issues highlighted in Sect. 4.2, the dependence of the pebble flux on η.Fig. 8 presents the CO 2/H2O retrieved column density ratio from our models. A full exploration of the CO 2 abundance structure and the response of its spectral features to the disk evolution in each model can be found in Appendix A. The results from Sellek et al. (2025) are shown in the grey line in Fig. 8, which is a model that accounts for changes in both dust and gas and contains a tra ffi c jam. Therefore, it should

This is caused by the abundance structure of CO 2 being much more intricate than H 2O (see Fig. A.1), and the most emissive CO 2 is predominantly found in a thin layer where H 2O cannot yet self-shield. > 4.4. Further considerations: spectral types and mixed ices

This work considers the evolution of only a single spectral type, as our models are based on the K5 star AS 209. However, model-ing work has demonstrated that the evolution of the disk’s com-position may play out di ff erently for stars of di ff erent spectral types (e.g. Mah et al. 2023). Depending on the spectral type of the central star, the spatial scales on which transport processes occur will be di ff erent as snowlines may be located closer in or further out. This, along with the mass of the star, will then also alter the timescales on which these processes occur. In particu-lar, the lower stellar mass and closer-in snowlines of low-mass stars allow for the composition of the disk to evolve on much shorter timescales, meaning that they may become carbon-rich much sooner than T Tauri disks (e.g. Mah et al. 2023; Sellek & van Dishoeck 2025). Still, the overall sequence plays out the same regardless: H 2Ois brought in first, making the inner disk oxygen-rich, followed closely by CO 2 and on longer timescales by carbon-rich gas, which raises the C /O ratio. Therefore, the trends observed in this work will likely remain, but may occur on shorter or longer timescales depending on the properties of the host star. On a pop-ulation level, the presence of stars of di ff erent spectral types may make it more di ffi cult to assess what stage of its evolution (H 2O-rich, CO 2-rich, carbon-rich) a population of disks is in, as it is likely that a range of conditions will be probed. Another consideration that the spectral type of the star brings to mind is the strength of UV radiation, which in turn may alter the chemistry in the disk (e.g. Walsh et al. 2015). AS 209 is quite luminous in the FUV, with an FUV luminosity of 8 × 10 −3

L⊙. Vlasblom et al. (2025b) tested the e ff ects of both higher and lower FUV luminosity on the H 2O abundance in the disk, and found only a minor impact. For stars with a lower FUV luminos-ity, the strength of the cold H 2O lines may be slightly increased (by ∼20-30%) due to cold H 2O being able to survive further out in the surface layers where it would otherwise be rapidly pho-todissociated. The hot H 2O reservoir may be slightly more af-fected in stronger FUV fields, as also discussed in Sect. 3.3. Still, this is unlikely to a ff ect the trends observed in this work. Finally, we have only considered the transport of pure ices in this work. However, H 2O ice also has the capability to trap some amount of volatiles such as CO, which will then desorb much closer to the star than they normally would (see, e.g., Collings et al. 2003; Ligterink et al. 2024). While the trapping of volatiles within H 2O ice will not a ff ect the delivery of H 2O itself, it may aff ect the delivery of oxygen as a whole. In Fig. 7, we demon-strate how the mass flow of oxygen is relatively constant with time, and therefore contributes to the continued presence of H 2Oin the inner disk via gas-phase chemistry. If a large part of the volatile oxygen, for example in the form of CO, could be trapped in H 2O ice, it could potentially be delivered to the inner disk much sooner than it otherwise would. However, only a limited amount of CO can be trapped by H2O ice (roughly 5-20% by mass; see Ligterink et al. 2024). Additionally, transport models that include CO trapping by H 2Oice from Williams et al. (2025) demonstrate that the gaseous CO released at the H 2O snowline will subsequently di ff use back out-wards towards its own snowline, and therefore e ffi ciently spread back out across almost the entire disk. The amount of gas-phase CO that advects onto the star at late times is thus not actually that significantly reduced. Therefore, the results from this work likely would not change significantly if trapping of CO ice were to be accounted for. 

5. Conclusions 

This work investigates how the emission of H 2O can be used as a tracer of pebble drift by combining the 1D transport code Dis-cEvolution with the 2D thermochemical code DALI. We sum-marize our main findings as follows: 

– The temperature structure of the disk is significantly a ff ected by the transport of both gas and dust to the inner disk (Fig. 2). The delivery of oxygen (mainly in the form of H 2O) to the inner regions brings the H /H2 transition deeper into the disk, and thereby raises the temperature in the IR emitting layers of the disk. A pile-up of dust in the inner disk, on the other hand, will lower the temperature in these layers. With time, as the dust depletes from the disk, the temperature in the entire disk rises strongly. 

– The IR emission from H 2O is a ff ected by both the abun-dance and temperature structure of the disk (Fig. 4, left col-umn). However, as many IR lines are found to rapidly be-come optically thick as the abundance increases, trends in line flux generally follow the evolution of the disk’s temper-ature structure. 

– The retrieved hot and warm H 2O mass from LTE slab fits shows e ff ects from both the changes in abundance as well as the changes in dust properties of the disk (Fig. 4, right column). When a tra ffi c jam is present in the inner disk, the delivery of gas-phase H 2O can proceed mostly unnoticed due to the co-delivery of dust, as no change in retrieved mass is observed. When no tra ffi c jam is present, the delivery can be traced through the change in retrieved mass. 

– The retrieved cold H 2O mass, on the other hand, is much more constant with time regardless of the dust properties of the disk, as it traces further out and is therefore less a ff ected by a potential tra ffi c jam. 

– When only gas-phase H 2O is delivered to the inner disk, the relative strength of the cold H 2O lines at 23.8 μm (which are thought to trace pebble drift) does not change (Fig. 5, leftmost panel; see also Fig. 6). This indicates that H 2O sub-limating at the snowline needs to undergo (rapid) radial out-ward di ff usion combined with vertical mixing for observa-tions of cold H 2O emission to truly trace H 2O that has been delivered to the inner disk. 

– Instead, the relative strength of the cold lines is most strongly aff ected by changes in the dust distribution of the disk when

A&A proofs: manuscript no. main 

a tra ffi c jam is present (Fig. 5, top row; see also Fig. 6). The pile-up of dust in this scenario allows for the preferential ob-scuration of the hot and warm H 2O lines, thereby producing a spectrum with strong cold H 2O solely through the delivery of dust, rather than the delivery of H 2O. 

– The use of the 1500 /6000 K H 2O line ratio as a proxy for the pebble mass flux crossing the H 2O snowline warrants cau-tion. It is found to provide a decent order-of-magnitude esti-mate of the pebble flux at early times ( <1 Myr), but system-atically overestimates the true pebble flux at late times ( >1Myr) by several orders of magnitude (Fig. 7). The 1500 /6000 K line ratio is shown to be sensitive to both the temperature and the dust distribution in the disk, and changes in this line ratio can often be induced by changes in the hot H 2O mass as well as the cold H 2O mass. This work demonstrates that modeling the transport of gas and dust to the inner disk in 2D brings interesting insights into the use of H 2O lines as a tracer of pebble drift. For future work, it will be important to fully consider the e ff ects of transport on the entire H 2O reservoir, not just the cold reservoir, as this may have considerable e ff ects on the observable tracers. Addition-ally, outward radial di ff usion has been shown to be important for properly modeling the cold H 2O sublimating o ff the dust grains. Finally, it will be important to also consider the e ff ects of gaps and dust traps, which may significantly alter the pebble flux and therefore can give further insights into the use of H 2O emission as a tracer of drift.

Appendix A: CO 2 

> A.1. CO 2abundance and flux

Fig. A.1 presents the gas-phase CO 2 abundance throughout the disk in two of our five grids (the two grids with varied dust and abundances), shown at the same three time steps as Fig. 3. The full version with all five grids is presented in Fig. B.7. As CO 2 forms e ffi ciently in the gas-phase at colder temperatures than H 2O ( ≲300 K; Charnley 1997; van Dishoeck et al. 2013; Walsh et al. 2015), its gas-phase reservoir is located further out in the disk. However, in the warmer, inner regions of the disk, the presence of gas-phase H 2O can have a large impact on the CO 2

abundance structure. When a su ffi cient column of H 2O is present (≳ 10 18 cm −2; Bethell & Bergin 2009), it can begin to self-shield against photodissociation. This quenches the formation of CO 2,as it relies on the presence of OH, a photodissociation product of H 2O. Simultaneously, the photodissociation rate of CO 2 is not decreased by a similar amount, as no su ffi cient column is present to benefit from self-shielding or mutual shielding e ff ects. There-fore, in regions where H 2O is abundant (around Z/R ∼ 0.08, within 0.5 au), one can observe a ‘hole’ in the CO 2 gas-phase abundance map (see also Bosman et al. 2022b). Above this hole, a thin layer of CO 2 is present where a large-enough H 2O col-umn has not yet been built up. This layer can actually contribute quite significantly to the total observed flux, especially when the temperature is high. In terms of abundance variations with time, similar trends are observed as for the H 2O abundance (Fig. 3). A small build-up of gas-phase CO 2 can be seen right at the midplane snow-line (located at ∼2 au) at 10 kyr. At 0.1 Myr, this enhancement has increased and distributed itself further across the inner disk. Still, since the CO 2 snowline is located further out than the H 2Osnowline, this inwards advection occurs on a longer timescale for CO 2, and therefore has not yet reached the innermost regions of the disk. This only happens after ∼0.4 Myr (see Fig. B.1). However, this enhancement in CO 2 will also linger for a longer time. Therefore, by 1 Myr, CO 2 is still enhanced, whereas H 2Oshows a clear depletion. The impact of the dust properties is most clear at the latest times, when the dust has significantly depleted from the disk. The dust τ = 1 surface is located much deeper into the disk, es-pecially in the models where no tra ffi c jam is present (see orange dashed line). This exposes more of the colder, deeper-lying CO 2

reservoir beyond 1 au than is otherwise typically visible. The left column of Fig. A.2 presents the integrated fluxes of the 12 CO 2 and 13 CO 2 Q-branches as a function of time for all grids. Globally, the flux of both CO 2 isotopologues closely follows the temperature evolution of the disk. When only the abundance is varied, a small temperature increase is present in the inner regions due to the influx of oxygen (see Fig. 2). This temperature increase is clearly traced by an increase in CO 2 flux. Though the CO 2 also emits from further out in the disk ( ≳0.5 au) than where this temperature increase is present, it is the flux contribution from the thin, warm layer of CO 2 within ∼0.2 au that is responsible for this flux increase. We note that this sensitivity of the CO 2 emission to the disk temperature is very well traced by the shape of its Q-branch. This is demonstrated in Fig. B.8 for 12 CO 2, but this is seen for all isotopologues. The leftmost panel of Fig. B.8 demonstrates how the Q-branch broadens around 0.1 Myr, and the peak shifts towards bluer wavelengths. This is a clear indication of an in-crease in temperature (see, e.g. Cami et al. 2000; Bosman et al. 2017), as higher vibrational levels are becoming more excited. When only the dust properties are varied, the flux of all CO 2

isotopologues remains relatively constant, until it strongly in-creases at late times when the dust depletes from the disk, and it starts to significantly heat up. This is also clearly visible in the shape of the Q-branch (Fig. B.8). When a tra ffi c jam is present, the increase in flux starts at later times, as the inner regions of the disk can remain cooler for longer in that scenario. However, whereas the warm H 2O flux showed a clear dip in flux around 0.1 Myr in this scenario due to the obscuration by dust (see left col-umn, middle panel in Fig. 4), this is not seen to the same extent in the CO 2 flux. This is likely caused by the CO 2 flux containing a contribution from regions which are either not a ff ected by the dust pile-up (e.g., further out in the disk) and /or are not limited by the dust optical depth (see Sect. A.2). When both the abun-dances and the dust are varied, a combined e ff ect is seen in both flux and Q-branch shape, though the global trend is more similar to the simulations with varied dust only than to those with varied abundances only. 

> A.2. Retrievals

The middle and right columns of Fig. A.2 present the results of LTE slab model fits to the emission of 12 CO 2 and 13 CO 2. These fits are performed to the full emission features of these species, thus including their P-, Q-, and R-branches, as well as their hot bands. This once again demonstrates how the emission from all CO 2 isotopologues is very sensitive to the temperature of the disk, as the retrieved temperatures very closely follow the pre-viously identified trends that were also visible in the absolute integrated fluxes. The retrieved temperatures also demonstrate how 13 CO 2 traces slightly deeper and further-out regions in the disk (see also Fig. A.1), as they trace colder temperatures. Unlike H 2O, however, the retrieved CO 2 column density shows a less clear picture. In the case of 13 CO 2, the emission is found to be (marginally) optically thin in all grids at almost all time steps. Therefore, the column density and emitting area are degenerate, and therefore the value of ∼ 10 17 cm −2 that is found at most time steps represents an upper limit. At 1 Myr, in both models with a tra ffi c jam present, the minor isotopologues show a spike in retrieved column density. This is most likely caused by the sharp drop in dust opacity across the H 2O snow-line, that temporarily exposes a deeper-lying reservoir of CO 2,which preferentially enhances the column of the minor isotopo-logues. Interestingly, this is not present in the models without the tra ffi c jam, despite the dust properties outside the H 2O snowline being the same in both models. However, the build-up of dust in-side the H 2O snowline by the tra ffi c jam also provides shielding of the radiation in the radial direction. Therefore, in the models without the tra ffi c jam, there is a much stronger temperature in-crease in the inner disk, which leads to a much more extended H2O gas reservoir (extending out to ∼2 au instead of 0.5, see Fig. 3). This H 2O provides shielding, allowing a larger midplane abundance of CO 2 to build up around ∼2 au, and allowing the thin surface layer of CO 2 to extend to the same radius. Therefore, most of the CO 2 emission in the model without a tra ffi c jam still comes from this thin layer, rather than the deeper-laying reser-voir. In the model with the tra ffi c jam, however, this thin surface layer is now much less radially extended, and a component from the deeper reservoir can be seen. The emission from 12 CO 2 is slightly more sensitive to the total column than the isotopologues. Since the delivery of H 2O to the inner disk may thus go largely unnoticed, Sellek et al. (2025) propose that the CO 2/H2O column density ratio may provide a better tracer of pebble drift, as the emission from CO 2 traces further out in the disk and is therefore not a ff ected by the pile-up of dust to the same degree as H 2O. Fig. A.3 presents the ratio of the retrieved H 2O and CO 2

column densities, where the warm H 2O column is used. In the grid with varied abundance only, the retrieved column density of both species traces its enhancement, and therefore the ratio nicely reflects how this happens in sequence: the H 2O abundance is enhanced at early times, leading to a decrease in the CO 2/H2Ocolumn density ratio, followed by an increase as the CO 2 abun-dance is enhanced instead and the H 2O is already being drained onto the central star. This follows the behavior seen in Scenario 1 presented in Sellek et al. (2025) (which has a tra ffi c jam; see grey line in Fig. A.3), though the relative enhancement of CO 2

with respect to H 2O is found to much more shallow in this work compared to Sellek et al. (2025). We also note that Sellek et al. (2025) generally retrieve larger CO 2 column densities, and there-fore their CO 2/H2O column density ratio is scaled down by a factor 50 in Fig. A.3 for easier comparison. In the grids where only the dust is varied, however, a di ff er-ent behavior is seen. Now, the CO 2 column density is found to be largely invariant to changes in the dust, so the behavior of the column density ratio is largely driven by H 2O. Therefore, when a tra ffi c jam is present, an increase in the CO 2/H2O column den-sity ratio is observed around 0.1 Myr due to the obscuration of H2O by dust (dashed light blue line in Fig. A.3). When no tra ffi cjam is present, the column density ratio instead remains roughly constant, perhaps showing a slight decrease with time. This may be due to the rising temperature in the disk at late times, which will allow the gas-phase H 2O reservoir to grow substantially, whereas the CO 2 reservoir becomes smaller by comparison (see rightmost columns in Figs. 3 and A.1). When the e ff ects of both dust and gas transport are com-bined, the CO 2/H2O column density ratio remains relatively con-stant with time. With a tra ffi c jam, now both the H 2O and CO 2

columns are found to be roughly invariant with time, and with-out a tra ffi c jam, both columns roughly trace a similar pattern of enhancement, and therefore mostly cancel out. Thus, unlike pre-dicted by Sellek et al. (2025), the CO 2/H2O column density ratio does not seem more sensitive to the delivery of material than the H2O column density by itself. Most of this can likely be attributed to the more complicated CO 2 abundance structure that is used in this work compared to Sellek et al. (2025). Since a significant amount of CO 2 emission traces the thin surface layer inside the H 2O snowline, its column is rather invariant and set by the H 2O shielding rather than the dust continuum. Sellek et al. (2025), on the other hand, assume a vertically constant abundance. Therefore, changes in the dust continuum do make a di ff erence in the total CO 2 column in their work, as the CO 2 emission from inside the H 2O snowline be-comes obscured, and the reservoir outside the snowline can start to dominate the CO 2 emission instead. This e ff ect is what al-lowed the CO 2 emission to be sensitive to the delivery in their work, but since it is not present in ours, we do not find the same trend. Therefore, the CO 2/H2O column density ratio may not pro-vide a better tracer of drift. The interpretation of the CO 2 emis-sion is not straightforward, as the emission is quite sensitive to the disk temperature, and its abundance structure is complex. One may ask, however, whether it is realistic that the CO 2 emis-sion traces such a thin surface layer, as e ffi cient vertical mixing may very rapidly spread it out, which is not accounted for in this work. The emission from 13 CO 2, along with the other iso-topologues, does emit from further out in the disk and therefore should be less a ff ected by this invariance of its column to the dust continuum. However, the column density of any isotopologues will likely be poorly constrained due to the emission being optically thin. This is seen in the behavior of the 13 CO 2/H2O column density ratio (bottom panel of Fig. A.3), which generally reflects the inverse of the H 2O column density as the 13 CO 2 column den-sity represents an upper limit for most models. Instead, one will need to consider the total mass or total number of molecules (N = N × πR2), as this quantity will be properly constrained in this case. To fully understand what tracers may best reflect the delivery of material to the inner disk, further investigation beyond the scope of this paper will be needed.
