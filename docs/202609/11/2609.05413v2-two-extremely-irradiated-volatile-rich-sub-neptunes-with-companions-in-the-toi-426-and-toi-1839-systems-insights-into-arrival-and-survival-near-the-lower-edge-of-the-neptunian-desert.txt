ABSTRACT 

Context. The origin of the lower edge of the Neptunian desert remains an open question. The leading hypotheses are tidal disruption of volatile-rich sub-Neptunes following high-eccentricity tidal migration (HEM) and atmospheric escape of their primordial H 2/He envelopes. 

Aims. We aimed to confirm and characterise two TESS candidates near the lower edge of the desert, TOI-426.01 ( Porb ≈ 1.32 d) and TOI-1839.01 (Porb ≈ 1.42 d), orbiting the solar-type stars TOI-426 (HD 34390; V = 10.1 mag, G2 V) and TOI-1839 (TYC 304-865-1; V = 11.0 mag, G9 V). 

Methods. We acquired 77 HARPS radial velocities (RVs) of TOI-426 and 70 of TOI-1839, and we performed a joint Bayesian analysis of the RVs and TESS photometry. The RVs of both targets show significant activity signals, which we modelled using shared-timescale and multidimensional Gaussian process frameworks to disentangle stellar variability and derive precise planetary parameters. 

Results. We confirm TOI-1839 b ( Rp = 2.27 ± 0.10 R ⊕, Mp = 7.10 ± 0.78 M ⊕) and TOI-426 b ( Rp = 2.19 ± 0.09 R ⊕, Mp = 6.7 ± 1.8 M ⊕), and we detect an additional transiting sub-Neptune, TOI-1839 c ( Porb ≈ 4.02 d, Rp = 2.50 ± 0.13 R ⊕, Mp = 6.0 ± 1.1 M ⊕), and a long-period giant planet, TOI-426 c (assuming a circular orbit, Porb = 235 .8+9.2

TOI-426 b ( F ≈ 1700 F ⊕) and TOI-1839 b ( F ≈ 1050 F ⊕)are among the most highly irradiated sub-Neptunes known to date, but their bulk densities nevertheless require a significant volatile component. For the three transiting planets, our interior-structure and atmospheric-escape models yield short mass-loss timescales of ∼ 1–100 Myr for H 2/He-dominated atmospheres but much longer timescales of ∼ 100–1000 Gyr for H 2O-dominated envelopes, favouring a steam-world scenario for these planets. We also explored the population of short-period low-mass sub-Neptunes with outer long-period giant companions and found a strikingly high incidence ( ∼ 35%) at the shortest orbital periods near the desert edge, where successful HEM outcomes are expected to circularise. 

Conclusions. The survival of highly irradiated volatile-rich sub-Neptunes near the lower edge of the Neptunian desert is di ffi cult to reconcile with a classical picture in which this boundary is set solely by the evaporation of primordial H 2/He envelopes. At the same time, the high incidence of long-period giant companions is consistent with an important role for HEM in shaping this region.

1. Introduction 

Over the past ∼20 years, exoplanet surveys have revealed a pro-nounced deficit of Neptunian planets at very short orbital peri-ods, commonly referred to as the Neptunian desert (Mazeh et al. 2005; Lecavelier Des Etangs 2007; Davis & Wheatley 2009; Szabó & Kiss 2011; Youdin 2011). While this feature is most pronounced in the Neptunian domain ( Rp ≈ 4–8 R⊕), it ex-tends from the sub-Neptune ( Rp ≈ 1.8 R⊕) to the Jovian ( Rp ≈

20 R⊕) regimes, where formation, migration, and atmospheric evolution theories predict fundamentally di ff erent outcomes (e.g. Dawson & Johnson 2018; Bean et al. 2021). Today, it remains an open question whether the desert is produced by a single global mechanism or arises from the interplay of multiple processes act-ing across these regimes (Mazeh et al. 2016; Matsakos & Königl 2016; Bourrier et al. 2018, 2023, 2025; Owen & Lai 2018; Vissapragada et al. 2022; Castro-González et al. 2024a,b, 2026). A first step towards understanding the origin of the desert is to characterise its shape. Mazeh et al. (2016) identified an up-

per boundary with a negative slope in the giant-planet popula-tion in the period–radius plane (a related trend was previously reported in the period–mass plane by Mazeh et al. 2005) and a lower boundary with a positive slope in the sub-Neptune pop-ulation. Their extrapolated intersection at Porb ≈ 10 d in the Neptunian domain defines the classical triangular desert shape. Castro-González et al. (2024a) re-analysed the Kepler sample, dominated by FGK hosts, correcting for observational biases. They found that the upper and lower boundaries are smoothly connected at Porb ≈ 3 d in the Neptunian regime, forming a ver-tical boundary where the slope changes sign. This continuous geometry suggests that the di ff erent regimes may be physically connected, either through a single mechanism or through the in-terplay of closely coupled processes. Matsakos & Königl (2016) proposed the first unified view of the desert. They suggest that its upper and lower boundaries can be reproduced by high-eccentricity tidal migration (HEM; e.g. Ford & Rasio 2008; Correia et al. 2011) of planets whose orbits were circularised near the tidal disruption limit (e.g. Roche 1849; Ford & Rasio 2006). Recently, Castro-González et al. (2026) re-

visited this scenario using updated mass–radius relations, find-ing good agreement with the revised desert geometry. Alterna-tively, atmospheric escape has also been invoked as a contribu-tor (e.g. Lecavelier des Etangs et al. 2004). In the sub-Neptune regime, atmospheric escape is expected to act e ffi ciently (e.g. Owen & Jackson 2012; Lopez & Fortney 2013) and has been shown to broadly reproduce the observed lower boundary of the desert (Owen & Lai 2018). In this context, the strong evapora-tion of Neptunes that migrated early through the protoplanetary disk has been proposed as an additional pathway contributing to the desert shape (Bourrier et al. 2023, 2025). In contrast, the up-per boundary has been shown to be stable against photoevapora-tion (e.g. Ionov et al. 2018; Vissapragada et al. 2022). Therefore, whether the desert is predominantly shaped by HEM or instead arises from a combination of HEM setting the upper boundary and atmospheric escape shaping the lower and Neptunian bound-aries remains an open question. Other phenomena, such as post-circularisation orbital decay, Roche-lobe overflow, and tidal in-flation, have also been discussed as potentially relevant in the desert region (e.g. Fossati et al. 2010; Matsakos & Königl 2016; Hallatt & Millholland 2026a,b; Liveoak et al. 2026). Additional insight into the origin of the desert can be ob-tained from the unusual planets found within it (e.g. West et al. 2019; Jenkins et al. 2020b; Ikuta et al. 2025; Carleo et al. 2026a) and the population near its boundaries (e.g. Baliwal et al. 2025; Barkaoui et al. 2025; Carleo et al. 2026b; Jenkins et al. 2025; Lockley et al. 2025; Naponiello et al. 2025; Yalçinkaya et al. 2025). Some Neptune-sized planets in the desert exhibit un-usually high densities ( ∼5–10 g cm −3; Armstrong et al. 2020; Naponiello et al. 2023) consistent with being the remnant cores of tidally disrupted giants after HEM, although alternative sce-narios have also been discussed (e.g. Armstrong et al. 2020). In the Jovian regime, the desert boundary coincides with the short-period edge of the hot-Jupiter pile-up, an overdensity of plan-ets at periods of ∼3–5 d (e.g. Udry & Santos 2007), thought to reflect the accumulation of successful HEM outcomes (e.g. Dawson & Johnson 2018). A similar overdensity has been ob-served in the Neptunian domain, known as the Neptunian ridge, identified in Kepler data (Castro-González et al. 2024a) and ten-tatively recovered in TESS samples (Cui et al. 2026), which has also been discussed as being linked to HEM (e.g. Bourrier et al. 2018, 2023, 2025; Castro-González et al. 2024b; Doyle et al. 2025; Yee et al. 2025; Almenara et al. 2026; Handley & Batygin 2026; Thomas et al. 2026; Zanazzi et al. 2026). In this con-text, Castro-González et al. (2026) showed that the tidal survival band, where HEM outcomes are expected to be preferentially de-posited (e.g. Fabrycky & Tremaine 2007), naturally traces both the pile-up and ridge extensions, providing a common explana-tion for the desert–overdensity structures in the Neptunian and Jovian domains. In contrast, no comparable overdensity has been observed in the sub-Neptune regime. There, the survival band is narrower and embedded within the high-occurrence small-planet population, making any excess more di ffi cult to identify. Addi-tional observables, such as the prevalence of companions capa-ble of triggering HEM, may therefore provide further support for its role in shaping the lower desert boundary. In this work, we investigate the lower edge of the Neptunian desert by confirming and characterising two TESS candidates in this region. 2. Observations 

2.1. Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite 

TOI-426 and TOI-1839 were observed by the Transiting Exo-planet Survey Satellite (TESS; Ricker et al. 2015) at a 2-min ca-dence. TOI-426 observations were carried out in sectors 5, 32, and 98 (hereinafter S5, S32, and S98), and TOI-1839 observa-tions were carried out in sectors 23, 46, and 50 (hereinafter S23, S46, and S50), resulting in a total of 75 767 target pixel files (TPFs) for TOI-426 and 47 185 TPFs for TOI-1839. These TPFs are publicly available at the Mikulski Archive for Space Tele-scopes. 1 They were processed by the Science Processing Opera-tions Center (SPOC) pipeline at the NASA Ames Research Cen-ter (Jenkins et al. 2016), which generated simple aperture pho-tometry (SAP) and presearch data-conditioned simple aperture photometry (PDCSAP; Smith et al. 2012; Stumpe et al. 2012). The SPOC photometric apertures were selected with the Create Optimal Apertures module (COA; Bryson et al. 2010; Smith et al. 2020), which also provides the FLFRCSAP and CROWDSAP metrics used to correct the PDCSAP light curves for crowding. The CROWDSAP values indicate negligible con-tamination for TOI-1839 ( < 0.1%), but almost 40% contamina-tion for TOI-426. We used TESS-cont (Castro-González et al. 2024b) based on Gaia sources (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2018, 2023) to identify the main contaminants. > 2.2. High Accuracy Radial velocity Planet Searcher

We observed TOI-426 and TOI-1839 with the High Accuracy Radial velocity Planet Searcher (HARPS; Mayor et al. 2003). HARPS is a fibre-fed, cross-dispersed echelle spectrograph mounted on the ESO 3.6-m telescope at La Silla Observatory, Chile. It provides a wavelength coverage of 378–691 nm and a resolving power of R ≈ 115 000, enabling RV measurements with precisions at the m s −1 level. We acquired 78 spectra of TOI-426 between 25 Septem-ber 2022 and 30 March 2023, and 71 spectra of TOI-1839 be-tween 9 January 2021 and 18 June 2023, yielding a total of 149 HARPS observations. We excluded one spectrum of TOI-426 (JD 2459949.7) and one of TOI-1839 (JD 2459272.9) owing to their anomalously low S /N per pixel of 8.9 and 17.1, respec-tively. The retained TOI-426 spectra have S /N per pixel ranging from 31.5 to 75.3, with a median of 54.0, while the retained TOI-1839 spectra range from 27.4 to 52.8, with a median of 40.2. The TOI-426 spectra were obtained as part of the HARPS-NOMADS programme 108.21YY.001 (PI: Armstrong), while the TOI-1839 data were acquired under the HARPS-NOMADS programmes 108.21YY.001 (30 observations) and 108.21YY.002 (7 observa-tions), as well as under the NCORES large programme 1102.C-0249(F) (34 observations; PI: Armstrong). All observations were performed in High Accuracy Mode with exposure times of 1800 s. The primary fibre was centred on the star, and the secondary fibre was placed on the sky to monitor the background light. We reduced the raw spectra through the HARPS Data Reduc-tion Software ( DRS ). The DRS extracts the RVs based on a mod-ified version of the cross-correlation technique (Baranne et al. 1996), where di ff erent spectral lines of a numerical mask are weighed according to their RV information (Pepe et al. 2002). We also used the HARPS DRS to extract several activity indica-tors such as the full width at half maximum (FWHM) and con-trast of the cross-correlation function (CCF), the bisector span (BIS), the H α, Ca II H&K doublet (Ca), and sodium doublet (NaD) line depths, and the S index. In Tables D.8 and D.9, we present the complete HARPS data set for TOI-426 and TOI-1839, respectively. The HARPS RV uncertainties of TOI-426 range from 1.4 to 3.2 m s −1 (median of 1.9 m s −1). These small values contrast with the large RV variations, which show a peak-to-peak amplitude of 156 m s −1 and a standard deviation of 34 m s −1. The RV uncertainties of TOI-1839 range from 1.5 to 3.0 m s −1 (median of 1.9 m s −1), which are also considerably smaller than the observed RV peak-to-peak amplitude of 49 m s −1 and standard deviation of 13 m s −1. These large di ff erences between the RV uncertainties and dispersions suggest the presence of prominent planetary or stellar signals. 

> 2.3. High-resolution imaging

High-resolution imaging is a crucial part of planet confirmation, as close stellar companions can contaminate the observed transit and bias the inferred stellar and planetary properties (Ciardi et al. 2015; Cabrera et al. 2017; Furlan & Howell 2020; Lester et al. 2021; Howell et al. 2021; Castro-González et al. 2022). To as-sess the presence of nearby companions, high-resolution imag-ing of TOI-426 and TOI-1839 was obtained with Gemini and SOAR in the context of the ExoFOP follow-up programme. The raw data, processed contrast curves, and ancillary diagnostics are available at the ExoFOP-TESS repository. 2

> 2.3.1. Gemini

TOI-426 was observed on 12 October 2019 and TOI-1839 on 9 June 2020 with the ‘Alopeke speckle instrument on the Gemini North 8-m telescope (Scott et al. 2021). ‘Alopeke provides si-multaneous speckle imaging in two bands (562 nm and 832 nm) with output data products including reconstructed images and robust magnitude contrast limits on companion detection. Thir-teen sets of 1000 × 0.06 s exposures were collected for TOI-1839, and five similar sets were collected for TOI-426. All im-ages were subjected to Fourier analysis using the standard reduc-tion pipeline (Howell et al. 2011). No close companions were detected around either TOI-426 or TOI-1839 within the di ff rac-tion limit (20 mas) out to 1 .2′′ , down to contrast limits of 5– 8 mag relative to the target stars.

2.3.2. SOAR 

We also searched for stellar companions using speckle imag-ing with the 4.1-m Southern Astrophysical Research (SOAR) telescope (Tokovinin 2018), observing TOI-426 on 17 March 2019 and TOI-1839 on 27 February 2021. 2.4. Wide Angle Search for Planets 

TOI-426 and TOI-1839 were extensively monitored by the Wide Angle Search for Planets survey (WASP; Pollacco et al. 2006; Hellier et al. 2011), which operated arrays of wide-field cameras in both the Northern and Southern hemispheres. For most of the survey, each array comprised eight cameras equipped with 200-mm f /1.8 lenses, 2048 ×2048 CCDs, and 400–700 nm bandpass filters. In 2012, the WASP-South array was changed to 85-mm f/1.2 lenses with SDSS-r filters, enabling a focus on brighter tar-gets ( V ∼ 6–9 mag) without reaching saturation. TOI-426 was observed from 2008 to 2014 (58 569 data points), and TOI-1839 from 2009 to 2014 (35 522 data points), with yearly campaigns of ∼160 and ∼140 d, respectively. We used these long-baseline photometric data to search for rotation-induced stellar activity signals, as described in App. A.2. The corresponding WASP data sets are listed in Tables D.10 and D.11. 

3. Stellar characterisation 

TOI-426 and TOI-1839 are bright stars ( V = 10 .1 and 11.0 mag, respectively; Table 1) located in the solar neighbourhood at he-liocentric distances of 113 and 117 pc, respectively. TOI-426 has a co-moving companion at a separation of 9 ′′ (TIC 189013222; Star 1 in Fig. 1), previously reported in historical catalogues (e.g. Schönfeld 1886; Aitken & Doolittle 1932) and confirmed by Gaia proper motions, parallaxes, and RVs. We found no evi-dence of additional co-moving companions around TOI-1839. 

3.1. Stellar atmospheric parameters and abundances 

The stellar atmospheric parameters ( Teff , log g, ξt, [Fe /H]) were derived using the ARES+MOOG methodology as described by Santos et al. (2013), Sousa (2014), and Sousa et al. (2021). For this analysis, we used the latest version of ARES 3 (Sousa et al. 2007, 2015). We combined the spectra of TOI-426 and TOI-1839 and measured the equivalent widths (EW) of the iron lines se-lected by Sousa et al. (2008). The parameters were derived using ionisation and excitation equilibrium together with a grid of Ku-rucz model atmospheres (Kurucz 1993) and the radiative transfer code MOOG (Sneden 1973). We also derived trigonometric sur-face gravities using Gaia DR3 following Sousa et al. (2021). The stellar masses and radii were inferred using the relations from Torres et al. (2010). We also determined the abundances of C, O, Mg, Si, Ti, Ni, and neutron-capture elements using the atmospheric parame-ters and the classical curve-of-growth method (Adibekyan et al. 2015, 2016; Delgado Mena et al. 2017, 2021). 4. Analysis and results 

Before carrying out the joint transit and RV modelling, we per-formed a frequency analysis of the TESS photometry, WASP photometry, and HARPS RVs and activity indicators, which is presented in App. A. This analysis independently recovers the TESS candidates, reveals the additional 4.02-d transiting sig-nal in TOI-1839, and confirms the presence of rotation-related stellar-activity signals. Owing to the large volume of TESS and HARPS data, our modelling was then performed in two steps. In Sect. 4.1, we de-trended the TESS photometry by jointly mod-elling the correlated noise and the transit signals to better pre-serve the transit shapes. In Sect. 4.2, we analysed the de-trended photometry together with the HARPS RVs. In Sect. 4.3, we de-rive sensitivity limits from the RV residuals. 

> 4.1. TESS photometry modelling and de-trending

To model the transit signals, we used the batman implementa-tion (Kreidberg 2015) of the quadratic limb-darkened model by Mandel & Agol (2002). The model parameters are the orbital pe-riod ( Porb ), time of inferior conjunction ( T0), orbital inclination (i), quadratic limb darkening (LD) coe ffi cients ( u1, u2), planet-to-star radius ratio ( Rp/R⋆), and scaled semi-major axis ( a/R⋆). We re-parametrised the LD following Kipping (2013) for e ffi -cient sampling: q1 = (u1 + u2)2 and q2 = 0.5 u1(u1 + u2)−1. The scaled semi-major axis was parametrised in terms of the stel-lar density ( ρ⋆) via Kepler’s third law (e.g. Sozzetti et al. 2007; Winn 2010), and the orbital inclination was parametrised via the impact parameter ( b; Seager & Mallén-Ornelas 2003). To model the correlated noise, we included a Gaussian pro-cess (GP) regression component (Rasmussen & Williams 2006; Roberts et al. 2012) defined by the approximate Matérn-3 /2 ker-nel as implemented in celerite (Foreman-Mackey et al. 2017). > 4.2. TESS and HARPS joint modelling

We modelled the TOI-426 and TOI-1839 systems by jointly fitting the HARPS RVs and the de-trended TESS photometry. The same transit model, parametrisation, and priors described in Sect. 4.1 were adopted. For the RVs, we tested several models combining Keplerian signals and GPs to account for activity-induced RV signals. To include the planetary signals in the RV time series, we parametrised them using radvel (Fulton et al. 2018) through the orbital period ( Porb ), time of inferior con-junction ( T0), RV semi-amplitude ( K), orbital eccentricity ( e), and argument of periastron ( ω). We also included an RV o ff -set ( γ) representing the systemic velocity, and an RV jitter term (σHARPS ). We adopted uniform priors for most model parame-ters and modified Je ff reys priors for the jitter terms, which re-main weakly informative over several orders of magnitude while preventing an unphysical preference for large noise amplitudes, as commonly done in Bayesian RV analyses (e.g. Gregory 2005, 2011; Rajpaul et al. 2021; Radica et al. 2022).

> 4.3. Sensitivity limits and constraints on additional planets

We derived RV sensitivity limits following Standing et al. (2022) and Standing et al. (2026). After subtracting the Keplerian and activity signals from the RV time series of TOI-426 and TOI-1839, we analysed the residuals with kima (Faria et al. 2018) to search for additional planetary signals. Once it was verified that no significant signals remained in the residuals, we ran kima 

with the number of planetary signals ( Np) fixed to one. The re-sulting posterior samples are consistent with the residual data, but correspond to signals that are not significantly detected. In total, more than 60 000 posterior samples were obtained for both TOI-426 and TOI-1839. A 3 σ upper limit was then computed in bins of orbital period, yielding the sensitivity limits shown by the blue and red curves in Fig. 5. For low-eccentricity orbits, the available data are sensitive at the 3 σ level to planetary signals lying above the red limits, as is the case for TOI-426 b and c, and TOI-1839 b and c. From Fig. 5, we conclude that, for low-eccentricity orbits in both systems, we can rule out the pres-ence of planets with minimum masses Mp sin i ≳ 2 M ⊕ at Porb ≲

1.5 d, Mp sin i ≳ 3 M ⊕ at Porb ≲ 10 d, and Mp sin i ≳ 10 M ⊕ at 

Porb ≲ 300 d. We note that although the blue curves include sam-ples with higher eccentricities, the projected limits do not cap-ture the full dependence of detectability on eccentricity, the ar-gument of periastron, and the time of periastron passage. Highly eccentric companions whose periastron passages coincide with the large gaps in the RV time series may remain undetected. 

5. Discussion 

Close-in sub-Neptunes with Porb ≲ 1.5 d are rare in exoplanet catalogues, making TOI-426 b and TOI-1839 b valuable labora-tories for investigating planetary evolution under extreme con-ditions. In this section, we place these planets in the context of the Neptunian desert, examine the role of outer companions near the lower desert boundary, and investigate their volatile content through interior-structure and atmospheric-escape modelling.

5.2. Volatile-rich sub-Neptunes under extreme irradiation 

In Fig. 6 (right panel), we contextualise TOI-426 b, TOI-1839 b, and TOI-1839 c in an insolation–radius diagram with the current sample of known small planets. Sub-Neptune planets are typi-cally found at F ≲ 300 F ⊕, whereas super-Earths span a much wider range of insolations. Notably, the combination of their short orbits and the relatively early spectral types of their hosts places TOI-426 b and TOI-1839 b among the most highly irra-diated sub-Neptunes known to date. In particular, TOI-426 b re-ceives the second-highest insolation ( F = 1710 ± 110 F ⊕) among planets in the upper mode of the bimodal radius distribution (i.e. 2 R ⊕ < Rp < 3 R ⊕), surpassed only by TOI-1408 c ( F ≈

2000 F ⊕; Korth et al. 2024). With this increased sample, we ex-plore whether the typically volatile-rich sub-Neptune population remains volatile-rich when subject to such extreme irradiation. In this diagram, planets are colour-coded according to their bulk densities, normalised to the density expected for a planet with a 100 % silicate composition (Zeng et al. 2019). Canonical mildly irradiated sub-Neptunes (e.g. Rp > 2 R ⊕; F ≲ 300 F ⊕) typically exhibit ρp/ρ rock < 1, whereas super-Earths (e.g. Rp < 2 R ⊕) gen-erally show ρp/ρ rock > 1. Notably, despite their extreme irradi-ation levels, TOI-426 b and TOI-1839 b require the presence of volatile elements to explain their densities ( ρp/ρ rock < 1). Primordial H 2/He envelopes of sub-Neptunes are expected to be e ffi ciently eroded under high irradiation (e.g. Owen & Wu 2017). The existence of highly irradiated volatile-rich sub-Neptunes such as TOI-426 b and TOI-1839 b may therefore be explained by scenarios in which the volatiles are not directly ex-posed to stellar irradiation (i.e. buried within the planetary inte-riors; e.g. Luo et al. 2024), or are more resistant to it (i.e. atmo-spheres composed of high mean molecular weight volatiles; e.g. Turbet et al. 2020). In Sect. 5.4, we further explore the internal structures of TOI-426 b, TOI-1839 b, and TOI-1839 c, as well as the impact of atmospheric escape under di ff erent scenarios.

p = 1.2 × 10 −4). To test the robustness of this trend, we per-formed a systematic exploration of the parameter space, varying the mass precision thresholds (33%, 25%, and 20%), the min-imum companion mass ( > 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0, and 1.5 M J), and the minimum orbital period ( > 0, 50, 100, 150, 200, and 300 d) used to define giant companions. Across all 90 tested config-urations, the enrichment of outer giant companions within the tidal survival band remains statistically significant (Fisher ex-act test p < 0.05 in 100% of cases), with p-values spanning 1.4 × 10 −4 to 2 .8 × 10 −2. The di ff erence in detection rates is con-sistently positive, corresponding to typical companion fractions of ∼ 24%–40% inside the band compared to ∼ 5%–9% out-side it. This shows that the observed trend is not driven by the adopted companion definition. We also verified that the compan-ion excess remains statistically significant when extending the inner-planet mass range up to 20 M ⊕, approximately spanning the broader sub-Neptune domain, although the limited number of planets at the high-mass end prevents us from assessing whether the trend holds independently in that regime. As a complemen-tary check, we repeated the analysis using a radius-selected sub-Neptune sample with measured masses (2 .0 ≤ Rp ≤ 3.5 R ⊕), broadly tracing the volatile-rich sub-Neptune regime and con-firming that the trend is not driven by the inclusion of smaller rocky planets. Taken together, these tests show that the excess of outer giant companions is robust to the adopted sample def-inition and is not an artefact of the overlap between rocky and volatile-rich planets in mass. They therefore strengthen the evi-dence of this trend among low-mass, volatile-rich sub-Neptunes near the lower edge of the desert. We note that the construction of the sample mitigates several key observational biases. The requirement that the inner sub-Neptunes have well-constrained masses inherently selects sys-tems with extensive RV data sets. In particular, longer-period planets require more follow-up on average to reach compara-ble mass precision, favouring the detection of outer companions, contrary to the observed trend. Both groups ex-hibit comparable data sets, with several tens to a few hundred RVs and baselines typically exceeding 100–200 days and often extending beyond 500–1000 days. Moreover, only 4 of the 30 comparison systems (13%) show long-term RV trends consistent with outer companions, well below the ∼ 36% fraction observed within the tidal survival band. However, the large gaps in the RV time series may still allow highly eccentric compan-ions whose periastron passages fall within unsampled intervals, as well as companions with longer periods. Additional well-sampled RV observations are therefore required to robustly con-strain the architecture of this system. We note that the short or-bital separation and low mass of TOI-1839 c make it unlikely to act as a HEM perturber for TOI-1839 b (e.g. Petrovich et al. 2019). However, alternative pathways such as low-eccentricity or obliquity-driven migration may operate through dynamical in-teractions between close-in planets such as TOI-1839 b and TOI-1839 c (Pu & Lai 2019; Millholland & Spalding 2020). Overall, whether TOI-1839 b reached the lower edge of the desert via HEM triggered by an as-yet undetected companion, through in-teractions with TOI-1839 c, or alternatively through disk-driven migration or in situ formation remains an open question that will require improved observational constraints together with dedi-cated theoretical and numerical studies. 

5.4. Volatile content of the three transiting planets 

As shown in the mass–radius diagram in Fig. 8, the masses and radii of TOI-426 b, TOI-1839 b, and TOI-1839 c require the pres-ence of a non-negligible volatile component, as their densities are inconsistent with purely rocky compositions at the > 3σ

level. Among them, TOI-1839 c is compatible with either a sub-stantial water-rich layer ( ≳ 50% by mass) or a thin H 2/He en-velope ( ≳ 0.1% by mass). The orbital architecture of TOI-426, namely a short-period sub-Neptune close to the tidal survival band expected after HEM accompanied by a long-period giant planet, makes it a com-pelling system for having undergone this process. In this context, we find a high incidence ( ≈35%) of outer giant companions to low-mass sub-Neptunes near the survival band, consistent with an important role for HEM in shaping the lower desert bound-ary. In contrast, TOI-1839 does not currently show evidence of an outer giant companion, although we cannot rule out its pres-ence based on the existing RVs. This leaves open the possibility that TOI-1839 b may have reached its current orbit through HEM or through alternative pathways, including disk-driven migration or in situ formation, highlighting the need for additional observa-tions and occurrence-rate studies to robustly constrain the system architectures near the lower desert edge. The combination of their short orbital periods and the rela-tively early spectral types of their host stars places TOI-426 b and TOI-1839 b among the most highly irradiated sub-Neptunes known to date, making them prime targets for probing plane-tary evolution under extreme irradiation conditions. ∼ 100–1000 Gyr, supporting a steam-world interpretation for all three planets. The survival of highly irradiated volatile-rich sub-Neptunes near the lower edge of the desert is di ffi cult to recon-cile with a classical picture in which this boundary is set solely by the evaporation of primordial H 2/He envelopes, and instead favours a scenario in which water-rich steam worlds can persist in this region. Future work will be required to assess the long-term stability of the lower edge of the desert against the evolution and erosion of steam-rich atmospheres.

Appendix A: Frequency analysis 

> A.1. Transit-like signals in the TESS photometry

We verified the planet candidates reported by the TESS Science Offi ce and searched for additional transit-like signals using the transit least squares algorithm ( TLS; Hippke & Heller 2019). For both targets, we first detrended the photometry using the biweight method (window length of 0.25 d) as implemented in 

wotan (Hippke et al. 2019), and combined the photometry from all sectors into a single light curve. Whenever a transit-like sig-nal with a signal detection e ffi ciency (SDE) greater than 10 was identified, we masked the photometry within 3 h of each mid-transit time and iteratively re-ran the TLS search. For TOI-426, we detected TOI-426.01 ( P ≈ 1.32 d) with an SDE of 13.8, and no additional significant signals were found in subsequent iterations. For TOI-1839, we first identified TOI-1839.01 ( P ≈ 1.42 d) with an SDE of 44.7. In the second iter-ation, we detected an additional transit-like signal with a period of P ≈ 4.02 d and an SDE of 45.9, which we denote as TOI-1839.02. This transit signal has also been independently identi-fied and statistically validated by Lafarga et al. (2026). No fur-ther signals were identified in the third iteration. 

> A.2. Stellar activity signals in the WASP photometry

We searched the WASP data for activity signals using the gener-alised Lomb-Scargle periodogram ( GLS ; Zechmeister & Kürster 2009). In Figs. C.1 and C.2, we show the light curves, season-by-season periodograms, and phase-folded photometry. False-alarm probabilities (FAPs) were empirically derived using a bootstrap approach (e.g. Politis & Romano 1994; Hara et al. 2022). The TOI-426 photometry exhibits a persistent modulation with a period of 12.6–13.1 d and an amplitude of 5–10 ppt. The signal is highly significant in 2012 and 2014 (FAP < 0.1%), sig-nificant in 2009 (0 .1% < FAP < 1%), and tentative in 2013 (FAP = 9%). In 2008, the second harmonic (6.2 d, 3.4 ppt, FAP 

= 0.15%) dominates the periodogram. We note that TOI-426 and its nearby companion TOI-949 lie within the photometric aper-ture, leading to dilution of the observed amplitudes. In particular, the WASP data alone do not allow us to determine which of the two stars is responsible for the detected modulation. TOI-1839 shows a stable modulation with a periodicity of 21.1–23.6 d and an amplitude of 5–6 ppt. The signal is highly significant in 2009 and 2010, and significant in 2011. In 2013 and 2014, the peri-odograms show no significant peaks (FAPs > 10%), which may be due to either the loss of photometric precision after the 2012 lens replacement or to a lower stellar activity level. Overall, TOI-426 and TOI-1839 exhibit significant and long-lived photometric modulations at ≈ 13 and 22 d, respectively, which might trace their stellar rotation periods. In App. A.3, we use the HARPS data to further assess the persistence of these signals and, in the case of TOI-426, to confirm their origin. 

> A.3. Stellar activity and planetary signals in the HARPS data

We also used the GLS periodogram to search the HARPS data for planetary and activity signals. In Figs. C.3 and C.4, we show the periodograms of the linearly de-trended RVs and four activity indicators (FWHM, BIS, H α, and S index) of TOI-426 and TOI-1839, respectively. The CCF contrast and the NaD doublet show a weaker response to stellar activity in our data set and were therefore not considered. The Ca index is largely redundant with the S index, so it was also excluded from the analysis. For TOI-426, the periodograms of the indicators H α, S in-dex, and FWHM show maximum power periods of 12.8 d, which correspond to highly significant signals (FAPs ≪ 0.1%). The second harmonic arises as the main periodicity for the BIS in-dicator (6.3 d; FAP = 0.2%). This behaviour is commonly ob-served for the BIS, which traces asymmetries in the CCF rather than bulk Doppler shifts. Stellar active regions can induce line-profile distortions that change sign twice per stellar rotation, often producing BIS variations dominated by the second har-monic of the rotation period ( Prot /2; e.g. Queloz et al. 2001; Boisse et al. 2011; Dumusque et al. 2014). This recurrent and highly significant 12.8-d periodicity coincides with the signal detected in the WASP photometry (Sect. A.2), indicating the ex-istence of an activity-related signal most likely caused by the ro-tation of TOI-426. The RV periodogram shows the same 12.8-d signal with a significant FAP of 0.07%, indicating that an im-portant component of the RV variations has a stellar origin. We also note that the RV periodogram of TOI-426 shows a tentative peak (FAP > 10%) at the 1.3-d periodicity of the TESS candidate TOI-426.01 with no counterpart in the indicators, suggesting that its signature is embedded in the RVs. For TOI-1839, the periodograms of H α, FWHM, and S in-dex show maximum power periods between 22 and 24 d with FAPs between 0.6% ( S index) and 3 × 10 −7% (H α). The second harmonic also arises as the main periodicity for the BIS indica-tor (12.2 d; FAP = 0.07%). This 22-d periodicity coincides with the signal detected in the WASP photometry (Sect. A.2) and the main periodicity in the RV time series (FAP of 5 × 10 −4%), indi-cating that stellar rotation also plays an important role in shaping the RV variability of TOI-1839. As shown in Fig. C.3, the RVs of TOI-426 show a strong up-ward trend, whereas the FWHMs exhibit a mild downward trend. Since long-term variations caused by stellar magnetic cycles of-ten produce trends of the same sign in the RVs and FWHMs (e.g. Lovis et al. 2011), the opposite signs observed in TOI-426 suggest that the RV trend might instead be produced by a long-period companion. Along these lines, we note that the three most recent RV measurements deviate significantly from the linear trend, while these measurements broadly follow the downward tendency in all the activity indicators, further suggesting that the long-term behaviours of the RVs and activity indicators are un-correlated. For TOI-1839, we observe mild trends in the RVs and FWHMs with the same sign (Fig. C.4), preventing us from drawing conclusions about their possible origin. 

Appendix B: Interior structure and atmospheric escape of TOI-426 b, TOI-1839 b, and TOI-1839 c 

> B.1. Interior modelling

We computed the bulk compositions of TOI-426 b, TOI-1839 b, and TOI-1839 c considering three classes of interior structures: (a) gas dwarfs with 1 × solar metallicity envelopes, (b) volatile-enriched envelopes with 50 × solar metallicity, and (c) envelopes composed of pure H 2O in a steam or supercritical state. For the hydrogen-dominated cases, we used the grid of interior structure models from Tang et al. (2025). For steam worlds, we adopted the models from Aguichine et al. (2025a). The original grid ap-plies to planets with equilibrium temperatures between 500 and 700 K, while an extended grid covering 400–1500 K is available at Aguichine et al. (2025b). Both model grids were smoothly connected to the Earth-like radius from Zeng et al. (2016) in the limit of zero envelope mass fraction, following Piaulet et al. (2021). Both model families describe the thermal evolution of 

> Article number, page 16

A. Castro-González et al.: Extremely irradiated volatile-rich sub-Neptunes in TOI-426 and TOI-1839 

planets composed of an Earth-like core surrounded by a volatile envelope, which experience a hot start during formation and sub-sequently cool by radiating energy through the atmosphere. As the interior loses heat, the planet contracts and its radius de-creases with time. These are fully coupled atmosphere–interior models, as they simultaneously track the thermal energy stored in the interior and the rate at which this energy is transported through and lost from the atmosphere. The planetary compositions were inferred within a Bayesian framework using MCMC sampling. We adopted Gaussian pri-ors on the planet mass and equilibrium temperature, as reported in Table D.5, and a uniform prior on the system age within the 1σ interval derived from gyrochronology (Sect. 3.2, Table 1). The observed planetary radius was incorporated as a Gaussian term in the likelihood function L. For the steam-world mod-els (Aguichine et al. 2025a), we assumed a uniform prior on the bulk water mass fraction (WMF) between 0 and 100%. For the gas-dwarf models (Tang et al. 2025), we sampled the enve-lope mass fraction using the transformed variable y = ln fenv 

and included the corresponding Jacobian term, ln |d fenv /dy| =

y, in the log-likelihood ln L, ensuring an e ff ectively uniform prior in fenv . The MCMC sampling was performed with emcee

B.2. Atmospheric escape 

We computed and integrated the atmospheric mass-loss rates of TOI-426 b, TOI-1839 b, and TOI-1839 c following the method-ology presented by Aguichine et al. (2021). Over the system life-time, atmospheric escape is primarily regulated by the evolution of the stellar XUV irradiation that drives photoevaporation. We therefore integrated the mass-loss rates assuming fixed plane-tary mass, radius, and equilibrium temperature, while allowing the stellar XUV luminosity to evolve with time. To account for model-dependent uncertainties, we computed mass-loss rates us-ing di ff erent prescriptions: the analytical fit from Owen & Wu (2017) (OW17), the analytical fit from Salz et al. (2016) (S16), and the grid-based calculations from Kubyshkina et al. (2018); Kubyshkina & Fossati (2021) (K21). Uncertainties were esti-mated through Monte Carlo sampling with 10 4 realisations, adopting the same priors on planetary mass, radius, equilibrium temperature, and age as in the interior modelling. The results are summarised in Table B.1. The OW17 prescription, which rep-resents an approximate fit to numerical simulations, yields sys-tematically lower escape e ffi ciencies, whereas the S16 and K21 prescriptions provide mutually consistent results. In the gas-dwarf scenario (i.e. H 2/He-dominated envelopes), the total mass lost over the system lifetime exceeds the present-day envelope mass ( ∆M ≫ Menv ), implying that primordial envelopes would be e ffi ciently removed. Using the S16 and K21 prescriptions, the corresponding mass-loss timescales are of order ∼ 1–100 Myr. The OW17 prescription yields longer timescales ( ∼ 0.1–2 Gyr), although these should be interpreted with caution given the limitations of the model. In the steam-world scenario, no dedicated grids of H 2Oescape rates are currently available. We therefore estimated present-day escape rates using Wind-AE (Broome et al. 2025). This model solves the atmospheric structure above the optical transit radius, and the mass-loss rate corresponds to the mass outflow at the sonic point.
