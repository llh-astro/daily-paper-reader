Abstract 

Bailey & Malhotra (2009; BM09) assigned dynamical classes to individual Centaurs using 2007 Minor Planet Center orbital elements, including a single quasi-stable (Q) designation for 2005 TH173 based on a 17-day observational arc. We revisit the BM09 classification scheme using modern JPL Small-Body Database orbital elements (snapshot 2026-07-26), REBOUND WHFast integrations, and diagonal clone ensembles to quantify the relative contributions of orbital uncertainty and intrinsic chaotic divergence to the spread in dynamical lifetimes. For Centaurs with observational arcs of ≥ 30 d, the within-object dispersion in log 10 lifetime is 0.54 dex, corre-sponding to a factor of 3.5 in lifetime. This dispersion remains independent of the relative semimajor-axis uncertainty, 

σa/a, over seven orders of magnitude once ( a, e, i, q) is fixed ( n = 65 complete cases; n = 96 using a Kaplan–Meier estimator). Semimajor axis and inclination account for the observed dispersion, whereas measurement quality does not. We find no evidence for a dependence steeper than 0.034 dex per decade in σa/a, placing an upper bound of 0.24 dex on the observationally reducible component relative to the intrinsic floor. Among 970 catalogued Centaurs with available covariance information, 560 fail the q > 5.2 AU membership criterion. Of the remaining 410 genuine Centaurs, 355 already lie at the inferred dynamical floor, indicating that continued astrometric observations are more likely to secure reliable orbital solutions than to substantially sharpen existing dynamical classifications. gApplying the same framework to the BM09 sample confirms the robustness of the original classifications. Among 50 comparable designations, the D-class assignments are reproduced at the 98% level, with only 2005 TH173 changing class, from Q to D. None of the 30 multi-opposition 2007 orbits changes classification. Two short-arc objects leave the Centaur region under modern orbital solutions: SN55 reaches q = 35 .5 AU, while 2002 FY36 moves beyond a = 30 AU. The rejection of the Q classification for 2005 TH173, whose modern orbit is Uranus-crossing ( e ≈ 0.31) and yields a median dynamical lifetime of 2.99 Myr at 10 Myr integration time, provides the clearest individual example. These results demonstrate that, for well-observed Centaurs, intrinsic dynamical chaos rather than current astrometric uncertainty sets the dominant limit on lifetime-based classification. 

Keywords: Centaurs, Celestial mechanics, Kuiper belt, Trans-neptunian objects, Comets, dynamics

Centaurs occupy heliocentric orbits with perihelia beyond Jupiter and semimajor axes interior to Nep-tune, with survey-dependent boundaries (Gladman et al., 2008). They link the trans-Neptunian region to Jupiter-family comets (Levison and Duncan, 1997; Duncan and Levison, 1997; Volk and Malhotra, 2008; Dones et al.,

Two qualitative modes recur: random-walk di ff usion of the semimajor axis and inter-mittent sticking in mean-motion resonances (Tiscareno and Malhotra, 2003; Di Sisto and Brunini, 2007). Bailey & Malhotra (Bailey and Malhotra, 2009), here-after BM09, turned that split into a per-object classifier. Using Hurst analysis of a(t) and an empirical longevity cut near 22 Myr, they defined a di ff using class (D), a resonance-hopping class (R), and a quasi-stable class arXiv:2609.08710v1 [astro-ph.EP] 8 Sep 2026 (Q). Their Table 2 initial conditions came from the MPC on 2007 March 6, including single-opposition arcs of only days. One object, 2005 TH173, was their only Q Centaur: a nearly circular orbit between Saturn and Uranus on a 17-day arc, occupying the low-e Saturn– Uranus niche they had flagged as a possible long-lived pocket. A classifier of that kind is only as current as its initial conditions. Subsequent work mapped Centaur sources, ensemble lifetimes, and end states without repeating BM09’s taxonomy on the same designations. The scat-tered disk remains the main prograde feeder (Duncan and Levison, 1997; Di Sisto and Brunini, 2007; Dones et al., 2015; Di Sisto and Rossignoli, 2020); plutino leakage is secondary (Di Sisto et al., 2010); the Oort cloud supplies a high-inclination tail (Emel’yanenko et al., 2005; Brasser et al., 2012); Kuiper-belt leakage is dy-namically colder (Duncan et al., 1995). Mean lifetimes in the Centaur zone are a few Myr, lengthening at high inclination and larger perihelion (Horner et al., 2004; Di Sisto and Rossignoli, 2020). Active and inactive Cen-taurs di ff er in lifetime and in whether they prefer JFC or Halley-type end states (Jewitt, 2009; Fernández et al., 2018). Most JFCs are argued to pass through a low-e

Gateway exterior to Jupiter (Sarid et al., 2019; Roberts and Muñoz-Gutiérrez, 2021). Individual clone studies find the same few-Myr scale, with a sticking tail (Wood et al., 2018). A minority of Centaurs reach the terrestrial-planet region (Galiazzo et al., 2019). OSSOS-calibrated models match the observed inclination distribution at survey level (Nesvorný et al., 2019). None of those stud-ies re-ran BM09’s Table-2 labels on modern osculating elements. This article addresses two linked questions. First, how much of the residual spread in a Centaur clone ensemble is set by orbit-determination quality and how much by intrinsic chaotic divergence—a bound on what contin-ued astrometry (including Rubin /LSST follow-up) can actually stabilize. Second, whether BM09’s Table-2 la-bels still attach to the same designations under a stated modern pipeline (JPL SBDB elements, snapshot 2026-07-26; REBOUND WHFast; diagonal clone ensembles). The reclassification is a sensitivity study, not a bit-for-bit reproduction of BM09’s integrator, planetary model, escape treatment, or Hurst classifier. A label change under our pipeline therefore does not, by itself, establish that modern astrometry caused it: orbit revision, numeri-cal setup, escape cuts, and classifier di ff erences can all contribute (Table 1; Experiment C in Section 3.6). Pro-duction population tables use classifier v2 (lifetime-first); an earlier Hurst-gated rule (v1) is retained for sensitivity comparison (Section 2.4.1). Our operational v2 R is not equivalent to BM09’s morphological R (Section 3.4.2); we do not claim that BM09’s R assignments were wrong. The defensible BM09 headline is narrower: under our stated rule, published labels do not generally reproduce. The BM09 census and the 2005 TH173 case study serve as empirical checks on the dispersion bound: where 2007 orbits were already multi-opposition, no label moves. Known omissions—full-covariance Q /R tables for the ten-object sample, terrestrial planets, SPK-forced giants, a re-derived 22 Myr cut—are listed in Section 5. A 20-clone TH173 covariance Q-test is reported there. 

2. Methods 

2.1. Study design and relation to BM09 

BM09 assigned per-clone labels from Hurst analysis of a(t) and an empirical longevity cut near 22 Myr. Our work asks whether those published labels still attach to the same designations when (i) initial conditions are updated to modern SBDB solutions and (ii) lifetimes are integrated with a stated modern N-body setup. A BM09 →modern label change therefore reflects orbit re-vision, integration /classifier di ff erences, or both; it is not, by itself, evidence that BM09’s original analysis was incorrect on its 2007 inputs. Table 1 lists components that di ff er from BM09’s pub-lished implementation and their expected e ff ect on labels. We treat these as explicit experimental choices rather than hidden corrections. 

2.2. Integrator and planetary model 

We integrate the Sun and the four giant planets with REBOUND’s WHFast symplectic integrator (Rein and Liu, 2012; Rein and Tamayo, 2015), timestep dt =

0.25 yr. WHFast is a Wisdom–Holman mixed-variable map (Wisdom and Holman, 1991) in the REBOUND implementation; we use it as the production workhorse because BM09-style Centaur lifetimes are set by giant-planet scattering on Myr scales, not by terrestrial close-encounter resolution. Giant-planet heliocentric states come from JPL DE441 (Park et al., 2021) at epoch J2000, transformed from ICRF to the ecliptic of J2000, then evolved freely with the massless Centaur. There is no continuous SPK forcing of the planets during the Centaur integration. DE441 is a planetary ephemeris, not a Centaur force model: once the giants are initial-ized, their subsequent motion is the four-planet N-body problem. 2.3. Centaur initial conditions and clones 

Modern osculating elements are queried from the JPL SBDB API (snapshot date 2026-07-26). Designations are matched to BM09 Table 2 by provisional name string. For the two headline OD cases we also record permanent identifiers: 2005 TH173 remains unnumbered (18 ob-servations, 366 d arc, condition code 6), and 1995 SN55 is identical to numbered TNO (523731) 2014 OK394 (MPC linkage published 2021). When element uncertainties are available we draw in-dependent Gaussian clones on the diagonal one-sigma uncertainties only—SBDB cross-covariances are not used in production tables. This is a reproducible sen-sitivity baseline, not a sample from the full observa-tional posterior. For chaotic Uranus-crossing objects (TH173) and still-short-arc solutions with large formal errors (QP112: σa ∼ 5.8 AU, σe ∼ 0.31 in the 2026-07-26 snapshot), ignoring element correlations can mis-represent which ( a, e, i) combinations are jointly accessi-ble: diagonal cloning explores axis-aligned perturbations that may include dynamically unlikely corners of phase space, while correlated clones would concentrate along the principal uncertainty directions. That distinction af-fects clone fractions and minority R /Q channels more than the TH173 Q headline, because 80 /100 production clones escape before 10 Myr on the modern orbit and the nominal clone (index 0) is itself short-lived. A 20-clone TH173 ensemble drawn from the published SBDB covariance ( Tmax = 10 Myr; seed 87) is reported in Sec-tion 3.3 as a Q-rejection check only. Modest floors apply if a sigma is missing. Clone index 0 is the exact nominal solution. The random seed is 87 for all ensemble runs reported here. Clone counts are chosen to match the inferential goal, not to deliver population-statistics precision. With n =

20, one clone is a 5% change in reported fraction; with 

n = 5, one clone is 20%. Modal classes can therefore appear stable while minority fractions remain poorly resolved. We report clone counts (D /R/Q) alongside modes and do not quote confidence intervals that would require additional bootstrap runs not performed here. 2.4.1. Classifier v1 versus v2: chronology and sensitivity 

Development followed a fixed integration pipeline; only the post-integration label rule changed. We first im-plemented a Hurst-gated tree (v1) that assigns D when-ever the linear Hurst fit is su ffi ciently good ( R2 ≥ 0.85), even for clones surviving ≥22 Myr. All exploratory Q /Rpilot integrations (20 clones, 40 Myr) were completed under v1 and yielded modal D for 10 /10 objects versus BM09—a 100% modal-flip rate that would overstate orbit-driven reclassification. A stop-check reclassified the same archived pilot lifetimes and Hurst diagnostics under v2 without re-integration. Under v2 the pilot was modal D for 7 /10 objects; FZ53, QP112, and RO43 recovered modal R (Ta-ble 2). We adopted v2 for all production tables because it implements BM09’s stated lifetime-first ordering (Hurst quantities remain diagnostics, not a gate that suppresses R on long-lived linear survivors). The choice was not pre-registered; it is documented here as a sensitivity be-tween two operational readings of BM09’s logic, not as tuning to improve headline flip rates.

2.5. Dispersion versus orbit quality 

To separate observational from intrinsic uncertainty we pool the clone ensembles integrated for this article— 172 objects from a stratified SBDB Centaur survey (10 clones, Tmax = 25 Myr, with 40 Myr extensions for cen-sored clones) plus 61 BM09 Table-2 objects (10 clones, 25 Myr, with 40 Myr extensions where needed)—for 219 unique designations (five overlap). Where an object ap-pears in more than one campaign, the longest integration supersedes the shorter one. The outcome variable is the within-object spread of log 10 lifetime across clones. For ensembles with no right-censored survivors we use the sample standard deviation of log 10 lifetime. For ensembles with censored clones we estimate the interquartile range of a Kaplan–Meier sur-vival curve in log 10 lifetime and divide by 1.349. Objects whose clones were removed before the first output step (t = 0) are excluded: their zero lifetimes reflect the es-cape cut, not dynamical dispersion. The analysis sample 4restricts to genuine Centaurs ( q > 5.2 AU, a < 30 .07 AU) with at least 30 d of astrometry, so that the nominal orbit is more than a single-apparition detection. The expected null is Lyapunov-time saturation, not a claim of new physics. Giant-planet-crossing Centaurs are strongly chaotic (Bailey and Malhotra, 2009; Gladman, 1997); their Lyapunov times are typically 10 3–10 5 yr (Tancredi and Simó, 1994; Tancredi et al., 2001; Wood et al., 2018), far shorter than our 25–40 Myr windows. Once integration extends well past the Lyapunov time, nearby clones lose memory of their initial separation and the spread of outcomes is set by the accessible chaotic phase space, not by σa/a (Holman and Wisdom, 1993; Shevchenko and Scholl, 2000). We treat the flat relation as a population-scale measurement of that saturation for clone-lifetime dispersion, not as the discovery of chaos itself. Orbit quality is measured by the relative semimajor-axis uncertainty σa/a from the SBDB covariance. The primary specification regresses dispersion on log 10 (σa/a) while conditioning on the object’s osculat-ing ( a, e, i, q), because the osculating elements indepen-dently predict dispersion and are entangled with observ-ability (planet-crossing objects are brighter and more strongly scattered). The inferential headline is the ex-cluded slope from this regression (95% CI). Kaplan– Meier dispersion, partial Spearman checks, a lifetime in-teraction term, and planet-crossing /detached splits were specified as sensitivity checks around the same null while the dispersion analysis was drafted; they were not pre-registered outside this project and test one hypothesis repeatedly rather than independent alternatives. Catalog projection applies the same membership and arc cuts to all 970 SBDB Centaurs with a usable covariance. 

2.6. Lyapunov-time proxy pilot 

To connect the saturation floor to a standard chaotic timescale without finite-time Lyapunov integrations on every object, we run a stratified pilot on 39 chaos-floor objects (10 diagonal clones, seed 87, 

Tmax = 25 Myr; one intended designation lacks a catalog match). While clones remain bound, we record osculating semimajor axis at snapshots t ∈{0.002 , 0.005 , 0.01 , 0.05 , 0.1, 0.25 , 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10 } Myr. The ensemble relative spread std( a)/⟨a⟩ grows approxi-mately exponentially in the first 0 .1 Myr; we estimate a proxy time τproxy as the geometric mean of pairwise growth rates in that early window, anchoring the initial spread to σa/a from the SBDB covariance. This is not a finite-time Lyapunov exponent from vari-ational equations or MEGNO: τproxy measures how fast a 

finite diagonal clone ensemble—seeded at observational uncertainty—diverges in osculating a, not the intrinsic tangent-vector growth rate of an infinitesimal perturba-tion on the nominal orbit. Sensitivity of ensemble spread to σa/a can partly reflect how orbit uncertainty maps onto phase-space geometry even when intrinsic chaos is fixed; the population correlation with floor height is the inferential target. Order-of-magnitude scaling is checked on three literature anchors only (Chiron, Pholus, 2005 TH173), using an explicit factor-of-four tolerance rela-tive to cited point estimates (Section 3.2).

3. Results 

3.1. Classification uncertainty and the chaos floor 

For Centaurs whose orbit rests on more than 30 d of astrometry, the spread of clone lifetimes is set by the object’s dynamical state and not by how well that state is measured—the signature of Lyapunov-time saturation applied to clone ensembles (Section 2.5). Pooling the 219 ensembles, 101 designations fail the Centaur mem-bership test ( q > 5.2 AU, a < 30 .07 AU); 21 of those register zero lifetime for every clone because perihelion lies inside the integrator’s q < 2.5 AU removal cut and carry no dynamical information. Among the 118 genuine Centaurs, 52 ensembles retain at least one clone alive at the integration cap. The complete-case sample ( n = 65 Centaurs with no censored clones) spans log 10 (σa/a) from −7.5 to −0.5— seven orders of magnitude in relative uncertainty—and observation arcs from 54 to 47 770 d. Mean dispersion is 0.54 dex (95% CI 0 .50–0 .58; median 0 .51), equivalent to a factor of 3.5 in lifetime even when σa/a approaches one part in 3 ×10 7 (Chiron-grade orbits). Conditioning on (a, e, i, q), the coe ffi cient on log 10 (σa/a) is −0.002 dex per decade (95% CI −0.034 to +0.030; p = 0.91). Semi-major axis and inclination predict dispersion ( p = 0.045 and p = 0.028); semimajor-axis uncertainty does not. Rank correlations after conditioning on state show no dependence on orbit quality ( ρ = −0.055; p = 0.66). The confidence interval excludes any e ff ect steeper than 0.034 dex per decade; across the observed range that bounds the observationally reducible share of dispersion at 0 .24 dex against a 0 .54 dex floor. Improving an orbit across the full observed span cannot change the lifetime spread by more than a factor of ≈ 1.7 under this bound; the point estimate is a factor of ≈ 1.0. A censoring-aware Kaplan–Meier estimator on 96 objects (three excluded at t = 0; four with undefined interquartile range) agrees: mean dispersion 0 .59 dex (median 0 .56); coe ffi cient on log 10 (σa/a) after condi-tioning on state +0.019 dex per decade (95% CI −0.023 to +0.062; p = 0.37). Inclination remains the only sig-nificant predictor ( p = 0.015). A lifetime interaction is absent ( p = 0.78): orbit quality does not matter more for long-lived objects than for short-lived ones once cen-sored ensembles are handled correctly. Splitting at the median lifetime gives coe ffi cients +0.031 ( p = 0.38; 

n = 48) and +0.008 ( p = 0.77; n = 48) respectively. The floor height depends on dynamical class. Planet-crossing Centaurs ( q below the sample median) show mean dispersion 0 .58 dex; detached Centaurs show 0.50 dex. Neither subgroup depends on σa/a (p > 0.57). Figure 1 plots dispersion against log 10 (σa/a); panel (b) shows the flat relation after conditioning on ( a, e, i, q), with dashed lines marking slopes excluded at 95%. Projecting onto the SBDB Centaur catalogue ( n =

61046; 970 with a usable covariance), 560 objects fail the q > 5.2 AU membership test—median perihelion 3.41 AU among failures, because SBDB’s class flag is on semimajor axis alone. Of the 410 genuine Centaurs, 355 already have arcs ≥ 30 d and therefore sit on the chaos floor; only 55 lack a real orbit yet. Continued astrometry can give those 55 a first dynamical solution, but it cannot, under this bound, reduce the classification dispersion of the majority that already have one. 

3.2. Lyapunov proxy and floor height 

On the pilot sample ( n = 39), τproxy spans ≈ 7 × 10 2–7 × 10 4 yr. Order-of-magnitude scaling is checked on three literature anchors only ( n = 3): Chiron (6 .3 ×

10 3 yr vs. literature ∼ 1.1 × 10 4 yr; ratio 0.6), Pholus (1 .9 × 10 4 yr vs. ∼ 5 × 10 3 yr; ratio 3.8), and TH173 (2 .4 × 10 4 yr vs. ∼ 8 × 10 3 yr; ratio 3.0). 4. Discussion 

4.1. What astrometry can and cannot fix 

The dispersion analysis (Section 3.1) answers a ques-tion that a class-based fragility survey cannot, because at Tmax = 25 Myr almost every object is modal D and the outcome variable has no variance (Appendix Ap-pendix B). Measuring the spread of clone lifetimes instead of a threshold-gated label recovers a floor of 

≈ 0.54 dex—a factor of ≈ 3.5 in lifetime—that is inde-pendent of σa/a across seven orders of magnitude once the object’s ( a, e, i, q) is fixed. This is the first population-scale quantification, for the known Centaur census, of Lyapunov-time saturation expressed as clone-lifetime 12 Figure 8: BM09 Table-2 → modern SBDB ( a, e) revision arrows for the ten Q /R objects with SBDB matches, plus SN55 and VR130 from the D census. Arrow tails: BM09 ( a, e); heads: modern SBDB (2026-07-26). TH173 and SN55 show the largest eccentricity and semimajor-axis revisions in the sample. 

dispersion: the mechanism is standard in celestial me-chanics (Gladman, 1997; Tancredi and Simó, 1994; Tan-credi et al., 2001), but the actionable number—how many published designations already sit on the floor versus still need a first orbit—has not been published. Semimajor axis and inclination set the floor height; orbit quality does not. The bound is not a proof that astrometry never helps: the confidence interval permits up to 0 .24 dex of reducible dispersion, but the point estimate is essentially zero for objects with real orbits. That result reframes Rubin /LSST follow-up for Cen-taur dynamical classification. Of 970 catalogued objects with a covariance, 560 fail a perihelion-based member-ship test because SBDB’s Centaur flag is on semimajor axis alone; among the 410 genuine Centaurs, 355 already have arcs ≥ 30 d and therefore sit on the floor. Observers gain most by securing first orbits for the 55 preliminary objects, not by refining classifications that are already chaos-limited. Where an object still lacks a month of astrometry, orbit revision can move the nominal—as TH173 and SN55 demonstrate—but that is a di ff erent regime from sharpening an existing ensemble. The BM09 census confirms the prediction in the com-plementary direction. None of the 30 comparable multi-opposition objects changes class; all three events (one reclassification, two membership departures) fall among short-arc 2007 entries. That is the pattern expected if la-bel instability is orbit-determination-limited only before a real orbit exists and chaos-limited afterward. 

4.2. What changed and why 

BM09 assigned 2005 TH173 as their sole Q Cen-taur from a 17-day, nearly circular Saturn–Uranus orbit. Under the 2026 SBDB solution that object is Uranus-crossing and loses clones on a ∼3 Myr median timescale (Section 3.3). Its Q label does not survive a modern orbit under any lifetime-faithful classifier. For the ten BM09 Q /R objects with modern SBDB matches, modal class is D under classifier v2 in 20-clone, 40 Myr runs, while substantial R minorities remain (Ta-ble 7; aggregate D =155, R =43, Q =2). RO43 is a tied en-semble (10 D / 10 R); QP112 carries 7 R and 2 Q among 20 clones. The Table-2 census (Section 3.4.2) places TH173 in context. Across the 50 designations that remain Centaurs on modern elements and are comparable on the D /Q axis, TH173 is the only class change; the D class reproduces at 98%. That is the useful framing for a single-object result: the pipeline agrees with BM09 almost everywhere it can be compared, so the disagreement at TH173 is not an artefact of a systematically divergent method. The census also shows the limits of the exercise. Two designations have left the Centaur population entirely, and BM09’s R class cannot be tested at all under a lifetime-gated rule. 13 These are reclassifications of a published Table-2 sam-ple under stated initial conditions and a stated pipeline. They are not a new Centaur population synthesis and do not imply that every historical R label should become D. 

4.3. Orbit determination versus pipeline choice 

Two distinct sources can move a BM09 label: updated orbital elements and di ff erences in integrator, escape cuts, integration length, or classifier implementation. Orbit determination drives the clearest cases. TH173’s Q rejection follows from the modern Uranus-crossing orbit and early escape; it does not require Experiment C. SN55 is the complementary case and the more instructive one: its 7708-day arc moves it out of the Centaur zone altogether, so the modal Q the classifier returns is a statement about a stable trans-Neptunian orbit and not a rediscovered Centaur Q niche. Both objects entered BM09’s table on arcs of a few tens of days. VR130 and UW292 illustrate smaller but still meaningful element revisions paired with modal changes or large R-fraction shifts (Table 8; Figure 8). The census sharpens this into a testable contrast. Of the 30 comparable objects whose 2007 orbits already rested on multi-opposition arcs, none changes class and none leaves the population; all three changes in the sam-ple occur among the 20 short-arc objects. Three events cannot establish a rate, and the classification split alone is not significant (Fisher exact p = 0.17). What the null control does support is narrower and still useful: where BM09’s initial conditions were well determined, this pipeline reproduces their labels, so the disagreements cannot be attributed to method alone. Pipeline and classifier choices matter separately (Section 3.6). Experiment C holds the modern DE441 +WHFast setup fixed and swaps BM09 versus modern ( a, e, i); published R /Q labels are not recovered automatically from 2009 elements alone, and branch-dependent flips (2 /10 under v2) do not isolate orbit re-vision as the sole cause of ensemble modal changes. Classifier v1’s Hurst gate would have labelled long-lived QP112 survivors as D; v2 was adopted after a stop-check because it follows BM09’s lifetime-first ordering (Ta-ble 2). That choice was not pre-registered and changes some Q /R modes (pilot modal D 10 /10 under v1 ver-sus 7 /10 under v2), but it does not a ff ect TH173’s Q rejection. Across the Q /R ten, Spearman correlations between absolute element revisions and R clone fraction are weak (n = 10): |∆a| versus R frac ρ = −0.08; |∆e| versus R frac ρ = −0.25; log 10 (modern arc days) versus R frac 

ρ = −0.27. Large OD revisions explain TH173 and SN55; they do not rank-order R minority size across the sample. 

4.4. Interpreting historical dynamical classifications 

BM09’s map—short-lived di ff usion (D), long-lived resonance hopping (R), and long-lived flat-a quasi-stability (Q)—remains a useful qualitative framework (Tiscareno and Malhotra, 2003; Di Sisto and Brunini, 2007; Bailey and Malhotra, 2009). What this work shows is that applying that framework to specific designations is sensitive to the orbital solution available at classifica-tion time and to operational choices in the integrator and classifier. The 2007 nearly circular TH173 niche was already flagged by BM09 as a possible long-lived pocket; the modern solution places the object among ordinary Uranus-crossing Centaurs (Figure 4), with a median life-time consistent with the established few-Myr Centaur scale (Horner et al., 2004; Di Sisto and Rossignoli, 2020). Rejecting Q is therefore the expected outcome for the modern orbit, not evidence that BM09’s taxonomy is invalid in general. For RO43 and QP112, the sticking tail that BM09 encoded as R persists in clone minorities even when the mode shifts to D under v2. Horner et al.’s longest-lived Centaur (2000 FZ53; half-life 32 Myr on 2004 elements) is modal D on 2026 elements (16 D / 4 R), showing that a published long half-life does not guarantee modal R after an orbit update. A Mercurius encounter check on 20 TH173 clones confirms class-level Q rejection (0 Q under both integra-tors; ρ ≈ − 0.44 on per-clone lifetimes). That test ad-dresses integrator choice for the headline Q call; it does not certify per-clone trajectory agreement in a chaotic regime. 

4.5. What this work does not imply 

We do not claim bit-for-bit reproduction of BM09’s integrator or Hurst code; v2 is a lifetime-first operational reading of their logic. We do not claim a universal law linking arc length or element revision to R fraction: the appendix arc-mixture scan is exploratory and object-dependent (Section Appendix A), and the class-based fragility survey of Appendix Appendix B had no vari-ance to test. We do not claim that better orbits can never reduce classification dispersion—the bound permits up to 0 .24 dex of reducible spread—only that no such re-duction is detected across seven orders of magnitude in 

σa/a for objects with real orbits. We do not claim that BM09’s R assignments are wrong; our rule cannot test 14 them. We do not read the 98% D reproduction as valida-tion of BM09’s method or of ours beyond the D /Q axis on which they can be compared. We do not treat three arc-correlated events as a measured contamination rate for short-arc classifications. We do not equate censored survivors at Tmax < 22 Myr with confirmed D or R la-bels. We do not estimate JFC injection rates, Gateway occupancy, or terrestrial-impact probabilities from these ensembles. 

5. Limitations 

Several limits bound interpretation; each is stated once here and referenced elsewhere only briefly. 1. Diagonal SBDB clones. Production Q /R tables resample independent Gaussian perturbations on one-sigma element uncertainties, not the full SBDB covariance. A 20-clone TH173 covariance check at 10 Myr still returns 0 Q (15 early escapes, 5 cen-sored). Full-covariance clones could still change minority R /Q fractions for short-arc objects such as QP112. 2. Confounded BM09 comparison. Orbit elements, integrator, escape cuts, and classifier all change relative to BM09; a modern label change is not at-tributable to astrometry alone. Experiment C (Sec-tion 3.6) partially isolates orbit revision but does not remove classifier or integrator di ff erences from the full census. 3. Finite integration windows and censoring. 

TH173 production at 10 Myr rejects Q but does not resolve R for 20 /100 censored survivors. A 100-clone 40 Myr run on the same seed yields 88 D /

12 R / 0 Q. Q /R baseline tables remain the 20-clone, 40 Myr matrix. 4. Post-hoc classifier v2. v2 was adopted after a v1 stop-check, not pre-registered. It changes some Q/R modes but not TH173’s Q rejection under any lifetime-faithful rule. 5. Census depth ( n = 10 ). The Table-2 census uses ten clones per object, so a single clone is a 10% shift in the reported fractions and modal class is decided by narrow margins for objects with mixed ensembles. It resolves modal class and censoring, not the fine structure of minority fractions; the 20-clone Q /R matrix remains the deeper sample. 6. R class not testable here. Classifier v2 assigns R only above the 22 Myr cut, whereas BM09’s R is a morphological statement about resonance hopping. The nine BM09 R objects are therefore excluded from the like-for-like comparison rather than re-ported as reclassifications, and this work o ff ers no test of their published labels. A resonance-angle di-agnostic independent of lifetime would be required and was not implemented. 7. Three events drive the arc-quality contrast. 0.17). The 0 /30 multi-opposition control is the more robust half of that comparison. 8. Null population survey superseded. A strati-fied 172-object class-based fragility survey (Ap-pendix Appendix B) had no power because every object was modal D at Tmax = 25 Myr. Section 3.1 replaces it with a dispersion analysis on 219 unique ensembles; the bound there is the substantive null on orbit quality. 9. Observational selection. Across the stratified fragility survey (Appendix Appendix B), median clone lifetime rises with arc length—most naturally read as a selection e ff ect. The chaos-floor sample deliberately excludes that regime: it requires arc 

≥ 30 d, where log 10 (arc) and median lifetime are not significantly correlated ( ρ = −0.14; p = 0.26; 

n = 65), and the null on σa/a survives conditioning on arc length and median lifetime ( p = 0.41 when both are included). Selection on stability cannot ex-plain the flat floor within objects that already have real orbits, though it remains relevant for the 55 preliminary Centaurs below the arc cut. 10. Multiple-comparison exposure. OLS, Kaplan– Meier, partial Spearman, lifetime interaction, and dynamical subgroups all probe the same null—that 

σa/a does not steepen dispersion once ( a, e, i, q) is fixed. We assembled these checks as a mutually consistent sensitivity suite while drafting the disper-sion analysis; they were not preregistered outside this project and are not independent hypothesis tests. The inferential headline is the excluded slope from the primary OLS specification and its 95% CI, not any single ancillary p-value. 11. Lyapunov proxy scope. The pilot checks order-of-magnitude scaling on n = 3 literature anchors with an explicit factor-of-four tolerance; it does not certify τproxy as a variational Lyapunov time. The quantity tracks finite ensemble spread seeded by 

σa/a, so part of the signal is sensitivity to orbit-uncertainty geometry, not purely intrinsic tangent-vector divergence. The floor–proxy correlation ( n =

39) is the powered population test. 15 12. Manuscript scope. The article combines a popu-lation chaos-floor analysis, a pilot ensemble proxy, and a BM09 reclassification census; a referee may reasonably suggest separating the method-focused and application-focused strands. 13. Missing terrestrial planets. The four-giant model follows classical Centaur lifetime experiments; close encounters with Earth /Venus /Mars are not resolved. 14. Appendix arc tiers ( ntiers = 5). Synthetic σ ∝√A/arc scans yield exploratory, object-dependent mixture trends, not survey-planning laws. 

6. Conclusions 

We rank conclusions by evidential strength. 

Robust.. 

1. Centaur classification dispersion sits on a chaos floor. For genuine Centaurs with arc ≥ 30 d, within-object dispersion of log 10 lifetime is ≈ 0.54 dex (fac-tor ≈ 3.5 in lifetime) and does not depend on σa/a

across seven orders of magnitude once ( a, e, i, q)is fixed ( n = 65 complete-case; n = 96 with a Kaplan–Meier estimator). Semimajor axis and in-clination predict dispersion; orbit quality does not. The data exclude any e ff ect steeper than 0 .034 dex per decade of σa/a, bounding the observationally reducible share at 0 .24 dex against the floor. 2. 2005 TH173 is not Q under the modern JPL SBDB orbit. Eighty of 100 clones escape before 10 Myr (confirmed non-Q); 20 are right-censored at Tmax ; median lifetime is 2.99 Myr. A 100-clone 40 Myr run on the same seed yields 88 D / 12 R /

0 Q. A Mercurius check on 20 clones and a 20-clone SBDB-covariance check at 10 Myr both return 0 Q. 

Strong but conditional on stated pipeline.. 

3. Continued astrometry cannot sharpen most ex-isting classifications. Of 970 SBDB Centaurs with a covariance, 560 fail a q > 5.2 AU membership test; 355 of the 410 genuine Centaurs already have arcs ≥ 30 d and sit on the chaos floor. Follow-up is most valuable for securing first orbits among the 55 preliminary objects. 4. The ten BM09 Q /R objects with modern SBDB matches are modal D under classifier v2 (20 clones, 40 Myr; aggregate D =155, R =43, Q =2), with substantial R minorities (notably RO43 and QP112) and rare Q clones (QP112 only). 5. Two BM09 Table-2 objects are no longer Cen-taurs. On modern elements 1995 SN55 =

(523731) 2014 OK394 lies at q = 35 .5 AU and 2002 FY36 at a = 31 .9 AU, outside the Centaur zone on any standard definition. Both were classi-fied in 2007 from arcs of tens of days. This conclu-sion depends on the element solutions and the zone boundaries, not on any classifier choice. 6. Across the comparable Table-2 census, TH173 is the only class change. Of 50 designations that remain Centaurs and can be tested on the D /Q axis, the D class reproduces at 98% and only TH173 moves (Table 6). The agreement elsewhere is what makes the single disagreement interpretable. 7. Where BM09’s orbits were already well deter-mined, nothing changes. None of the 30 com-parable multi-opposition objects changes class or leaves the population; all three changes fall among the 20 short-arc objects. With three events this is a direction, not a rate (Fisher exact p = 0.17), and it matches the chaos-floor prediction. 8. Large orbit revisions accompany the clearest changes (TH173’s Q rejected; SN55 displaced out of the population). Experiment C shows that integra-tor /classifier di ff erences add to, rather than replace, orbit-update e ff ects. 9. A lifetime-gated rule cannot test a morphological R class. None of the nine BM09 R objects is modal R here, and only 53 of 610 census clones carry an R label, because v2 admits R solely above the 22 Myr cut. Comparisons of D /R/Q labels between studies require an explicit resonance diagnostic; the letter alone does not transfer. 

Exploratory or sample-limited.. 

10. Rank correlations between element revision and R fraction across the Q /R ten are weak (n = 10); OD explains case studies, not a global ordering. 11. Class-based orbit-quality fragility is null in a stratified 172-object survey (Appendix Ap-pendix B): the outcome variable had no variance at Tmax = 25 Myr. The dispersion analysis in Sec-tion 3.1 is the powered replacement. 12. Synthetic arc-length scans (appendix) show object-dependent mixture trends at fixed modern nominals; modal class stays D at all tiers for seven test objects.

Appendix B. A null survey of orbit quality against classification fragility 

The appendix above varies uncertainty synthetically at fixed nominals. Before the dispersion analysis of Sec-tion 3.1, we also asked whether real orbit-determination quality predicts class-based fragility across the Centaur population. That design failed for lack of variance in the outcome variable; the negative result is recorded here so that the class-based approach is not repeated uncritically. The substantive bound on orbit quality is the chaos-floor analysis in the main text. 

Design.. From the JPL SBDB Centaur catalogue ( n =

1046, retrieved 2026-08-14) we drew a sample strati-fied on data-arc length, condition code, and estimated number of oppositions, yielding 172 objects spread over 13 strata. Each object received ten diagonal clones to 

Tmax = 25 Myr under classifier v2, seed 87: 1720 integra-tions in total. Predictors were log 10 (arc days), condition code, and opposition count. Outcomes were the minority R/Q clone fraction, disagreement between the nominal clone and the modal class, censoring rate, and the frac-tion of clones with lifetime ≥22 Myr. 

Result.. None of the twelve predictor–outcome pairs is significant. Spearman |ρ| never exceeds 0.12 and the smallest p-value is 0.14 (opposition count versus minor-ity R /Q fraction). The signs are weakly positive, i.e. in 18 the opposite sense to the expectation that poorer orbits give less stable classifications. 

Why the test has no power.. All 172 objects are modal D. Classifier v2 requires survival to 22 Myr before it can emit R or Q, and at Tmax = 25 Myr the median clone lifetime is 0.19 Myr in the short-arc stratum, 0.80 Myr at medium arc, and 2.67 Myr at long arc. Almost no clone reaches the cut, so modal class is constant across the sample and the surviving outcome variables (mi-nority fractions of 0.02–0.06) carry very little variance. A meaningful version of this test needs either a sub-stantially longer Tmax or a fragility metric that does not depend on the 22 Myr threshold. 

A confounder worth recording.. Median clone lifetime rises monotonically with arc length across the three strata. This is most naturally read as an observational selection eff ect—objects on more stable orbits remain observable and accumulate longer arcs—rather than as a dynami-cal relation. That trend operates mainly below the 30-day arc cut used in Section 3.1: within the chaos-floor complete-case sample, arc length and median lifetime are uncorrelated ( ρ = −0.14; p = 0.26), and the null on σa/a survives conditioning on both arc and median lifetime. Any future study correlating arc length with dynamical outcome across the full catalogue must still control for selection; our headline floor is restricted to objects that already have real orbits.
