Tensor-Train Weak SINDy: Identifying High-Dimensional Nonlinear Dynamics 

Will Houser ∗ , Vanja Dukic ∗ , and David M. Bortz ∗    

> Abstract. In recent years, weak-form methods have made significant advances in data-driven discovery of dy-namical systems. However, in high-dimensional settings, current techniques can prove expensive in both computation and memory. In this work, we introduce TT-WSINDy, which combines tech-niques of the Multidimensional Approximation of Nonlinear Dynamics (MANDy) and Weak Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (WSINDy) methods, implementing requisite computations in the tensor-train (TT) format. We demonstrate that this method is able to search an exponentially-growing space of candidate functions – performing weak-form transformation, regression, and spar-sification – without suffering from the curse of dimensionality.
> Key words. weak form, tensor-train decomposition, sparse regression
> MSC codes. 65F55, 15A69, 93B30, 65L09

1. Introduction. Recent years have seen an explosion of interest in data-driven model creation. At the interface of applied mathematics, statistics, and computer science, work in this area can be traced back to Akaike’s seminal work in the 1970s [1]. Historically, the accuracy of a model to data has been determined using an output-error (OE) approach: given a candidate parameter vector, numerically solve the corresponding ODE/PDE, and compare output to observed data. This approach, however, suffers from computational inefficiency, potential solver inaccuracy, and sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning [39]. In contrast, equation-error (EE) methods circumvent the forward simulation procedure by substituting data directly into the model and minimizing the resulting residual. One such method is Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) [5], which learns a sparse set of functions from data by refactoring the nonlinear model selection problem into a linear system. SINDy generated substantial interest in EE-based model discovery, and has been extended in the contexts of PDEs [43], control inputs [6], and implicit or rational dynamics [23]. The framework has seen applications across various scientific domains, including fluid dynamics [27], systems biology [30], and chemical kinetics [20]. Two areas of concern for SINDy-based methods are the computation of derivative data and the effect of noise. Often, only the raw data X is known, but the derivative data ˙X is necessary for computation of the residual. The need to approximate ˙X can render the learning problem sensitive to noisy data. Building off of SINDy, several strategies have been developed to address computation of the derivative and accommodate imprecise data [51, 55, 22, 10]. 

Weak-form methods circumvent the derivative estimation problem by integrating both sides of the equation against a smooth, compactly-supported test function, and applying integration by parts to yield an equation that is independent of the unknown derivative data. The integration also smooths the raw data, making the ensuing regression problem more robust to noise. Such ideas date back to Shinbrot’s 1954 work [46], but had not been investigated in

Tensor decompositions have also seen increased interest in recent decades. The concept of a tensor decomposition dates back to Hitchcock’s 1927 work on the canonical polyadic (CP) decomposition [19], rediscovered in 1970 by Carroll and Chang as CANDECOMP [7] and Harshman as PARAFAC [15]. Subsequent to these works and the 1960s work of Tucker [50], each of which originated in psychometrics literature, tensor research increasingly spread to applied math-related fields such as signal processing, numerical linear algebra, and computer vision before being synthesized in the foundational 2009 work by Kolda and Bader [25]. The classical tensor formats often suffer from the curse of dimensionality, meaning poor scaling in tensor order. Finding the CP rank of a general tensor is NP-hard [21], and the storage cost of the Tucker decomposition grows exponentially with the number of modes. This motivated the development of tensor formats whose storage is linear in order, most notably the tree-structured hierarchical Tucker format [13]. A special case of this is the tensor-train (TT) format, introduced in 2011 by Oseledets [40], and having been known to the computational physics community since the early 1990s as the matrix product state (MPS) [9]. This format will be a focus of this work. Tensor decompositions have shown recent promise in improving existing data-driven meth-ods, providing the ability to work in a high-dimensional space without forming a correspond-ing high-dimensional tensor in memory. [56] decomposes high-dimensional moment tensors to form a polynomial-time variant of the generalized method of moments (GMM) procedure. Klus, Gelß, and others showed that the tensor-train format can be applied to dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) [24] and SINDy [11]. The latter method is abbreviated MANDy, and, along with WSINDy, provides motivation for this paper. Our work proceeds as follows. Section 2 establishes notation, gives exposition on SINDy and WSINDy, and defines the tensor-train format and relevant tensor operations. Section 3 presents the TT-WSINDy problem construction and the TT-MSTLS algorithm to identify a sparse solution. Section 4 proves the connection between the TT-WSINDy problem con-struction and the original WSINDy problem. Section 5 establishes the time and memory complexity of TT-MSTLS and shows that it circumvents the curse of dimensionality, and introduces an algorithm that improves computational efficiency for large datasets. Section 6 provides results demonstrating the effectiveness of TT-WSINDy, both in robustness to noise and computational efficiency.

2.3. Tensor preliminaries. In this work, a tensor is a multidimensional array, generalizing a matrix. As we specify a matrix X ∈ Rm×n by the dimensions ( m, n ), we specify the shape of an order-N tensor X ∈ RI1×···× IN by the tuple ( I1, . . . , I N ), called the modes of X . The storage cost of such a tensor is O(IN ), where I := max n∈[N ] In is the maximum mode size. For large 

N , storage of such an object may easily be infeasible, and the time cost of performing even basic algebraic operations can explode. This is referred to as the curse of dimensionality .However, it is often the case that immense amounts of memory and time can be saved by recasting low-dimensional problems into a high-dimensional tensor. This is achieved through a tensor decomposition that exploits a low-rank correlation structure. We will see that the WSINDy learning problem can admit such a structure. In order to discuss relevant tensor decompositions in detail, we introduce two binary tensor operations.

We then propose an enhanced regression procedure that combines modified sequential thresholding least squares (MSTLS) with TT regression. The method effectively works entirely in a low-rank latent space consisting of “atomic” candidate terms. Using the weak form, we are able to stay within this space while gaining robustness to noise. 5. TT-WSINDy outperforms WSINDy in many dimensions. In this section, we will demonstrate that TT-WSINDy can circumvent the curse of dimensionality. This manuscript is for review purposes only. 7. Conclusion. We have extended the WSINDy algorithm using techniques from the MANDy method, achieving significant time and memory savings over a rich search space of candidate functions. We have shown that the strong-form problem can be translated into the weak form at little cost, and that the problem can be solved with existing WSINDy sparsi-fication techniques without losing the time and memory complexity benefits of the tensor-train format. In particular, we showed that the techniques presented subvert the curse of dimen-sionality, having time and memory complexities that are polynomial in the dimension of the ODE rather than exponential. We have also made the algorithm tractable for datasets that are dense in the temporal axis, using the low-rank weak feature tensor construction. We will close with future directions for this work. The first is to investigate more closely the statistical properties of the TT-MSTLS coarse screening stage. We would like the algorithm to avoid false negatives, so that the fine WSINDy pass reliably has the full library of true terms. Secondly, we would like to examine the settings in which one feature tensor construction is applicable over the other (i.e., the original construction vs. that given in Algorithm 5.2). While the original construction demonstrably overcomes the curse of dimensionality, it requires that the number of time snapshots is at most on same order of magnitude as the search space size 

JD. The low-rank construction makes the MANDy regression applicable to high-data settings, but its time and memory complexities become dependent on the correlation structure of the data. A deeper understanding of this tradeoff can increase robustness of the tensor-based approach. We also aim to extend this work to PDEs, in which dimensionality comes from the scale of the incoming data, in addition to the potentially large search space. Recent work has explored a similar candidate library in the setting of strong-form PDEs, taking the dictionary to be products of partial derivatives of basis functions [16]. Additionally, a first pass at adapting MANDy to PDEs has been presented in [26]. We would like to take a more detailed look at the time and memory complexities of this approach, and see whether it can be improved while adapting it to our own algorithmic techniques. In particular, we believe that the low-rank feature tensor approach can be effective in lessening the computational load that a large spatiotemporal dataset entails.

So for a given support S, upon finding the associated set of traces, we can cache the low-memory set and reuse it when we need to threshold a different parameter λ on S. This avoids unnecessary repeated calculations. Second, we implemented a boolean one pass parameter that forces only a single coarse pass of TT-STLS. This is intended for “medium-size” search spaces – those that are large enough that TT-WSINDy is computationally preferable, but small enough that a single it-eration of coarsening is typically enough to hand off the problem to matrix MSTLS. For example, if we have J = 5 , D = 5, the total search space for a single equation is 5 5 = 3125. If, say, we find only 2 basis terms to be supported, the search space drops to 2 5 = 32, at which point naively calling the TT pseudoinverse algorithm is unnecessary and can negate the computational savings of the algorithm. For the Lorenz 96 system, the one-pass approach was sufficient. In more generality, this would take the form of a heuristic that reads the current shape parameters of the feature tensor and decides whether to perform TT sparsification or hand off the problem to the flat solver. We leave an investigation of such a heuristic to future work.
