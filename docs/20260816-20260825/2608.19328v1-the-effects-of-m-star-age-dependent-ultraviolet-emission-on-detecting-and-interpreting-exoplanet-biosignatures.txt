Submitted to ApJ ABSTRACT Given their abundance and observational advantages, M stars will arguably be the best candidates for charac-terizing and searching for biosignatures on terrestrial exoplanets in the near future. However, photochemistry that can suppress or enhance key biosignature molecules in planetary atmospheres is primarily driven by UV flux from the host M star, which is influenced by stellar activity that decreases with age. Here, we simulate Pre-Industrial Earth-like and Archean Earth-like atmospheres around M4 and M8 stars from 650 Myr to 5 Gyr old. We find that our Pre-Industrial Earth atmospheres around 5 Gyr M stars have up to ten times more CH 4 than those around 650 Myr M stars, producing 68 % stronger methane bands in NIR transit spectroscopy. Addition-ally, photochemical shielding from O 2 in our Pre-Industrial Earth atmospheres reduces the impact UV-driven photochemistry on composition, while the Archean Earth exhibits larger compositional changes due to weaker shielding from CO 2. Lastly, enhanced CO 2 photolysis, driven by the strong net UV flux and high Far/Near-UV ratios of 650 Myr and 1 Gyr M stars, cause our Archean Earth-like planets to produce up to 5.4 dex more O 3 than when around 5 Gyr M stars. The excess O 3 causes the Archean Earth to become half as reflective in the 0.2-0.3 

μm Hartley band feature in ultraviolet reflectance spectroscopy, which the Habitable Worlds Observatory may be sensitive to. Without the context of the star’s real-time, age-dependent UV radiation, this O 3 feature could be misinterpreted as a proxy for low, biogenic O 2.

1. INTRODUCTION In the near term, M stars ( 0.075 ≲ M ≲ 0.6M⊙, Bene-dict et al. 2016) will arguably be the best host candidates for characterizing resident terrestrial exoplanets and the search

for life (see reviews by Shields et al. 2016; Meadows et al. 2018). M stars make up about 70% of the local stellar neigh-bourhood (Bochanski et al. 2010), and the conditions within their proto-planetary disks favor the formation of low mass planets with small semi-major axes (Wu & Lithwick 2013; Mills et al. 2016). As such, many have been found to host ter-restrial exoplanets (Anglada-Escud´ e et al. 2016; Gillon et al. 2017; Luger et al. 2017) in their habitable zones (HZ). M stars are also less luminous than stars like the Sun, and plan-ets in the HZs of M stars have orbital periods on the order arXiv:2608.19328v1 [astro-ph.EP] 19 Aug 2026 2 DAVIS ET AL .of days (Dressing & Charbonneau 2015). The small radii of M stars also make their planets more amenable to transit studies, as the transit depth, (Rp/R ∗)2, is higher for these smaller stars. These characteristics make the atmospheres of planets orbiting M stars accessible to both JWST (Lustig-Yaeger et al. 2019; Meadows et al. 2023; Greene et al. 2023; Zieba et al. 2023) and ground-based ELTs (L´ opez-Morales et al. 2019; Currie et al. 2023), and as such are prime targets for the characterization of resident terrestrial exoplanets in the HZ. Observations of terrestrial exoplanets orbiting M stars with JWST have ushered in the era of terrestrial exoplanet atmo-sphere characterization and the search for biosignatures. Re-cent studies on TRAPPIST-1 b with JWST are most consis-tent with the planet having little or no atmosphere (Greene et al. 2023; Gillon et al. 2026). For TRAPPIST-1 c, initial observations were consistent with an airless world, or mod-eled steam atmospheres of up to 3 bar, which were 1.7–1.8 σ

from the data (Zieba et al. 2023; Lincowski et al. 2023). JWST thermal phase curve observations are also consistent with an airless world, and suggest that massive steam at-mospheres are unlikely (Gillon et al. 2026). However, ten-uous O 2-dominated atmospheres with traces of greenhouse gases such as CO 2 and H 2O are still consistent with the cur-rent data (Gillon et al. 2026). While Piaulet-Ghorayeb et al. (2025) find that NIRSpec/PRISM spectra of TRAPPIST-1 d are consistent with stellar contamination and an airless transiting body, they are also marginally consistent (within 2σ) with thin Mars-like and cloudy Venus-like atmospheres. Several studies have discussed the feasibility of TRAPPIST-1’s habitable zone planets — TRAPPIST-1 e and f — possessing atmospheres, with some studies predicting sec-ondary atmosphere retention depending on initial volatile in-ventory and sequestration (Krissansen-Totton 2023; Piaulet-Ghorayeb et al. 2025; Gialluca et al. 2026, In Review) while others predict complete atmospheric loss within a Gyr over a wide range of atmospheric compositions (Van Looveren et al. 2024), especially if heavier volatiles (like C, S, O, and N) are subject to direct hydrodynamic escape. Indeed, recent JWST transit-transmission spectroscopy of TRAPPIST-1 e showed that spectral retrievals were most consistent with a flat line, which could be indicative of an airless body, a high mean-molecular-weight atmosphere, or a thick, high-altitude cloud deck (Espinoza et al. 2025; Glidden et al. 2025). Characterization of M star terrestrial exoplanet atmo-spheres, should they exist, may be possible via transit trans-mission spectroscopy and secondary eclipse and phase curve measurements (e.g. Morley et al. 2017; Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018; Lustig-Yaeger et al. 2019; Kreidberg et al. 2019; Greene et al. 2023). Ground-based Extremely Large Tele-scopes (ELTs) may also be capable of searching for O 2

and other biosignature molecules, such as the CO 2/CH 4

disequilibrium pair, in M star terrestrial exoplanet atmo-spheres, using both transmission and reflected light spec-troscopy (Snellen et al. 2015; L´ opez-Morales et al. 2019; Currie et al. 2023; Currie & Meadows 2025). Further in the future, a space-based direct-imaging observatory, such as the Habitable Worlds Observatory prioritized by the As-tro2020 Decadal Survey (NASEM 2021), may be sensitive to molecules like O 2 and O 3 (Feng et al. 2018) and will search for signs of these molecules on dozens of planets orbiting F, G, K, and earlier-type M stars (The LUVOIR Team 2019; Gaudi et al. 2020). Potentially countering their observational advantages, M stars exhibit substantially different ultraviolet (UV) activity compared to stars like our Sun (Stelzer et al. 2013; Shkol-nik & Barman 2014; Schneider & Shkolnik 2018a; Johnstone et al. 2021) that also changes as a function of stellar age, po-tentially impacting both planetary habitability and the detec-tion and interpretation of biosignatures. Compared to Sun-like stars, ultraviolet flux makes up a significantly larger frac-tion of an M star’s bolometric luminosity. Additionally, the ratio of Far-UV (FUV, 130nm < λ < 170nm ) to Near-UV (NUV, 170 < λ < 280nm ) flux for an M star is ≈1 ( ≈10 −3

for Sun-like stars, France et al. 2013). Observations show that this high-energy (i.e. X-ray and UV) radiation from M stars is correlated to stellar magnetic fields which are gen-erated through rotation and convection (Reiners et al. 2022). As a star spins down over time, its X-ray flux remains at a “saturated” level until reaching a critical rotation rate, after which its X-ray flux falls as a power-law with stellar rotation rate (Pizzolato et al. 2003; Preibisch & Feigelson 2005). UV flux from M stars also exhibits this saturation behavior on timescales similar to that for X-ray flux (Shkolnik & Barman 2014; Schneider & Shkolnik 2018b). While G dwarfs like our Sun likely remain in the saturation regime for only ten to a hundred million years (Ribas et al. 2005; Tu et al. 2015; Johnstone et al. 2021), early M stars (M0 – M4) do so for a few hundred million years and late M stars (M4 – M9) do so for up to a few billion years (West et al. 2008; Lammer et al. 2009; Shkolnik & Barman 2014; Schneider & Shkolnik 2018b; Johnstone et al. 2021). The UV environments around M stars, especially late types, are spectrally distinct (emit-ting less Near-UV and more Extreme-UV radiation, Richey-Yowell et al. 2023), and longer lived when compared to stars like our own Sun. This distinct UV environment for M star systems can po-tentially change the evolution of a planet’s atmospheric com-position, including the chemical lifetime and abundance of biosignature molecules. Ultraviolet light can catalyze the for-mation of (O 2 and O 3) or directly photolyze (CH 4, H 2O, and CO 2) molecules in the atmospheres of terrestrial exoplan-ets, either directly through photolysis or indirectly through production of reactants/radicals (Segura et al. 2005). Photo-3chemical models suggest that CH 4 will be more abundant in the atmospheres of Earth-like planets orbiting M stars due to their relatively weak NUV flux which would otherwise pro-duce destructive radicals from the photolysis of water and ozone (Segura et al. 2005; Rugheimer et al. 2015). Carbon dioxide is readily photolyzed by the characteristically strong FUV flux that M stars emit to produce CO and O. The direct recombination of these two is spin forbidden and slow — chemical catalysts or collisions with a third body are needed for efficient CO 2 recombination (Meadows & Crisp 1996; Selsis et al. 2002) — so M star planets with CO 2 as a major constituent of their atmospheres may have a much stronger photolytic source of free oxygen radicals than G dwarf plan-ets. The resulting free O can then recombine to form O 2 and O3, the latter of which is photolyzed via radiation at NUV wavelengths 200nm < λ < 300nm where M stars are char-acteristically deficient. Thus, O 2 and O 3 are thought to have stronger production and longer lifetimes on planets orbiting M stars, and several studies have explored the possibility for O2 and O 3 to build up on these worlds (Segura et al. 2005; Segura et al. 2007; Domagal-Goldman et al. 2014; Tian et al. 2014; Harman et al. 2015, 2018; Ranjan et al. 2022, 2023). Despite the potential for M star UV emission to alter at-mospheric composition through photochemistry, the impact of stellar age-dependent changes in UV on the presence and strength of biosignatures in Earth-like planetary atmospheres has not been extensively explored. In this study, we explore the impact of quiescent, age-dependent UV emission on the chemical compositions of these atmospheres, and the poten-tial for detection and interpretation of biosignatures within them. We do so by modeling high resolution (R >10 5) spec-tra of M4 and M8 stars at ages between 650 Myr and 5 Gyr as well as using previously published spectra in Peacock et al. (2019a, 2020). We focus our study on later type M stars be-cause, in addition to the advantages described above, their saturation phases and subsequent UV flux evolution extend well into the time period in which life is thought to have de-veloped on Earth (200 – 800 Myr old, Pearce et al. 2018). We then use these modeled stellar spectra as inputs to two different terrestrial atmospheres — the Pre-Industrial Earth (≈4.5 Gyr old) and the Archean Earth ( ≈1.5 Gyr old) — that are simulated with a coupled photochemical-climate model to ensure that their compositions and climates are consistent with the spectral energy distribution of their host stars. The Pre-Industrial Earth is a heavily oxidized, haze-free atmo-sphere with a significant temperature inversion in its strato-sphere due to heating via O 3 absorption, while, in contrast, the Archean Earth had a much more reducing atmosphere, likely with an intermittent hydrocarbon haze (Pavlov et al. 2001; Zerkle et al. 2012), and no stratospheric temperature inversion.

METHODS In this section, we present our methodology for exploring the impacts of M star UV flux across stellar age on Earth-like exoplanet atmospheres. Broadly, models and inputs are used to simulate the coupled climate-photochemical states and spectra for planets orbiting M4 and M8 dwarfs over a range of stellar ages and quiescent UV emission levels. Our experiments focus on the potential for these atmospheres to exhibit spectral signatures and gas abundances that could be considered biosignatures, and how the range of quiescent UV emission the host star exhibits at different ages impacts the strength of those biosignatures. We clarify that the work pre-sented here does not consider the effect of flares on atmo-spheric chemistry, but rather quiescent UV emission only. More specifically, we use a stellar atmosphere model (§2.1.1) to simulate 10 nm – 10,000 nm spectra of M4 and M8 stars at ages between 650 Myr and 5 Gyr. These stellar spectra are used as inputs to a photochemical model (§2.1.2) and climate model (§2.1.3), which are coupled (§2.1.4) to simulate two atmospheric archetypes, the Pre-Industrial Earth and the Archean Earth. Finally, we use a radiative transfer model (§2.1.5) to produce transit and reflectance spectra of the resulting planets. Below, §2.1 outlines the stellar atmosphere, photochemi-cal, climate, and radiative transfer models, while §2.2 speci-fies the input parameters to each of these models. 2.1. Models 

This section describes the suite of models used in this study and the experiments that led to our photochemical and cli-mate results. §2.1.1 describes the PHOENIX stellar atmo-sphere code and the resulting stellar spectra produced that are used in this study. §2.1.2 details the Atmos photochemi-cal model. §2.1.3 describes the climate model, VPL Climate, used in this study. §2.1.4 details how the photochemical and climate models are coupled together to simulate the atmo-spheric states in 3. Finally, §2.1.5 describes SMART, the ra-diative transfer model we use to produce the planetary spectra presented in §3. 2.1.1. Stellar Atmosphere Model

We discuss the potential caveats of this choice in spectral modeling in Section 4.6 below. 2.1.2. Photochemistry Model 

The photochemical model used in this study, Atmos, is based on Kasting et al. (1979) and was updated considerably in Zahnle et al. (2006) and Lincowski et al. (2018). Atmos has been validated on terrestrial planets in our own Solar System including Venus (Lincowski et al. 2018) and Earth (Meadows et al. 2023). In this study, we update the model to further include H 2O cross-sections published in Ranjan et al. (2020). This model has seen extensive use simulat-ing many different types of atmospheres including: anoxic Archean (Kharecha et al. 2005; Arney et al. 2017), highly oxidized (100bar O 2) (Schwieterman et al. 2016), hazy (Ar-ney et al. 2016) and non-hazy (Meadows et al. 2018; Mead-ows et al. 2023), as well as atmospheres of planets in orbit around M stars (Segura et al. 2003; Segura et al. 2005; Se-gura et al. 2007; Domagal-Goldman et al. 2011; Domagal-Goldman et al. 2014; Rugheimer et al. 2015; Lincowski et al. 2023; Meadows et al. 2023). 2.1.3. Photochemical - Climate Model Coupling 

To ensure that our atmospheres are photochemically and climatically consistent with the spectral energy distributions of our M star spectra, we couple the photochemical model and climate model together. We begin with the photochem-ical model’s “template” simulations of the Pre-Industrial Earth and Archean Earth atmospheres around the Sun. For each M star-planet pair, we then point the photochemical model to the case’s respective M star spectrum and input pa-rameters described in Section 2.2 and run the model. Once the photochemical model has reached convergence, we input the resulting gas mixing ratio profiles to the climate 6 DAVIS ET AL .model. The climate model uses the gas abundances to cal-culate a new temperature-pressure structure, and condensible gas mixing ratio profiles. Once the climate model has con-verged, its outputs are passed back into the photochemical model and the cycle repeats until both models reach conver-gence in one model run in succession. We note that convergence criteria differ between these two models. For the photochemical model, we define conver-gence as the model reaching 10 17 simulated seconds in <

100 time-steps. In contrast, the climate model will run for the user-supplied number of time-steps (10,000 in our case) and is considered converged if, for all atmospheric layers in the final state, the flux is balanced to within 1 W/m 2 and the heating rate is less than 10 −4 K/day (Lincowski et al. 2018). We clarify that the photochemical & climate models and the results they produced are not time-dependent. We do not simulate how atmospheres co-evolve with the stellar spec-trum continuously through a given time range. We instead compute steady-state atmospheres given a discrete stellar spectrum as input. 2.1.5. Radiative Transfer Model 

To produce the planetary spectra we show in Figures 2 through 5, we use the Spectral Mapping Atmospheric Ra-diative Transfer (SMART) model (Meadows & Crisp 1996; Crisp 1997), a 1D, line-by-line, multiple scattering radiative transfer code. Using novel spectral mapping techniques and the widely-used DISORT 2.0 (Stamnes et al. 1988; Stamnes et al. 2000) code, the model efficiently simulates high reso-lution reflected light and emission spectra, with the capabil-ity to produce transit transmission spectra added in Robin-son (2017). SMART ingests wavelength-dependent surface albedo, as well as gas abundances, aerosol and cloud opti-cal depths, pressure-temperature profiles, and gas absorption coefficients. Gas absorption coefficients are computed with the Line-by-Line Absorption Coefficient code (Meadows & Crisp 1996; Crisp 1997). SMART has been validated against observations of Mars (Tinetti et al. 2005), Earth (Robinson et al. 2011), and Venus (Arney et al. 2014), and can model a diversity of atmospheres with multiple molecular compo-nents, as long as absorption data exists. 2.2. Model Inputs 

This section describes the key inputs for each of our mod-els, including physical planetary and system parameters (e.g. radius, semi-major axis), planetary surface boundary con-ditions, panchromatic stellar spectral energy distribution, UV–visible molecular cross sections, collision-induced ab-sorption coefficients, rotational-vibrational line lists, and wavelength-dependent surface reflectivity (albedo). 2.2.1. Stellar Atmosphere Parameters 

We generated our quiescent, age-dependent M4 spectra us-ing a grid of PHOENIX upper atmosphere models from Pea-cock et al. (2020) that have similar stellar parameters to GJ 876, an M4 star. The upper atmospheric models consist of ad-hoc prescribed structures for the chromosphere and tran-sition region, attached to underlying photospheres computed for a given T eff , log(g), and M ⋆. In Peacock et al. (2020), 8 total grids of 46 UV spectra each were computed by sys-tematically varying the upper atmospheric structures for 0.35 M⊙ and 0.45 M ⊙ stars at ages of 10, 45, 120, and ≥200 Myr. Stellar parameters for the models were determined from Baraffe et al. (2015, BHAC15), which indicates that T eff and log(g) remain nearly constant for stars of a given mass be-yond 200 Myr. After a few hundred million years of rela-tively constant UV flux levels, early M type stars exhibit a decrease in NUV flux proportional to t −0.85 and FUV flux

We note that while we model quiescent stellar spectra for 650 Myr, 1 Gyr, 3 Gyr, and 5 Gyr stars for the representa-tive M4 stars, we only model M8 stars at 650 Myr and 5 Gyr. While M4 stars ( ≈0.35 M ⊙) have “saturation times” — the amount of time for which a star’s XUV flux is at a maxi-mum and is decoupled from its slowing rotation rate — of 

≈1 Gyr, M8 stars ( ≈0.1 M ⊙) can have saturation times of 

≈4 Gyr or more (Johnstone et al. 2021). This means that a) the decrease in XUV flux between 650 Myr and 5 Gyr is sig-nificantly larger for an M4 star than for an M8 star (this can be seen in the upper right panel of Fig. 1) and b) 1 Gyr and 3 Gyr M8 stars are still in the saturated regime and thus nearly identical in XUV flux to a 650 Myr M8. We therefore elect to model spectra for M8 stars at 650 Myr and 5 Gyr only. The full resolution (R ≥ 100,000) spectra are then binned to R ≈300 and R ≈3000 for use in our photochemical and climate models, respectively. We confirmed that these res-olutions produce nearly identical results to the full resolu-tion input spectra while being less computationally expen-sive. We do this via wavelength-averaged flux binning: for each lower-resolution wavelength bin, we use trapezoidal in-tegration to calculate the net flux within the bin and divide the result by the bin’s wavelength range. We use a bin size of 100 wave-numbers in the photochemical model, which is equivalent to spectral resolution R ≥ 330 in the 118-300nm region, while the spectra used in the climate model are binned at 10 wavenumbers ( R ≥ 3300). 2.2.2. Modeling the Range of Stellar Activity 

To simplify references in the remainder of this paper to the range of quiescent UV emission exhibited by a star of a given spectral type, we will refer to the difference between the lowest and highest emission states — across age and ac-tivity level — as the “considered range of UV emission”, or CRUVE, for that spectral type. For the M4, the CRUVE can be described with the equation 

CRUVE M4 = X650MyrM4 ,UQ − X5GyrM4 ,LQ 

and similarly for the M8 

CRUVE M8 = X650MyrM8 − X5GyrM8 

where X is some quantity in question (net UV flux, FUV/NUV ratio, etc.) and UQ and LQ refer to the upper and lower quartile states, respectively. 8 DAVIS ET AL .Since the GALEX measurements of the many M stars used to derive equations 1 and 2 contain a 1–2 dex spread in mea-sured FUV & NUV flux at each epoch (Shkolnik & Barman 2014) we model spectra that reproduce the observed me-dian, lower quartile, and upper quartile FUV & NUV fluxes of each M4 stellar age population bin. Therefore, for our M4 stars, the CRUVE is the difference between the maxi-mum UV flux model (650 Myr upper quartile) and minimum UV flux model (5 Gyr lower quartile) states. Similarly, the CRUVE for our M8 stars is the difference between the maxi-mum UV flux model (650 Myr) and minimum UV flux model (5 Gyr) states. For figures that showcase the stellar spectra or atmospheric composition (e.g. Figures 1 – 5), the solid colored lines reflect the median stellar activity state and, for the M4 stars and their planets only, the semi-transparent en-velopes represent the interquartile ranges (IQR, 50% range around the median) of stellar activity. Note that as our CRUVE metric is derived from a large sample of M stars, it represents the inner range of FUV & NUV flux densities that a star of given spectral type is likely to exhibit, not the FUV & NUV range that an individual star will exhibit. The IQR for the 650 Myr and 5 Gyr M4 stars were determined from GALEX measurements and bound the FUV & NUV flux densities to within a factor of 2. The IQRs of the 1 and 3 Gyr M4 stars are estimated by identifying mod-els with FUV & NUV flux densities that bound the UV emis-sion to within a factor of ≈2, which is consistent with that of the observed sample. 2.2.3. Planetary System Parameters 

The planetary parameters include surface gravity, solid-body radius, rotation rate and surface albedo. Surface grav-ity (g = 9.8 m/s2 in the photochemical and climate models) and planetary radius ( Rp = 6,371 km in both photochemi-cal and climate models) are constant throughout all cases in this study. Rotation rates and orbital periods are equivalent due to an assumption of synchronous rotation for these close-in M dwarf planets, and are calculated using Kepler’s Third Law. Planetary albedo is treated differently between the pho-tochemical and climate models; a wavelength-independent surface albedo suffices for the photochemical model as pho-tochemically active UV photons are unlikely to reach the surface and the longer wavelengths that influence climate are not involved in the photochemical reactions. We use a wavelength-independent surface albedo of 0.25 for the Pre-Industrial Earth (representative of vegetated continents) and 0.3 for the Archean Earth (representative of bare rock conti-nents) in the photochemical model, both of which include an approximation for the effects of water clouds in the Earth’s atmosphere (Kopparapu et al. 2013). In contrast, for the cli-mate model, all wavelengths have the potential to modify the temperature structure of the atmosphere, especially in the vis-ible to mid-infrared, and so the climate model’s RT solver (SMART, §2.1.5) uses a wavelength-resolved albedo to ac-curately account for climatic effects that depend on the spec-tral shape of both the host-star’s flux and the planet’s sur-face (e.g. ice-albedo feedback, Shields et al. 2013) and to model the reflected light spectrum of the planet (e.g. Mad-den & Kaltenegger 2020). The climate model’s wavelength-resolved Pre-Industrial Earth surface albedo is based on a di-urnally averaged equatorial Earth view during spring equinox (Robinson et al. 2011, composite 1), and is comprised of 65.6% seawater, 13.6% grassland/brush, 11.3% snow/ice, 5.5% soil/desert, and 4% conifer forest. We use a similar albedo for the climate model’s Archean Earth surface, but with the vegetated regions replaced by soil/desert such that it is comprised of 65.6% seawater, 23.1% soil/desert, and 11.3% snow/ice. These spectral surface albedos are from the USGS spectral library (Clark et al. 2007), except for the conifer forest, which is from the ASTER spectral library (Baldridge et al. 2009). Water clouds are explicitly modeled in the climate model (Meadows et al. 2023), so no cloud ap-proximation is included in the climate model’s albedo. System parameters include semi-major axis, orbital period, and the solar zenith angle. The right side of Table 1 de-tails the orbital parameters we use in our model runs, and we adopt a solar zenith angle of 60 ◦ to approximate the global diurnal average illumination of the Earth. The semi-major axes of our planets are calculated by integrating each spec-trum over wavelength-space and using the resulting luminos-ity to set each planets instellation to 66% the Earth’s irra-diance. This is the reported instellation for Proxima Cen-tauri b (Anglada-Escud´ e et al. 2016) and near the instella-tion of TRAPPIST-1e (Agol et al. 2021). Due to the higher NIR/VIS ratio of M star spectral energy distributions com-pared to those of Sun-like stars, this smaller instellation pre-vents our terrestrial planets from entering runaway green-house states (Kopparapu et al. 2016), and the resulting sur-face temperatures of our planets are 283.6 ± 1.6K. We note that while the 1D climate models used here do not explicitly model the effects of tidal locking and synchronous rotation on atmospheric dynamics, 1D models have been shown to accurately model globally averaged climate states for planets with thick atmospheres, even if they are synchronously ro-tating (Godolt et al. 2016; Meadows et al. 2018; Lincowski et al. 2018). 2.2.4. Atmospheric Parameters 

The modeled atmospheres presented here are comprised of many molecular species, the interactions of which are modeled via chemical reaction networks, UV–visible molec-ular cross-sections, and collision-induced absorption pro-files. The two atmospheres modeled in this study, the Pre-Industrial Earth and Archean Earth, are described in greater 9detail in (Meadows et al. 2023). In the photochemical model, the Pre-Industrial Earth atmospheres contain 71 species that interact via 324 reactions, with a bulk composition of 68% N2, 21% O 2, and 10% CO 2. The Archean Earth atmo-spheres contain 74 species and 453 reactions, with the ma-jor gases being 10% CO 2 and 89% N 2. We treat lightning-induced NO x chemistry in the photochemical model by scal-ing the modern Earth’s NO production from lightning ( ≈

6 × 10 8 cm −2s−1) to the N 2/CO 2/O 2 abundances in our at-mospheres (Harman et al. 2018, see their Fig. 1). This in-duces NO production rates of 6.1 × 10 8 cm −2s−1 for the Pre-Industrial Earth and 2.5 × 10 8 cm −2s−1 for the Archean Earth. Collision-induced absorption (CIA) is included in the climate model for N 2-N 2 (Lafferty et al. (1996), as de-scribed in Schwieterman et al. (2015)), O 2-O 2 (Hermans et al. 1999; Greenblatt et al. 1990; Mat´ e et al. 1999), and CO 2-CO 2 (Moore 1972; Kasting et al. 1984; Gruszka & Bo-rysow 1997; Baranov et al. 2004; Wordsworth et al. 2010; Lee et al. 2016). We use the HITRAN2016 line list (Gordon et al. 2017) for IR gas absorption in our climate model and radiative transfer model. Our atmospheres are divided into 81 layers in the climate model and 200 layers in the photochem-ical model, with upper atmospheric maximum altitudes of 95–104 km and minimum pressures of 38–59 nanobar. The climate-photochemcial coupling code interpolates the result-ing layer grid of one model to the other’s layer grid between model runs (2.1.4). Our model atmospheres conserve reduction-oxidation (re-dox) balance both within the atmosphere and between the atmosphere and ocean (Domagal-Goldman et al. 2014; Har-man et al. 2015). We validate that the majority (86%) of our models have an atmospheric redox imbalance of less than 10 ppm (one part in 100,000), while only one exhibits an im-balance of ≈ 40 ppm (one part in 25,000). We also validate that the oxidizing fluxes flowing from the atmosphere into the ocean — ranging from 6.4×10 9 to 4.1×10 10 cm −2s−1 — are well inside the range of Ffloor tested in Domagal-Goldman et al. (2014). Our modeled atmospheres include pressure-dependent wa-ter vapor (strato-cumulus) and ice (cirrus) clouds. The prop-erties of these clouds and their constituent particles include pressure-dependent optical depths, phase functions or par-ticle asymmetry parameters, extinction, scattering, and ab-sorption efficiencies, and are pre-computed with a single scattering model for a range of particle types. A more de-tailed description of the cloud treatment used in this work can be found in Meadows et al. (2023). To support a habitable surface temperature given the re-duced instellation (0.66 S eff , §2.2.3) of our modeled plan-ets, we set the CO 2 abundance of each atmosphere to 10%, which resulted in surface temperatures of 283 .6±1.6 K. This assumption of higher atmospheric CO 2 for planets of lower instellation is not unreasonable, given that atmospheric CO 2

abundance is believed to be buffered by the recycling of car-bonates and silicates over geologic timescales. This process depends on the hydrological cycle and is temperature depen-dent, and it has been argued that Earth would have produced higher atmospheric CO 2 concentrations during cooler peri-ods in it’s history (Walker et al. 1981; Kasting & Catling 2003). Therefore, exoplanets further out in their star’s HZ could also build up enough CO 2 to stay temperate via this mechanism (Kasting et al. 1993). We did not simulate hydrocarbon hazes in our Archean Earth atmospheres as they are unlikely to form given the low P[CH 4]/P[CO 2] ratio of these atmospheres (Trainer et al. 2006). Hydrocarbon hazes are capable of altering an at-mosphere’s temperature profile and obscuring spectral fea-tures in transit transmission (Arney et al. 2016), and re-cent work has shown that photochemical haze formation in Archean Earth-like atmospheres can be sensitive — at an observable level — to the UV continuum of host M stars (Teal et al. 2022). However, Arney et al. (2017) showed that for P[CH 4]/P[CO 2] ≤ 0.2, oxygen radicals limit haze production and the resulting spectral effects in their M star + Archean Earth atmospheres. The atmospheres in this study peak at CH 4/CO 2 = 0 .07 , with most falling around 

CH 4/CO 2 = 0 .01 , and therefore are unlikely to be subject to the photochemical, climate, and spectral effects of hydro-carbon hazes.

RESULTS Here we show the simulated impact of UV radiation from M stars at our sampled ages on planetary atmospheric com-position and spectra, and describe the underlying photochem-ical processes. Sections 3.1 and 3.2 describe how the abun-dances of molecules and their spectral features change with stellar age around M4 and M8 stars, respectively, while Sec-tion 3.3 shows how the abundances of two canonical biosig-nature molecules, O 3 and CH 4, are sensitive to the net UV flux and FUV/NUV ratio (FNR) of the host star. We find that the increase in methane as the host M star ages is significantly more correlated with the net UV flux than the FNR of the host star. μbar), methane is destroyed primarily by direct photolysis via FUV radiation ( λ ≲ 155 nm). In the dense lower atmo-sphere (P ≳ 100 μbar), methane is destroyed primarily by reaction with OH sourced from water photolysis. While wa-ter is photolyzed at both FUV and NUV wavelengths ( λ ≲

240 nm), in the lower atmosphere this photolysis is primarily driven by NUV flux as most FUV radiation is absorbed in the upper atmosphere (Harman et al. 2015). Thus, methane’s at-mospheric lifetime in our models is largely a function of the incident NUV flux, rather than FNR. DISCUSSION We have modeled the photochemical and climatic impact of M star age-dependent UV flux on Earth-like atmospheres, and find that abiotic ozone may be produced in Archean Earth-type atmospheres around young M stars, with up to 5 dex more ozone in a 10$ CO 2 Archean Earth-like atmosphere around 650 Myr M4 than a 5 Gyr old M4 star. We also find that Earth-like planets around old M stars have longer atmo-spheric lifetimes for CH 4, allowing accumulation of up to 36 times as much methane in their atmospheres than planets with similar surface fluxes of CH 4 around young M stars. Additionally, we find that the methane abundance of our Archean Earth atmospheres are strongly correlated with the net UV flux coming from the host star and that the Archean Earth’s ozone abundance has complex dependencies on both the net UV flux and the ratio of FUV to NUV flux coming from the host star. The strong ozone Hartley-band features described above — resulting from abiotic O 2 and O 3 buildup via CO 2 photol-ysis — is unlikely to be detectable with current telescopes. However, it may constitute a false positive biosignature for oxygenic photosynthesis in observations of Earth-like exo-planets with future UV direct imaging observatories like the Habitable Worlds Observatory. Additionally, while the M4 planets considered here see large changes in methane abun-dance dependent on the host star’s age and UV emissivity, the resulting atmospheric transmission features are only of the order of a few ppm, and so even these large changes in abundance are unlikely to be detectable with JWST. In the following sections, we discuss our results. In §4.1, we discuss how photochemical shielding leads to the reduced impact of stellar-age-dependent UV emission on the Pre-Industrial Earth vs. the Archean Earth. Section 4.2 discusses how O 2 and O 3 build up abiotically in our Archean Earth atmospheres, and how this abiotic production compares to those of previous studies. Section 4.3 discusses how the O 2

and O 3 buildup behavior presented here is robust to unphys-ical and model-driven phenomena. In Section 4.4, we dis-cuss which atmospheric composition changes are likely or unlikely to be observable with current and future observato-ries. Section 4.5 discusses how our abiotic O 2/O 3 buildup compares to and may be distinguished from previously sug-gested O 2/O 3 biosignatures. Finally, Section 4.6 discusses how characterization of M-dwarf UV flux and activity will be critical in interpreting the composition of exoplanet atmo-spheres in the future. 4.1. Photochemical Shielding 

For both the M4 and M8 spectral types, we found that our Pre-Industrial Earth atmospheres were less sensitive to age-dependent M star UV flux than our Archean Earth atmo-spheres (c.f. Figures 2–5, which is likely due to differences in the strength of photochemically mediated UV-shielding. The Pre-Industrial Earth has abundant (21%) O 2, which has a strong cross section in the FUV (see lower left panel of Fig. 1). The short-wave ( λ < 175nm) radiation incident on the Pre-Industrial Earth is strongly absorbed by O 2 high in the atmosphere, which a) shields molecules in the deep atmo-sphere from photodissociation, and b) produces substantial O3 via (R1) and (R2) of the Chapman mechanism (Chapman 1930): 

O2 + h ν −−→ O + O (R1) 

O2 + O −−→ O3 (R2) 

O3 + h ν −−→ O2 + O (R3) As depicted in the lower left panel of Fig. 1, O 3 is also a strong UV absorber and thus further enhances this shield-ing phenomenon in the Pre-Industrial Earth. This leads to less direct photolysis of CH 4, H 2O, and HO x molecules — as well as O 2 and O 3 themselves — deeper in the Pre-Industrial Earth atmospheres. In contrast, the anoxic Archean Earth lacks strong photochemical shielding via O 2, but CO 2

(10% in these atmospheres) absorbs across a similar wave-length range as O 2 with a smaller cross-section. Thus, CO 2

provides weaker photochemical shielding in the Archean Earth, and photochemical reaction rates are higher in the Archean Earth’s dense lower atmosphere compared to the Pre-Industrial Earth. These differences in the abundance and cross section of O 2 and CO 2 lead to the dense lower atmo-spheres of the Pre-Industrial Earth being more composition-ally stable to the evolution of M star UV flux, when compared to those of the Archean Earth. 4.2. Oxygen/Ozone Buildup 

The abiotic buildup of O 2 and O 3 in the Archean Earth at-mospheres around young M4 and M8 stars presented here is primarily attributed to the high abundance (10%) of CO 2,which imparts two main effects. First, CO 2 is readily pho-todissociated by ≲170 nm photons (see Fig. 1) into CO and O, the recombination of which is spin-forbidden and slow, (CO 2 recombination primarily takes place via CO + OH in Earth’s atmosphere, McElroy & Hunten 1970), leaving free O to bond with itself to form O 2 and subsequently O 3. Sec-ond, CO 2’s absorption of FUV photons in the upper atmo-sphere shields other molecules, namely H 2O and CH 4, from being dissociated into reducing H radicals that would destroy O2 and O 3. In short, a CO 2 abundance of 10% and a suffi-cient net UV flux and FNR allows for O 2 and O 3 production to be strongly favored over destruction. The buildup of O 2 and O 3 in M star exoplanet atmospheres has been a subject of study throughout the literature sur-rounding biosignatures around these small stars (Domagal-Goldman et al. 2014; Harman et al. 2018; Hu et al. 2020), and a comparison to these studies show our values are in 17 agreement to varying degrees. Domagal-Goldman et al. (2014) shows that an anoxic Earth with 5% CO 2, 1S eff in-stellation from GJ 876 (M4V), and an H 2 out-gassing rate equivalent to what we use in this study ( 4 × 10 8 molecules cm −2 s−1) developed an O 3 column density on the order of 

16 ≤ ηO3 ≤ 16 .5. Most of the O 2/O 3-buildup Archean Earth cases in this work have O 3 column depths fall within or be-low this range ( 11 .7 ≤ ηCH4 ≤ 17 .0), with the 1 Gyr median, 650 Myr median, 650 Myr upper quartile M4 stars inducing 

16 .5 ≤ ηCH4 ≤ 17 .0. Harman et al. (2018) modeled an anoxic, 5% CO 2, 0.6S eff Earth-analogue around GJ 876, re-sulting in an O 2 surface mixing ratio of 7 × 10 −13 , which we reproduce to within 25% in our old (3 Gyr and 5 Gyr) M4 Archean Earth planets. However, when comparing simula-tions of planets orbiting M8 stars, the 10% CO 2 TRAPPIST-1 e modeled by Hu et al. (2020) does not agree with our results. Their models resulted in 2 × 10 −7 bar of O 3, about forty times what our Archean Earth builds up around our most UV emissive star ( 5.4 × 10 −9 bar of O 3). The abiotically-generated 0.26 μm O 3 feature depicted in Figures 2 and 4 are strikingly similar to those produced by models of seasonal photosynthetic activity on the Protero-zoic Earth (e.g. Figure 3 of Olson et al. 2018). During that time in Earth’s history, Olson et al. (2018) assume an O2 surface abundance of 15 ppm, which is close to 10 −4

of the present atmospheric level of O 2 and consistent with geochemical proxies that constrain the O 2 abundance at that time. This small amount of O 2 produces extremely weak ab-sorption at visible wavelengths, but its photochemical proxy, O3, produces a strong Hartley-band feature in the UV (Ol-son et al. 2018) like the one seen in this work. Compared to the 15 ppm of O 2 and the peak O 3 volume mixing ratio of ≈10 −9 in the G dwarf planet of Olson et al. (2018), the Archean Earth atmospheres presented here build up to 50– 100 ppm of abiotic O 2 and a peak O 3 volume mixing ratio of 2.9 ×10 8 due to the stronger CO 2-photolyzing FUV flux from our M stars. It will be important to characterize the planet’s CO 2 abun-dance in interpreting any O 2 & O 3 signals as the higher CO 2

abundances expected for planets further out in the habitable zone could provide the substrate for abiotic production of O 2

and O 3. CO 2 is the major source of abiotic oxygen buildup discussed in this study, and its abundance and photolysis rate are critical to the interpretation of O 2 and O 3 as biosignatures. As described in §2.2.4, all of the model atmospheres in this study have a lower instellation (66% S ef f ) than Earth, and need significant abundances of CO 2 (10%) to retain a hab-itable surface temperature (284K ± 2K). This higher abun-dance is consistent with carbonate-silicate cycling, a neg-ative feedback loop between planetary surface temperature and CO 2 abundance. This process, which requires an ocean as well as active volcanism & plate tectonics, is thought to have buffered the Earth’s surface temperature over Earth’s geological history (Walker et al. 1981; Kasting et al. 1993) and is hypothesized to be able to operate to the outer edge of the HZ, where CO 2 buildup past ≈10 bars can no longer warm the planet (Kopparapu et al. 2013). Consequently, al-though the 10% CO 2 used here is substantially larger than the true Pre-Industrial Earth (280 ppm) and the value assumed for the Archean Earth (640 ppm), it is near the lower end of CO 2 abundances expected for a planet within the HZ. Thus, M star planets with different instellations and CO 2 concen-trations may also be vulnerable to this process (Domagal-Goldman et al. 2014; Harman et al. 2018; Hu et al. 2020; Ranjan et al. 2022), but this work illustrates how oxygen & ozone buildup can occur, perhaps preferentially, in Earth-like atmospheres around young, UV emissive M stars that receive less than Earth’s instellation. Carbon monoxide is a potentially powerful discriminant of photochemically-produced false positive O 2 and O 3 (Schwi-eterman et al. 2016). The 4.6 μm CO feature in transit spec-troscopy varies by 1.0 ppm for the M4 stars (Fig. 2) and 9.5 ppm for the M8 stars (Figure 4) over each spectral type’s CRUVE. Given the strength of the CO feature for the 10% at-mospheric CO 2 abundance used here and the ≲ 5 ppm noise floor of JWST (Lustig-Yaeger et al. 2023), if the current high stellar contamination in transmission spectra (eg. Lim et al. 2023; Radica et al. 2025) can be ameliorated, CO may be a potential false positive discriminator for abiotic O 2 produc-tion, depending on a planet’s atmospheric CO 2 abundance and CO production rate. A compounding factor in the abiotic production of O 3 is that the anoxic, CO 2-rich atmospheres that are most suscep-tible to abiotic O 2 and O 3 buildup may be more likely (vs. their Modern Earth counterparts) to exist around the young stars that induce this phenomenon. On Earth, oxygenic pho-tosynthesis is thought to have developed between 2.35 and 3.8 Gyr ago, and the Great Oxidation Event, when O 2 be-came a major constituent of Earth’s atmosphere, is estimated to have taken place between 2.3 and 2.5 Gyr ago (Lyons et al. 2014). Additionally, Catling & Zahnle (2020) discussed how CO 2 was significantly more abundant during Earth’s Archean eon, with abundances of approximately ≈0.1 and ≈0.03 bar at 4 and 2.5 billion years ago, respectively. It is therefore plausible that an Earth-like planet in orbit of a young ( ≤1Gyr) M star would closely resemble the anoxic and CO 2-rich Archean Earth cases presented in this study. However, Luger & Barnes (2015) showed that the pre-main sequence phase of later type M stars in particular are capable of turn-ing Earth-like planets into dessicated worlds with hundreds of bars of O 2. The chemistry (both at the surface and in the atmospheres) of such worlds are entirely different from those modeled here, but it is plausible that hundreds of bars of oxy-gen produced by a late type M star could result in significant 18 DAVIS ET AL .photochemical shielding (Section 4.1) that restricts host star age-related effects on photochemistry to the planet’s upper atmosphere. 4.3. A Robust O 2 and O 3 Buildup Mechanism 

Several studies have shown that photochemical models can produce unphysical O 2 and O 3 buildup if various mecha-nisms (e.g. redox balance) and assumptions (e.g. molecu-lar absorption cross sections and pressure-altitude grid res-olution and range) are neglected. In this section, we show that the work we present here has considered each of these mechanisms and that the build-up of O 2 and O 3 driven by the host-star’s UV spectrum remains robust. The imposition of global (atmospheric + oceanic) reduction-oxidation, or “redox”, balance is one such criti-cal mechanism in determining the equilibrium abundances of biosignature gases like O 2 and O 3 (Domagal-Goldman et al. 2014; Harman et al. 2015). As described in Section 2.2.4, each of the simulated atmospheres we present here are redox balanced to within ≲ 40 ppm and the net oxidizing flux enter-ing the ocean from each atmosphere is well within the range of plausible reducing fluxes at the ocean mantle boundary, 

Ffloor . If we had instead limited Ffloor to the smaller values quoted in Domagal-Goldman et al. (2014), enforcing redox balance may have required lowering the surface deposition velocities of reducing gases such as CO, thus accelerating the recombination of CO 2 and inhibiting the build up of O 2

and O 3. Further work will be needed to determine how sen-sitive the O 2 and O 3 buildup behavior seen here is to varying assumptions about redox sources and sinks. However, we stress that the maximum oxidizing flux entering the ocean from the atmospheres we present here is just 8% of the esti-mated Ffloor on the early Earth (Holland 1984). That is, the Archean Earth ocean-mantle boundary could easily neutral-ize the oxidizing power from any O 2 and O 3 buildup if the atmosphere-ocean system was not already in redox balance. We therefore argue that our atmospheres do not exhibit O 2

and O 3 buildup due to redox imbalance. The impact of the CO 2 cross-section on anoxic atmo-spheric chemistry was recently discussed by Broussard et al. (2025), which showed that their “least conservative” CO 2

cross-section resulted in significant CO 2 photolysis. This led to subsequent higher abundances of O 2 across a large range of CO 2 surface mixing ratios and stellar spectral types. We use the Lincowski et al. (2018) CO 2 cross-section in this work, which is at or below the “recommended” cross sec-tion and well below the “least conservative” cross section of Broussard et al. (2025) (see their Figure 1.) Their Figure 8 showed that the Lincowski et al. (2018) cross section re-sulted in surface O 2 mixing ratios that are within ≈0.5 dex of their “most conservative” cases for a 10% CO 2, anoxic atmo-sphere — akin to the Archean Earth in this work — around GJ876 (M4V), Proxima Centauri (M6V), and TRAPPIST-1 (M8V). We therefore argue that the O 2 & O 3 buildup seen in this work is not caused by an over-estimation of the CO 2

absorption cross-section. Ranjan et al. (2023) found that photochemical models us-ing an insufficient maximum altitude (z max ) — or too low an altitude grid resolution — resulted in significant CO 2 pho-tolysis at the top-most atmospheric layer, referred to in that work as a failure to “resolve the CO 2 photolysis peak.” This in turn drove a sharp increase in CO and O 2 abundances in their models. Specifically, Ranjan et al. (2023) showed that, for a 0.1 pCO 2 bar atmosphere like those presented in this work, a z max = 54 km (minimum pressure of 0.34 μbar) was insufficient to resolve the CO 2 photolysis peak in the atmo-sphere, while pushing z max = 100 km (minimum pressure of 4 nanobar) prevented CO 2 photolysis from occurring in just the top most atmospheric layer, thus avoiding the exagger-ated CO and O 2 levels previously produced. As discussed in Section 2.2.4, the Archean Earth atmospheres in this work are modeled up to altitudes of 98-104 km (38-40 nanobar), depending on the incident stellar spectrum, and vertically-resolved reaction rates from our photochemical model con-firmed that the model was appropriately spreading the high-altitude CO 2 photolysis across the upper atmospheric layers. We thus argue that the O 2 & O 3 buildup seen in this work is not due to an incorrectly resolved CO 2 photolysis curve in the upper atmosphere. 4.4. Observational Consequences 

We find that the majority of the changes to transmission and reflected light spectral features in our study will be dif-ficult to detect via JWST and the next generation ELTs, re-spectively. Methane’s abundance increases by ≈36x over the CRUVE of our M4 and by a factor of 2 over the CRUVE of our M8 stars. While the variability in methane’s trans-mission features in the M4 cases are below the approximate JWST noise floor of ≲ 5 ppm (Lustig-Yaeger et al. 2023), methane’s transmission features in the M8 cases possess 8 and 5 ppm variability (Pre-Industrial Earth and Archean Earth, respectively) and could potentially be detectable with JWST, though the signal would need to be disentangled from the much stronger stellar contamination (Lim et al. 2023; Radica et al. 2025). Oxygen, however, will prove difficult: Lustig-Yaeger et al. (2019) showed that O 2-O 2 CIA bands at 1.1 and 1.3 μm can be detected with JWST with several bars of O 2 present in the atmosphere, but the maximum O 2 abun-dance of 0.21 bar in our atmospheres make these bands weak and the resulting features and changes to the features via UV emission are well below JWST’s noise floor. The upcoming generation of ground-based ELTs may be more suited to pur-suing these features and their UV emission driven changes Snellen et al. (2015), but they may still require years of obser-19 vation to detect for distant planets around late-type M-dwarfs Currie et al. (2023) like the M8 stars in our study. While most of the UV-driven compositional changes are likely extremely challenging to detect in transmission spec-tra with JWST or the ELTs, the Habitable Worlds Observa-tory may be sensitive to the abiotically-generated UV Hartley band O 3 reflectance feature, which could constitute a false positive biosignature for oxygenic photosynthesis. The LU-VOIR Mission Concept Study Final Report (The LUVOIR Team 2019) shows that an 8m LUVOIR-B-class observatory, which is potentially analogous to HWO, would be likely to detect ≈9 “warm” terrestrial exoplanets, with 3 of those ex-pected to be “exo-Earth” planets, around M stars. Our re-sults show that this O 3 spectral feature may grow stronger for anoxic, Archean-like high-CO 2 planets orbiting younger (≤1 Gyr), more UV emissive M stars. The trend towards higher production of abiotic O 3 around younger, more UV emissive stars is potentially applicable to earlier-type M stars and late-type K stars (Arney 2019) that may be prime targets for large space-based direct imag-ing missions. Early-M and K stars often have substan-tially lower FNR (Richey-Yowell et al. 2019) than the late-type M stars discussed here. However, Richey-Yowell et al. (2023) showed that young (650 Myr) early-type M stars and K stars can exhibit ≈200 mW m −2 of total habitable zone FUV+NUV flux, which is comparable to the total UV fluxes that produced the abiotic O 2 and O 3 buildup behavior seen in this work. Detailed stellar atmosphere and photochemical models of K stars and their planets are needed to robustly test whether these planets are susceptible to this phenomena. 4.5. Biosignature Consequences 

Without knowledge of the parent star’s UV spectrum and its capability to produce O 2 and O 3 in a planetary atmo-sphere of a given composition, discriminating between the abiotic and biogenic production of trace amounts of O 3

could be challenging. For example, the abiotic O 3 gener-ated in our M4 Archean Earth simulations produced a UV feature that is comparable to that seen by Olson et al. (2018) for O 3 generated from small amounts of biogenic O 2 on a planet developing an oxygenic photosynthetic biosphere, and planets orbiting early type M stars may be observed in the HWO target sample (The LUVOIR Team 2019; Mamajek & Stapelfeldt 2024). While the CH 4 fluxes in our simulations were within Earth’s biogenic range and therefore character-istic of a methanogenic biosphere, whether the O 3 signal was due to a growing photosynthetic biosphere, coincident with the methanogenesis, might be harder to interpret. Consequently, every effort should be made to character-ize the UV spectrum of the parent star and determine the presence and abundance of other atmospheric species, which could help reveal the source of the O 2 and/or O 3. One such approach is to quantify the available CO 2 and look for byproducts of CO 2 photolysis. For example, several stud-ies have suggested that abundant CO in tandem with abun-dant O 2 or O 3 makes a strong case for extensive CO 2 pho-tolysis rather than biologic activity, although significant CO buildup is favored in non-habitable, very low water environ-ments (Gao et al. 2015; Schwieterman et al. 2016). How-ever, seasonal variability enhanced by a non-homogeneous distribution of land may result in changing strength of the UV O 3 feature with orbital position (Olson et al. 2018) which could provide an additional discriminant. For a circular orbit, similar variability would not be expected for a purely atmo-spheric, abiotic signal, although an elliptical orbit may pro-duce similar behavior to the biogenic case and would need to be ruled out. A strong reduction in obliquity via tidal locking (Heller et al. 2011) could also weaken the seasonality of the biological O 2 and O 3 signal, although planet-planet interac-tions between closely-packed M star planets could maintain a non-zero obliquity (Ribas et al. 2016; Meadows et al. 2018; Bolmont et al. 2015) that may still permit some seasonality. Like previous work by Segura et al. (2005), we find that methane concentrations are higher in Earth-like atmospheres orbiting M stars, but also that methane’s strong dependence on the total incident UV flux (§3.3.2) and falloff of M star UV flux with age results in higher methane abundances in these atmospheres than previously modeled. Segura et al. (2005) found that a modern Earth-like planet is capable of building up significant amounts of surface methane when orbiting AD Leo (400–500 ppm) compared to an Earth-Sun analogue (1-2 ppm). The Pre-Industrial Earth cases we present here are indeed rich in methane compared to a preliminary Pre-Industrial Earth + Sun photochemical model (0.6 ppm), but surface methane mixing ratios reach well above 1000 ppm around the older, less UV-emissive M stars included in this study. Segura et al. (2005) describes how their increased surface abundance of CH 4 is primarily due to a significant reduction in the methane-destroying OH radical, which is in-directly produced in Earth’s atmosphere via photochemistry driven by 200–300 nm radiation. The AD Leo spectrum used in that work insolated their Earth-like planet with ≈100 mW m−2 of “UV-C” (200-280 nm) radiation, while the lowest UV activity star included here (lower quartile 5 Gyr M4) insolates the Pre-Industrial Earth with only 11.5 mW m −2,about 10% of the AD Leo insolation. This leads to the Pre-Industrial Earth + lower quartile 5 Gyr M4 case building up 

≈2700 ppm of methane at the surface. Thus, our work sug-gests that the increase of methane in Earth-like atmospheres around M stars may be stronger than previously thought, and is a strong function of age and UV emission from the host M star. 20 DAVIS ET AL .4.6. Characterizing M Star UV Flux 

Our study shows that the abundances of several key biosig-nature gases are dependent on photochemistry driven by both net UV flux and FNR of the host M star’s UV spectrum (§3.3), and multi-modal characterization of M star UV spec-tra will be needed to interpret the presence (or lack thereof) of a range of biosignature gasses. As discussed above, the correlations between M star UV variability and biosignature gas abundance may change with different atmospheric compositions and boundary con-ditions, but several other studies have shown that spe-cific characteristics of the host star’s UV spectrum impact specific planetary atmospheric characteristics. Teal et al. (2022) showed that haze formation and the spectral feature strength due to hazes in Archean Earth-like atmospheres were strongly dependent on the host-star’s UV continuum. We do not include haze production in our Archean Earth cases, so further work will be necessary to understand how the variance of UV flux from M stars across stellar age im-pacts photochemical haze production. Stellar flares are also a strong source of UV flux from M stars (Mirzoian & Bru-tian 1980; Loyd et al. 2018), and the effects of both singular flare events (Segura et al. 2010) and repeated flaring (Tilley et al. 2019) on terrestrial worlds around M stars have shown that while radiation-only events cause negligible change in atmospheric ozone abundance, radiation + proton events can cause a ≥90% reduction in the O 3 column depth of an Earth-like atmosphere. We note that the results shown in this study are products of only quiescent UV emission, and the impact of differences in flare rates and energies across stellar age on terrestrial atmospheres will need further study. Lastly, we discuss the implications of using the Peacock et al. (2019a) TRAPPIST-1 spectra in this work in lieu of the more recent work of Wilson et al. (2021). For a Pre-Industrial Earth-like TRAPPIST-1 e, Cooke et al. (2023, see their Fig. 1 and Section 2.1) showed that O 3 abundances in-creased by a factor of 26 (1.4 dex) when the planet was ir-radiated with the Peacock et al. (2019a) 1A spectrum com-pared to the UV spectrum of Wilson et al. (2021) due to the Peacock et al. (2019a) spectra over-predicting emission line fluxes that the noiseless Wilson et al. (2021) spectrum was modeled to match. Far-UV emission lines (e.g Si III, Fe II, Si II, and Al I) are between −1 and 3.7 dex lower/higher in the Peacock et al. (2019a) spectrum used in this work com-pared to the Wilson et al. (2021) spectrum, and as such likely play an important role in the photolysis of CO 2 seen in the Archean Earth atmospheres presented here. However, while emission lines make up the majority of integrated flux in the FUV, the Wilson et al. (2021) spectrum only matched the Mg II (2796 ˚ A) emission line in the NUV, thus underestimates the star’s NUV spectrum compared to WCF3 NUV photometry (Wilson, private communication). This results in a GALEX FNR of 0.39 for the Wilson et al. (2021) spectrum. In con-trast, the Peacock et al. (2019a) spectra used in this work are based on a statistical, empirically-guided stellar model that includes a physical treatment of the UV continuum and are consistent with the GALEX FNR of 0.26 of a field-age M8.5 star. While the M8 spectra used in this work might not be entirely representative of TRAPPIST-1, variations between individual late M stars at field ages vary across an order of magnitude in NUV flux and over two orders of magnitude in FUV flux (Schneider & Shkolnik 2018b). We stress that the M8 spectra we have used here best represent the population average of late M stars and not any single individual star such as TRAPPIST-1. We showed in this work that the abundance of O 3 in the Archean Earth is dependent on both the net UV flux incident on the planet and the FNR of the UV spectrum. Should this study be repeated using the current Wilson et al. (2021) spectrum, we expect that the lower net UV flux — especially from FUV emission lines — would push the at-mospheres towards less O 2 and O 3, but this effect would be balanced by the Wilson et al. (2021) spectrum’s higher FNR pushing the atmosphere towards more O 2 and O 3. Further work will be needed to disentangle the impact of these op-posing properties of the UV spectrum on the abundances of O2 and O 3 in Earth-like atmospheres.

CONCLUSION Using coupled climate/photochemical models we have ex-plored the impact of age-dependent changes in the host stellar UV spectrum and flux on the composition of the atmospheres of Earth-like planets orbiting M4 and M8 dwarfs. We find that the heightened total UV flux and higher FUV/NUV ra-tios (FNR) of young (650 Myr and 1 Gyr old) M stars drive a more than 5 dex increase in the abundance of O 3 and a 1.6 dex decrease in the abundance of CH 4 compared to older (5 Gyr) M stars. Subsequent changes in the spectral features of some of these gases are potentially detectable with cur-rent transit-transmission and next-generation direct-imaging spectroscopic missions. Our study shows that Earth-like planets orbiting M stars may be more susceptible to abiotic oxygen and ozone buildup if the host star is younger than ≈1 Gyr, especially for O2-poor, CO 2-rich atmospheres that may be characteristic of young planets without oxygenic biospheres. Our Pre-Industrial Earth atmospheres have enough biological O 2

(21%) that significant photochemical shielding occurs and the impact of each star’s UV emissivity range (§2.2.2) on the abundances of atmospheric O 3 and CH 4 is reduced. Our Archean Earth atmospheres are more susceptible to stellar UV emissivity as they are relatively anoxic, but still pos-sess 10% CO 2 that provides weaker photochemical shielding. Thus, heightened UV emission when the host M star is young (≲1 Gyr) drives extensive CO 2 photolysis in the Archean 21 Earth and the accumulation of up to 90 ppm of O 2 at the planet surface. While this amount of O 2 produces only very weak spectral features in the visible, O 3 — a photochemical byproduct — reaches concentrations sufficient to produce a pronounced Hartley band (0.26 μm) feature in UV reflectance spectroscopy. These changes in the strength of the Hartley band feature due to the age and UV-emissivity of the host M star may be detectable with the Habitable Worlds Obser-vatory, making stellar-age-dependent UV emission a critical piece of context in the detection and interpretation of O 3 as a biosignature, especially for early-Earth-like environments. Similarly, characterizing the ages of potential target systems will be crucial in designing precursor studies for the Habit-able Worlds Observatory. We find that methane’s photochemical lifetime increases as stellar age increases from 650 Myr to 5 Gyr so that, for the same CH 4 surface flux, the abundance of CH 4 increases by up to ≈36x (M4 + Archean Earthcases). This behavior is inversely related to the abundances of O 2 and O 3 in our atmo-spheres. The increase in methane’s photochemical lifetime is mainly due to a) weakening of direct CH 4 photolysis, and b) fewer reactions with the destructive radicals OH (via water photolysis) and O( 1D) (via photolysis of O 2, O 3, and CO 2)as the host star ages. The concentration of CH 4 increases by, at maximum, ≈ 1.5 dex and it is possible that the max-imum strengthening of its spectral features ( ≈8 ppm) could be resolved with JWST (5 ppm noise floor, Lustig-Yaeger et al. 2023), though this signal would need to be disentan-gled from much stronger stellar contamination (Lim et al. 2023; Radica et al. 2025). Therefore, our results suggest it may be possible that stellar-age-dependent UV emission from M stars could impact future work in constraining at-mospheric methane abundances of planets similar to those presented here via JWST. Finally, we show that the abundances of two key biosigna-ture gasses, O 3 and CH 4, in the Archean Earth are strongly correlated with the net UV flux and/or FNR of their host M star’s UV spectrum. Ozone abundances are strongly tied to both the net UV flux and the UV spectral shape of the host star, while methane abundances are primarily correlated with net UV flux with little discernible dependence on the shape of the UV spectrum. Thus, characterizing these two proper-ties of a M star’s UV spectrum will be critical to interpreting the abundance of these two biosignature gases in Earth-like atmospheres around these small stars.
