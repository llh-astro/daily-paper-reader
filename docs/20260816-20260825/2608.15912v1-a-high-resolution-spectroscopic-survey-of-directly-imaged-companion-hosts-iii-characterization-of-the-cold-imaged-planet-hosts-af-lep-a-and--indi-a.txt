ABSTRACT JWST has enabled the measurement of carbon, oxygen, and sulfur abundances in the atmospheres of directly imaged planets. Interpretation of these abundances from a planet formation standpoint requires the corresponding abundances for the host star. In this work, we present detailed characteri-zations of the cold imaged planet hosts AF Lep A and ϵ Indi A using high-resolution Gemini/GHOST spectra. We derive the atmospheric parameters Teff and log g using two different approaches, revealing differences in Teff up to ∼ 260 K. The derived parameters are subsequently incorporated in measure-ment of 16 elemental abundances (C, O, Na, Mg, Si, S, K, Ca, Sc, Ti, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Zn, Y) and several abundance ratios. Utilizing both the spectral fit and the equivalent width methods, we find solar C/O, C/S and O/S ratios ( < 1.5σ) for AF Lep A and ϵ Indi A. We compare our measured abundances and their ratios with those of the planets AF Lep b and ϵ Indi Ab, with the elevated abundances for the planets relative to their host stars strongly indicating formation by core-accretion pathways. 

1. INTRODUCTION Direct imaging surveys have led to the discovery of several dozen planets over the past two decades, with this planetary population uniquely characterized by their Jovian to super-Jovian masses and wide range of separations (3–5000 au) from their primary (e.g., Nielsen et al. 2019; Vigan et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021; Hink-ley et al. 2023). These diverse properties imply that there is likely no single pathway for the formation of im-aged planets, with both bottom-up core accretion or top-down gravitational/disk instability being viable. While past models of core accretion struggled to reach the crit-ical core mass for runaway accretion ( ∼ 10 M⊕) beyond 

∼ 5 au (Dodson-Robinson et al. 2009), recent theoreti-cal advances in pebble accretion are able to form planets up to 10 MJup even at ∼ 40 au before dissipation of the disk (e.g., Emsenhuber et al. 2021a,b). However, the

masses and separations of imaged planets are also con-sistent with the lower mass end of formation predictions by gravitational instability (Kratter & Lodato 2016). Studies of exoplanet atmospheres are a potential av-enue to break the degeneracy between the various for-mation scenarios, with atmospheric abundance ratios like the carbon-to-oxygen ratio (C/O) proposed as for-mation fingerprints ( ¨Oberg et al. 2011; Madhusudhan et al. 2011). This led to a slew of C/O measurements for directly imaged planets (e.g., Konopacky et al. 2013; Ruffio et al. 2021; Petrus et al. 2021; Hoch et al. 2023; Xuan et al. 2024; Balmer et al. 2025a). However, there are considerable degeneracies involved in the use of C/O ratio as a formation tracer (e.g., Madhusudhan 2019; Turrini et al. 2021; Pacetti et al. 2022). For instance, Turrini et al. (2021) show that all scenarios of com-bined gas+solid or gas-only accretion during the run-away phase of gas giant formation lead to nearly stel-lar final (post-migration) atmospheric C/O ratio for the planet, regardless of the initial core formation lo-cation. Furthermore, Pacetti et al. (2022) note that similar volatility of both C and O reduce the diagnos-arXiv:2608.15912v1 [astro-ph.EP] 16 Aug 2026 2tic ability of the C/O ratio, making the atmospheric C/O alone insufficient for constraining the accretion his-tory of the planet without additional measurements of its atmospheric metallicity. Additionally, observations of directly imaged planet and brown dwarf atmospheres have measured C/O ratios similar to solar values (e.g., Xuan et al. 2022, 2024; Hsu et al. 2024; Costes et al. 2024; Gonz´ alez Picos et al. 2024; Snellen 2025). This has prompted investigation into other elemental ratios as tracers of formation histories, with subsequent work proposing volatile-to-sulfur ratios (C/S and O/S) as tracers of a planet’s accretion history (Crossfield 2023). Highly refractory elements like soldium (Na), potassium (K), iron (Fe), and silicon (Si) can be used to mea-sure volatile-to-refractory ratios, with these ratios trac-ing both formation and accretion histories (Schneider & Bitsch 2021; Turrini et al. 2021; Pacetti et al. 2022; Chachan et al. 2023). More recently, elevated metallicities of exoplanet at-mospheres have been proposed as a more reliable tracer of planet-like formation. Several wide separation planets show hints of super-stellar metallicities (e.g., AF Lep b: Balmer et al. 2025a; Denis et al. 2025, HR 8799 planets: Wang et al. 2023; Nasedkin et al. 2024; Balmer et al. 2025b), suggesting an extensive history of solid accre-tion (Wang 2025; Ruffio et al. 2026; Xuan et al. 2026). This has been further reinforced by the revised mass-metallicity relations (Chachan et al. 2025), where the metallicity plateau even at super-Jupiter masses hint at accretion of several tens to hundred of Earth masses of solids as being a characteristic signature of planet-like formation. However, elevated atmospheric metallicities do not reveal the extent of solid accretion in the plan-etary atmosphere. These results have led to observa-tional studies seeking to measure the enrichment of both volatile (C, O) and refractory elements (S) in exoplanet atmospheres, with the latter being tracers of solid ac-cretion in the planetary atmosphere (Xuan et al. 2026). The super-stellar abundances of C, O, and S for the HR 8799 planets (Ruffio et al. 2026; Xuan et al. 2026) as well as super-solar elemental abundances for Jupiter (Wong et al. 2004; Li et al. 2020) and Saturn (Briggs & Sack-ett 1989; Fletcher et al. 2009; Atreya et al. 2018) in our Solar System indicate this might be a promising avenue. However, determination of elemental enrichment in the context of planet formation requires the correspond-ing elemental abundances for the host star. Direct imag-ing surveys primarily observe young ( <100 Myr) stars (Nielsen et al. 2019; Vigan et al. 2021) which are often poorly characterized compared to their older counter-parts owing to their high rotation velocities ( v sin i > 

10 kms −1) which leads to significant blending of spectral lines. As JWST expands our ability to measure detailed elemental abundances in exoplanet atmospheres, it is imperative that we measure the corresponding abun-dances for the host star. This has been the motiva-tion behind our previous work (Baburaj et al. 2025, 2026a), which has measured abundances of up to 16 elements (C, O, Na, Mg, Si, S, K, Ca, Sc, Ti, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Zn, Y) for 10 directly imaged companion host stars, including primaries of multi-planetary sys-tems like HR 8799, YSES-1, and β Pic. Our methods have enabled abundance measurements for rotators up to v sin i ∼ 120 kms −1, obtaining abundance precisions 

∼ 0.1 dex, which is sufficient for constraining formation pathways (e.g., Schneider & Bitsch 2021). In this work, we utilize methods developed in Babu-raj et al. (2026a) to obtain detailed elemental abun-dances for directly imaged planet hosts AF Lep A and 

ϵ Indi A. AF Lep b is one of closest Jupiter analogs both in terms of mass ( ∼ 3.5 MJup ) and orbital sep-aration ( ∼ 9 au, Franson et al. 2023; De Rosa et al. 2023; Mesa et al. 2023), while ϵ Indi Ab is the coldest known imaged planet ( Teff ∼ 275 K, Matthews et al. 2024). Thus, these planets have been of great inter-est among the imaging community as their atmospheres can be characterized using direct spectroscopy. We de-termine the stellar atmospheric parameters of the host stars, and subsequently use these to obtain abundances of C, O, Na, Mg, Si, S, K, Ca, Sc, Ti, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Zn, Y at precisions 0.1–0.2 dex. Section 2 describes our targets in detail, after which we briefly detail our observ-ing plan and data reduction (Section 3). Our methods and the changes compared to Baburaj et al. (2026a) are explained in Section 4, with the results of our analysis highlighted in Section 5. In Section 6, we perform liter-ature comparison for our stellar parameters and abun-dances, with the latter subsequently compared to the abundances for the planets. Finally, we summarize our conclusions in Section 7.

TARGETS 2.1. AF Lep A 

AF Lep A is a young, F8V star at a distance of 26.843 ± 0.014 pc (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2021). A confirmed member of the β Pictoris moving group (BPMG), it has a well-constrained age of 18–26 Myr (Malo et al. 2014; Bell et al. 2015; Miret-Roig et al. 2020). It also has a literature mass estimate of 1.20 ±

0.06 M⊙ (Kervella et al. 2022). A combination of as-trometric and direct imaging techniques led to the dis-covery of the Jovian planet AF Lep b, with a dynamical mass of 3.75 ± 0.5 MJup , orbiting the primary at a sepa-ration of 8.98 +0 .15

At the time of its discovery, AF Lep was the lowest mass Jupiter ana-log orbiting its host star at Solar System scales, leading to extensive work on its atmospheric characterization. Studies of its atmosphere using photometry (Zhang et al. 2023; Palma-Bifani et al. 2024; Franson et al. 2024), low-resolution (Balmer et al. 2025a), and high-resolution spectroscopy (Denis et al. 2025), utilizing both forward modeling (with self-consistent atmospheric model grids) and retrieval analyses, have consistently found super-solar metallicities and solar C/O ratio ([M/H] = 0.75

OBSERVATIONS AND DATA REDUCTION The spectra were obtained using the Gemini High-resolution Optical SpecTrograph (GHOST; Mc-Connachie et al. 2024; Kalari et al. 2024) at the Gem-ini South Observatory. GHOST is an echelle spectro-graph with a red arm and a blue arm separated by a dichroic at 530 nm. The detector corresponding to the blue arm is sensitive to wavelengths 347–542 nm, while the red arm detector is sensitive to wavelengths 520– 1060 nm. The GHOST high-resolution mode allows us to obtain R ∼ 76 , 000 spectra of our targets across both arms. Our observations seek to mitigate the probability of chance events (e.g., cosmic rays) affecting our obser-vations, hence we divide our observations into multiple integrations of equal exposure times. The exact details of our spectral observations are given in Table 1. The data reduction is performed using the DRAGONS (Data Reduction for Astronomy from Gemini Observa-tory North and South) data reduction pipeline (Labrie et al. 2023; Simpson et al. 2026), a Python package pro-vided by Gemini Observatory for the reduction of all astronomical data. Data reduction by the GHOST in-strument pipeline within DRAGONS follows the same procedure discussed in Baburaj et al. (2026a). Simi-lar to the latter work, we do not apply sky correction for tellurics as the telluric features are important for precise wavelength solutions in our spectral fits. Flux calibration using a spectrophotometric standard is not required as we perform an order-specific continuum nor-malization of all spectra before any further analysis.

METHODS 4.1. Determination of Atmospheric Parameters 

Initially, we utilize a similar approach to our previous work in Baburaj et al. (2026a) for the determination of stellar parameters like temperature, gravity, and metal-licity. The telluric features in order #55 are used to constrain the telluric parameters. The H α line of the Balmer series (6562.79 ˚ A) is used to estimate the ef-fective temperature ( Teff ). Metallicity ([M /H]) is con-strained using the Na i D doublet, and wavelength re-gions from 5600–5750 ˚ A and 6000–6200 ˚ A. Mg ib triplet (5150–5200 ˚ A) provides estimates of the surface gravity (log g). This is followed by multi-order fits to refine Teff ,log g, and [M /H]. Application of this approach to the spectrum of AF Lep A leads to Teff = 5814 ± 100 K, log g = 4.36 ± 0.18, [M/H] = −0.16 ±0.09 dex. However, spectroscopic determination of atmospheric parameters can have systematic offsets of ∼350 K in Teff (Mucciarelli et al. 2021; Heil & Juul Hansen 2026). Thus, we com-plement our spectroscopic analysis with atmospheric pa-rameters determined from evolutionary models. and log g from our previous spectroscopic/evolutionary model analyses, fixed metallicity, [C/H] ∈ [−0.4,0.4], and [S/H] ∈ [−0.5,0.5], with the latter abundances varying in steps of 0.1 dex . The sulfur features chosen have negligible con-tributions from carbon atomic lines. Regardless, we fix the carbon abundance to the value determined using the MARCS-C/O grid fits while estimating the sulfur abundance. As an additional check to the robustness of our determined abundances, we use the MARCS-C/S 

grid to fit for carbon abundances (at fixed [S/H]) for the same orders for which abundances were previously measured using the MARCS-C/O grid. We recover the same values as determined using the MARCS-C/O grid. In addition to spectral fitting, we use the equivalent width (EW) approach to measure the abundances of car-bon, oxygen, sulfur, and 13 additional elements (Na, Mg, Si, K, Ca, Sc, Ti, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Zn, Y). The approach is again similar to Baburaj et al. (2026a); the spectral analysis software MOOG (Sneden 1973) and the Kurucz-ATLAS9 model atmospheres generated using the Ba-sicATLAS framework (Larkin et al. 2023) are used to convert the EW measurements to corresponding abun-dances for each spectral species. We use two different drivers within MOOG for the measurement of our metal abundances. For lines without significant blending, we use the ‘abfind’ driver that force-fits abundances to sin-gle line EW to obtain abundance values corresponding to those spectral lines. For lines with significant rota-tional blending, we use numpy.trapz to determine the EW of the blended spectral feature. The NIST Atomic Line Database (Kramida et al. 2024) provides us with the individual spectral lines present in each blended fea-ture. The ‘blends’ driver in MOOG is then used to ob-6tain abundance corresponding to the spectral species of interest. ‘blends’ uses the Kurucz-ATLAS9 model pro-vided to calculate the EW for the lines not correspond-ing to the species of interest in the blended spectral fea-ture. The residual EW encapsulates the contribution from the desired species and is converted to an equiv-alent abundance. For each target, uncertainties in the EW abundance account for the line-to-line abundance scatter, as well as errors in Teff and log g. For species with only a single line, we use the measurement errors in the EW as a substitute for the line-to-line abundance scatter. As we are estimating the absolute abundance of a species, the metallicity ([M/H]) uncertainties have only a minor effect on the result, with the majority of the metal abundance uncertainty coming from Teff and log g. For O i, we use 3D NLTE to 1D LTE corrections from Amarsi et al. (2019). For K i, we use 1D NLTE to 1D LTE corrections from Reggiani et al. (2019). In their paper, the latter show that both 1D NLTE and 3D NLTE corrections for K i have comparable values. Thus, the use of 1D NLTE (rather than 3D NLTE) for potassium does not significantly affect our derived abun-dances.

This suggests that we can compare the abundances of AF Lep A to other stars from the beta Pic moving group. In particular, we use abundance measurements for the slow rotating F-type star HD 181327 from Reggiani et al. (2024). As a mem-ber of BPMG, its abundances have been suggested as a reliable tracer for protoplanetary disk composition in the β Pictoris planetary system. We compare our abun-dances in Table 6. Sulfur abundance from both approaches agrees with the corre-sponding value for HD 181327. The greatest tension is in the oxygen abundance, where both equivalent width and spectral fit measurements for AF Lep A differ by

As a slow-rotating ( v sin i < 2 kms −1) main-sequence star just 3.6 pc from the Sun, ϵ Indi A has been studied extensively, with a small subset of results highlighted in Table 5. Summarizing the numerous studies on the atmospheric properties of this star (e.g., Santos et al. 2001, 2004; Ram´ ırez & Mel´ endez 2005; Sousa et al. 2008; Casagrande et al. 2011; Adibekyan et al. 2012; Boyajian et al. 2012; Ram´ ırez et al. 2013; Tsantaki et al. 2013; Delgado Mena et al. 2015, 2017; Luck 2018; Soto & Jenk-ins 2018; Hojjatpanah et al. 2019; Hubbard-James et al. 2022; Soubiran et al. 2022; Kolecki & Wang 2022; Soubi-ran et al. 2024; Perdelwitz et al. 2024), we find Teff =4550–4900 K, log g = 4.25–4.68, [M/H] = -0.23–0.01. Our derived parameters fall well within this range. 

ϵ Indi A also has previous abundance measurements, with the most recent by Kolecki & Wang (2022). The latter used archival FEROS spectra to obtain abun-dances of several elements critical to planet formation, including C, O, Na, Mg, Si, S, K, and Fe. We compare our abundances to their values in Table 7, finding that our abundances agree with their values within a max-imum of 1.5 σ for both the spectral fit and equivalent width approaches. In addition, it is also apparent that our abundance uncertainties are higher than those measured by the for-mer, especially for sulfur. This is primarily due to the weak sulfur feature, with even a small discrepancy in equivalent width leading to large abundance errors. 6.2. Stellar vs Planetary Abundances 

As mentioned in Section 1, the C/O ratio has been widely used as a planet formation diagnostic. Theo-retical models indicate that formation via gravitational instability would lead to stellar C/O ratios owing to the non-separation of gas and solids. Meanwhile, the peb-ble/core accretion formalism is proposed to lead to a range of C/O ratios depending on planet formation lo-cation relative to the various snowlines, such as H 2O, CO 2, CH 4, and CO (e.g., ¨Oberg et al. 2011; Piso et al. 2015; Schneider & Bitsch 2021; Chachan et al. 2023). In Table 8, we present both the planetary abundances as well as the stellar abundances measured in this work. While AF Lep b has similar C/O ratio to AF Lep A, the large uncertainties prevent us from relying solely on this diagnostic. A recent JWST study on AF Lep b (Xuan et al. 2026) measured super-stellar atmospheric abundances for carbon, oxygen, and sulfur, strongly indicating that this planet formed by pebble/core-accretion. Furthermore, their sub-stellar C/S and O/S ratios point toward formation between the H 2S and CO snowlines. A detailed discussion on the formation of AF Lep b will be included in Xuan et al. (2026) and is beyond the scope of this paper. For ϵ Indi Ab, both Matthews et al. (2026) and Sanghi et al. (2026) estimate C/O = 2.5 × solar ( ∼1.38), which is significantly higher compared to ϵ Indi A. The atmospheric C/O, in conjunc-10
