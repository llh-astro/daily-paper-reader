Abstract 

Physics reasoning requires constructing a con-sistent model of the underlying physical sys-tem rather than relying solely on symbolic or formula-based manipulation. Although large language models have shown strong ability in solving math and coding problems, they still struggle with physics problems, as these prob-lems entangle the physical modeling process with mathematical calculations. Humans ap-proach physics by first building a representa-tion of the system before performing calcula-tions. Inspired by this, we introduce a unified framework that distills intermediate representa-tions that explicitly encode the physical model-ing process and adopt a two-stage post-training strategy, where supervised fine-tuning estab-lishes structured modeling, and reinforcement learning with rubric-based feedback improves the quality of the modeling process. Experi-ments on multiple multimodal physics bench-marks show that our approach leads to consis-tent improvements in reasoning performance across different models and datasets. On Phys-Reason, PhyX and SeePhys benchmarks, phys-ical modeling output performs GRPO by an average ~3%, showing that explicit physical modeling is an efficient strategy of improving physics reasoning for small LLMs. 

1 Introduction 

Solving physics problems requires more than se-lecting and applying equations. Human experts usu-ally organize the problem into a structured physical representation that captures the relevant principles and relationships (Chi et al., 1981; Kohl and Finkel-stein, 2008; Maloney, 1993). In this view, physics reasoning involves constructing and operating on an internal model of the system, rather than per-forming symbolic manipulation alone. Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have demonstrated strong performance in a wide range of reasoning tasks, particularly in mathemat-ical (Zhang et al., 2024a; Lu et al., 2024; Zhang

2 Structured Physical Modeling for Physics Reasoning 

2.1 Overview and Task Formulation 

We decompose the physics problem-solving into two stages: physical modeling and mathemati-cal execution . In the physical modeling stage, the large language model applies relevant concepts and constraints to understand the system, while in the mathematical execution stage, these abstract physics concepts and constraints are formulated into specific equations for execution. Given the input problem x, the model first generates τ to con-struct the physical modeling, and then produces 

(z, y ) based on this representation: 

πθ(τ, z, y | x) = πθ(z, y | τ, x ) πθ(τ | x). (1) To formalize structured reasoning, we conduct a two-stage post-training strategy. We first perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) as a cold start. We further refine this capability using Group relative Policy Optimization(GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) with rubric-guided rewards, which improves mod-eling quality and encourages physically grounded and informative representations (see Figure 2). 

2.2 Physics-Modeling Data Construction Structured Modeling Trajectory Generation. 

We construct the multimodal problem pool from PhysReason (Zhang et al., 2025), PhyX (Shen et al., 2025), and SeePhys (Xiang et al., 2025), and the text-only pool from SCP-116K (Lu et al., 2025) and MegaScience (Fan et al., 2025). We further synthe-size additional multimodal problems that preserve the underlying physical principles and concepts by using Gemini-2.5-Flash (Comanici et al., 2025). For each problem xi, we generate a structured phys-ical modeling trajectory τi by rejection sampling using Qwen3.5-Flash (Qwen Team, 2026). To im-prove visual grounding, we use Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025a) to extract image captions. We then apply a two-stage verification process to re-tain high-quality modeling trajectories. Finally, we obtain the execution trajectory zi by following the verified modeling trajectory, thereby deriving the correct solution. Details of the generation pipeline and dataset statistics are provided in Appendices C and D, respectively.

2.3 Two-Stage Post-Training 

We adopt a two-stage post-training pipeline. We perform SFT on structured trajectories (τ, z, y ),where τ is the physical modeling, z is the execution process, and y is the final answer: 

LSFT = −E(x,τ,z,y )∼D [log πθ(τ, z, y | x)] . (2) We use a text-to-multimodal curriculum: text-only data first establishes the modeling format, and mul-timodal data then teaches visual grounding. We optimize the model with GRPO using a reward that combines final-answer correctness 

Routcome , format validity Rformat , and rubric-based evaluation Rrubric of the intermediate physical mod-eling: 

Rtotal = Routcome + Rformat + Rrubric . (3) Details are provided in the Appendix E. 

3 Results 

Performance of Physical Modeling. Table 1 shows that the physical-modeling GRPO outper-forms the base model and the standard GRPO method across different model scales and evalu-ation benchmarks. For relatively weak base mod-els, such as Qwen2.5-VL-3B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Bai et al., 2025b), improvements in physics reasoning are significant. The gains are espe-cially clear on PhysReason, where P@1/P@5 im-proves from 13.3/33.6 to 22.9/47.1 for the 3B model, and from 25.0/47.8 to 32.1/58.2 for the 7B model. For Qwen3-VL-8B (Bai et al., 2025a), the improvement is relatively marginal, as the base model already achieves strong performance across benchmarks (e.g., 51.8/70.7 on PhysReason and 43.2/61.4 on PhyX). Nevertheless, Physics-Modeling + GRPO still consistently improves the performance to 53.3/74.7 on PhysReason and 45.1/64.8 on PhyX. One possible reason is that the base model already tends to generate reflec-tive reasoning traces, in which it explores multiple physics concepts before converging to an answer, resulting in an extremely long output response that ultimately leads to the correct answer. 

Effect of SFT Cold Start. Table 2 shows the effect of SFT cold start across different models. For Qwen2.5-VL-7B, the proposed SFT cold start consistently improves performance on PhysReason, PhyX, and SeePhys. In contrast, the improvement on Qwen3-VL-8B is relatively inconsistent. Qualitative Analysis. The Carnot engine-refrigerator example (see Appendix I) illustrates how structured modeling supports reasoning in coupled physical systems. The model first analyzes the heat engine to determine the available work output, and then transfers this quantity to the refrigerator stage, enabling a coherent multi-step solution. This example highlights that the benefit of structured physical modeling lies in explicitly organizing key variables, constraints, and governing relations before numerical execution. Such intermediate representations provide a clear pathway that guides subsequent reasoning steps. We observe similar patterns across other subfields. 

4 Conclusion 

In this work, we study physics reasoning from the perspective of structured physical modeling. We formulate physics reasoning as a two-stage process that separates physical modeling from mathemati-cal execution, and introduce an intermediate repre-sentation to explicitly capture the modeling stage. To support this formulation, we develop a data con-struction pipeline that provides structured supervi-sion, supervised fine-tuning, and rubric-based rein-forcement learning. The rubric signals are derived from high-quality modeling traces and provide fine-grained feedback on whether the constructed physi-cal model is consistent and complete. Experiments across multiple multimodal physics benchmarks show that explicitly improving physical modeling leads to more reliable reasoning performance. Limitations 

In this study, we find that explicit physical model-ing in post-training improves LLMs physics rea-soning capability. However, our proposed method relies on the automatic pipeline to generate phys-ical modeling traces and physics-specific criteria. The quality of the data depends on the underlying LLMs’ reasoning ability. Although we apply multi-stage verification, the generated data still contains noise and flawed physical modeling. In addition, our formulation is designed for physics reasoning. The generalization to other domains remains unex-plored. Finally, the proposed framework introduces additional compute cost due to the rubric-based RL requiring LLM-as-a-judge during training.

A Related Work 

LLMs for Physics Reasoning. Recent work has introduced a variety of benchmarks for evaluating physics reasoning across both textual and multi-modal settings, covering a broad range of diffi-culty levels from high-school to advanced prob-lems (Wang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025). Early works treat physics reasoning as part of general scien-tific benchmarks (Lu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Rein et al., 2023), while more recent work focuses on dedicated physics benchmarks that em-phasize more challenging and diverse reasoning scenarios. In particular, multimodal physics rea-soning requires models to jointly reason over tex-tual and visual information to construct and solve physically grounded representations of the under-lying system (Dai et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025). Across these benchmarks, physics reasoning is typically characterized by a modeling-centric process, where the large language model must construct a struc-tured representation of the physical system be-fore performing symbolic or numerical computa-tion (Dai et al., 2025). Prior works have explored approaches such as knowledge-augmented reason-ing (Pang et al., 2024) or iterative refinement meth-ods (Jaiswal et al., 2024) that aim to enhance inter-mediate reasoning quality. A recent study further suggests that scaling inference-time computation alone is often insufficient and that effective physics reasoning is typically based on more structured and consistent reasoning patterns (Gao et al., 2025; Dan et al., 2025). These observations highlight the importance of explicitly modeling the underlying physical system, rather than relying on implicit gen-eration without structured modeling supervision.

C Data Generation and Curation Details 

C.1 Data Source PhysReason (Zhang et al., 2025) is a multimodal physics reasoning benchmark constructed from di-verse sources including college entrance exams and international physics competitions. It consists of 1,200 carefully curated problems with diagram-grounded contexts, structured solutions, and step-level annotations, emphasizing long-horizon, multi-step reasoning. Notably, each problem often con-tains multiple sub-questions, which we decompose into independent samples to construct finer-grained training instances. 

PhyX (Shen et al., 2025) focuses on visual physics reasoning across diverse scenarios. It contains 3,000 curated questions spanning six reasoning types, 25 sub-domains, and six core physics domains, including mechanics, electromag-netism, thermodynamics, optics, modern physics, and wave-related topics. Unlike purely symbolic benchmarks, PhyX emphasizes integrating visual understanding with physical principles. 

SeePhys (Xiang et al., 2025) emphasizes vision-dependent physics reasoning grounded in diagrams. It contains 2,000 curated questions paired with 2,245 images, covering 7 physics domains and 21 heterogeneous diagram categories across knowl-edge levels from middle school to PhD qualifying exams. A substantial portion of the benchmark is vision-essential, requiring models to extract indis-pensable information directly from diagrams rather than relying solely on textual cues. 

SCP-116K (Lu et al., 2025) is a large-scale dataset of high-quality problem–solution pairs de-signed for scientific reasoning across STEM do-mains. It contains 116,756 rigorously curated examples automatically extracted from heteroge-neous educational resources, including textbooks and academic materials. The dataset is constructed via a generalized multi-stage pipeline with strin-gent filtering to ensure scientific rigor and appro-priate educational levels. Unlike manually curated benchmarks, SCP-116K emphasizes scalable data construction, providing both the dataset and the extraction pipeline to facilitate future extensions and domain transfer. MegaScience (Fan et al., 2025) is a large-scale scientific reasoning dataset consisting of 1.25 mil-lion instances of diverse STEM domains. It is de-signed to support complex multi-step reasoning and scientific problem solving. We construct our training data from both tex-tual and multimodal physics sources. For tex-tual data, we sample 12,000 physics-related prob-lem–solution pairs from SCP-116K and Mega-Science. For multimodal data, we randomly sample instances from PhysReason, PhyX, and SeePhys. We first filter out non-English problems to ensure consistency. To establish a clean evaluation proto-col, we reserve a held-out set of 1,000 problems, including 467 from PhysReason, 412 from PhyX, and 121 from SeePhys. These samples are excluded from all stages of data generation and training. The remaining data is used as input to our data gener-ation pipeline for constructing structured physical modeling traces and rubrics . 

C.2 Data Generation Pipeline Problem Augmentation. To expand the multi-modal physics problem pool, we use Gemini-2.5-Flash (Comanici et al., 2025) to synthesize addi-tional problem instances from existing multimodal physics data. Specifically, we apply the following transformations: • Objective variation: modifying the target quantity or question objective while keeping the physical setup unchanged; • Numerical perturbation: altering numerical values (e.g., masses, distances, or constants) within physically valid ranges; • Linguistic rephrasing: rewriting problem de-scriptions to introduce surface-level diversity without changing semantics. We further filter out samples with visual conflicts or underlying physics inconsistencies using Gemini-2.5-Flash (Comanici et al., 2025). 

Visual Captioning. To improve visual ground-ing, we first extract a caption from the image using Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025a). The caption is constrained to describe only observable and physically relevant elements in the scene, in-cluding object identities, spatial relationships, ge-ometric configurations, and annotated quantities (e.g., angles, lengths, or directions), without intro-ducing additional assumptions or inferred conclu-sions. 

Structured Physical Modeling. We construct the model input by combining the image, question, generated caption, and reference solution. The ref-erence solution is used only to guide the modeling structure and does not directly determine the final answer during plan generation. We adopt a best-of-

N strategy ( N = 5 ) to generate multiple candidate physical modeling trajectories. Each trajectory is required to explicitly represent the physical sys-tem in a structured form, rather than a high-level summary. Specifically, each trajectory includes: • System Understanding: the target physical quantity or system property to be solved; • Constraints: conditions implied by the prob-lem setup, diagram, or geometry, such as boundary conditions, conservation constraints, or kinematic relations; • Governing principles: the physical laws or principles relevant to the problem, along with how they apply to the current system; • Equations and physical relationships: sym-bolic formulations that instantiate the govern-ing principles and explicitly connect variables through the underlying physical relationships. 

Two-stage Quality Control. To ensure that the generated modeling plans provide reliable supervi-sion for physical modeling, we apply a two-stage filtering procedure at both the modeling process and outcome levels. 

Stage 1: Modeling-level filtering. We first eval-uate each candidate modeling plan using Gemini-2.5-Flash, focusing on three aspects: (1) internal coherence , which checks whether the reasoning steps are logically consistent and free of contradic-tions; (2) multimodal consistency , which verifies that the modeling process is grounded in the vi-sual scene and does not conflict with the image or problem description; and (3) modeling specificity ,which requires the plan to explicitly represent key elements of the physical system, including vari-ables, constraints, governing principles, and their relationships. Plans that are inconsistent, under-specified, or remain at a generic high-level descrip-tion are discarded. 

Stage 2: Execution-based validation. Among the physical modeling traces that pass the first stage, we further validate whether the modeling plan can support correct downstream reasoning. Specifi-cally, we provide the question, image, and can-didate modeling plan to Gemini-2.0-Flash(Team et al., 2025) and require the model to strictly follow the plan to derive the final answer. Only plans that lead to correct solutions are retained. This two-stage procedure ensures that the re-tained plans are both physically grounded at the modeling stage and executable toward correct final answers. 

C.3 Rubric Construction Rubric Derivation from Physical Modeling Traces. For each problem, we derive a set of fine-grained evaluation criteria from the filtered modeling plan. Each criterion corresponds to a spe-cific requirement of the modeling process, ensuring that the rubric is grounded in the underlying phys-ical representation rather than generic reasoning patterns. 

Design Principles. To ensure that the rubrics pro-vide meaningful and reliable supervision, we en-force that each criterion is atomic , capturing a sin-gle aspect of the modeling process; verifiable , cor-responding to a concrete and independently check-able requirement; and physically grounded , reflect-ing explicit physical concepts such as variables, constraints, governing principles, and their rela-tionships. In addition, we explicitly avoid vague or generic criteria (e.g., “correct reasoning” or “proper simplification”) to ensure that all criteria are non-trivial and informative. 

Rubric Structure. Each rubric consists of 8-12 criteria that collectively capture the construction of a physical system representation. Specifically, the criteria cover key aspects of the modeling process, including: • Visual Criteria: Verify the correctness of key information from physics diagrams (e.g., nu-merical values and spatial relationships); • Physical Modeling Criteria: Evaluate the construction of the underlying physical mod-eling (e.g., concepts, constraints, and physics process); • Strategic Planning Criteria: Evaluate the decomposition of a complex physics problem into appropriate subproblems; • Execution Criteria: Evaluate the translation from abstract concepts to equations and sym-bolic representations, and intermediate an-swers. This process converts modeling traces into struc-tured, physically grounded rubrics that provide ex-plicit supervision over the modeling process.

G Evaluation Details 

Evaluating open-ended physics reasoning poses unique challenges due to the diversity of valid an-swer forms, including numerical values with units, symbolic expressions, and free-form textual re-sponses. To ensure reliable and consistent evalua-tion, we adopt a hybrid evaluation framework that combines rule-based matching with LLM-assisted verification. 

Answer Types. We categorize answers into four types: Numerical , Symbolic , Textual , and Multiple-choice . Each type is evaluated using a specialized strategy tailored to its structure. 

Numerical Evaluation. For numerical answers, we first normalize both predicted and ground-truth responses into a canonical value–unit representa-tion. This normalization handles diverse formats, including scientific notation, LaTeX expressions (e.g., fractions and square roots), and implicit unit expressions. We then evaluate correctness based on (i) numerical equivalence under a relative toler-ance, and (ii) unit consistency. When units differ but are dimensionally equivalent, we convert val-ues to a shared canonical form before comparison (e.g., eV ↔ J, deg ↔ rad ). The evaluation also supports multiple-value answers by matching pre-dicted and ground-truth value–unit pairs. Symbolic Evaluation. For symbolic answers, we normalize LaTeX expressions to remove format-ting variations and parse them into symbolic rep-resentations. We then evaluate equivalence using the math-verify toolkit. To further improve ro-bustness, we apply additional normalization steps such as removing presentation-level commands and, when applicable, comparing only the right-hand side of equations. 

Multiple-Choice Evaluation. For multiple-choice questions, we extract the predicted option (e.g., A–E) using pattern matching and directly compare it with the ground-truth label. 

Fallback LLM-as-Judge. When rule-based eval-uation fails (e.g., due to parsing errors, unsupported formats, or ambiguous answers), we employ an LLM-based judge as a fallback. The model is prompted to determine whether the predicted an-swer is equivalent to the ground truth and outputs a binary correctness decision. This mechanism ensures coverage for edge cases while preserving consistency with the primary evaluation criteria. This hybrid design balances precision and ro-bustness: rule-based evaluation provides strict and interpretable correctness signals, while the LLM-based fallback handles the inherent variability of open-ended physics answers.
